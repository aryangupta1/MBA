01 / The core idea

Debt, equity and business value

The optimal capital structure is the debt-to-equity ratio, or range, that maximises business value by balancing the tax benefits of debt against financial distress costs.

As debt-to-equity rises Effect on business value Explanation
Low to moderate debt Value rises Interest deductions reduce tax, increasing cash available to capital providers
Around the optimum Value peaks or plateaus The net benefit from debt is greatest
Excessive debt Value declines, potentially rapidly Additional financial distress costs outweigh the benefits of further borrowing
Vwith debt = Vwithout debt + PV(tax benefits) − PV(financial distress costs)

Here, V is the business value and PV is present value.

02 / Why debt initially adds value

The tax saving is the benefit

The benefit is the tax saving, not the reduction in profit itself. Cash saved through lower tax can support distributions or reinvestment.

💡

Interest still costs cash. Tax deductibility offsets part of that cost; it does not make borrowing free

03 / Why excessive debt destroys value

Required returns and operating cash flows

High debt affects valuation through two channels: lower expected cash flows and higher required returns.

Effect of excessive debt Mechanism and valuation implication
Higher shareholder return expectations Leverage adds financial risk to existing business risk. Levered beta and the required equity return rise, reducing the present value of equity cash flows, all else equal
Suppliers withdraw credit Earlier payment or cash on delivery reduces payable days, widens the funding gap and ties up cash that could support other activities
Customers leave Doubts about product quality, service continuity or warranties weaken demand and future cash flows
Employees leave Reduces bonuses and job security encourages departures, particularly among capable employees with alternatives, weakening delivery and future performance
💡

Supplier credit withdrawal lengthens the funding gap, rather than the operating cash cycle itself. The operating cash cycle concerns inventory and receivable days; supplier credit affects payable days

04 / Management’s risk appetite changes

Distress distorts strategic choices

  • Severe distress may encourage management to gamble on risky acquisitions or projects to rescue the business
  • Management may reject modest-return investments in pursuit or a larger turnaround
  • Financing pressure can therefore distort strategic choices, adding further uncertainty to future cash flows

For example

Customers may avoid a financially distressed software provider because they doubt its ability to maintain the platform and provide ongoing support.

05 / Analytical takeaways

Judging debt capacity

  • Debt capacity depends on operational resilience: how would suppliers, customers and employees respond if the business came under financial pressure?
  • Assess borrowing against cash flows under weaker trading conditions, including the additional funding if supplier credit disappears
  • A proposed acquisition during distress warrants security: does it create value or reflect pressure to rescue the company?
  • The appropriate debt-to-equity range is company and industry-specific

07 / Measuring the annual tax benefit

The tax shield

The annual tax saving is the tax shield:

Annual tax shield = Interest expense x T = D x Rd x T
  • D is debt
  • Rd is the interest rate
  • T is the corporate tax rate

For example

50 x 10% x 30% = $1.5 million per year

💡

This equals both the reduction in tax and the increase in total investor distributions. Its size depends on the interest expense and the applicable tax rate, assuming the deduction can be used.

08 / From annual saving to business value

Converting the tax benefit into business value

Assuming the $1.5 million saving continues unchanged forever, it can be discounted as a perpetuity using a 12% hurdle rate:

Additional business value = Annual tax shield / r = 1.5 / 0.12 = $12.5 million
  • r is the discount rate applied to the tax savings and 12% is supplied as an example, rather than derived from the 10% borrowing rate
  • The $12.5 million is an increase in value, not the company’s total value or an annual cash receipt
  • The result assumes continuing tax savings and excludes offsetting financial distress costs
09 / Analytical takeaways

Reading the financing effect

  • Lower reported profit after introducing debt does not necessarily indicate weaker operations: separate financing effects from changes in EBIT
  • Assess the combined cash available to lenders and shareholders when evaluating the financing benefit; dividends alone give an incomplete picture
  • Before relying on the valuation uplift, assess whether interest deductions remain usable and the assumed debt level and tax savings can persist.
  • The arithmetic measures the benefit under those assumptions; it does not establish that this capital structure is optimal
10 / The practical task

Four indicators of debt capacity

💡

The practical task is to estimate an appropriate debt-to-equity (D/E) range by combining several indicators

Indicator What it reveals Implication for capital structure
Unlevered beta Underlying business exposure to market risk, excluding the effect of debt Lower beta generally supports more debt; higher beta suggests less capacity to add financial risk
Target credit rating The creditworthiness the company aims to maintain, influencing its borrowing cost. Relevant measures include debt/EBIT, debt/EBITDA and interest cover Debt should remain consistent with the desired rating. A stronger rating generally means a lower credit spread and interest rate, all else equal.
Industry benchmarks D/E ratios of comparable businesses with similar risk and cash-flow characteristics Peer ratios provide a reference range, rather than proof of the company’s optimum
Interest cover The earnings buffer available to meet interest expenses Higher cover may indicate capacity for additional debt; low cover limits further borrowing. Interpretation depends on the business risk and industry conditions
11 / Precision and context

Beta’s limits, and why industry characteristics matter

💡

Precision on beta: Unlevered beta concerns sensitivity to market movements, rather than every source of cash-flow volatility. A low beta alone does not establish that debt repayments are safe

  • Consumer staples: demand for essentials remains relatively stable across economic conditions, generally supporting greater debt capacity
  • Hospitality and tourism: demand is more sensitive to economic conditions, generally supporting lower debt capacity
  • Comparisons are most useful when peers share similar risk and cash-flow characteristics, rather than merely the same broad industry model
12 / Using interest cover

The earnings buffer over interest

Interest cover = EBIT / Interest expense

For example

With an EBIT of $20million and interest expense of $2 million:

Interest cover = 20/2 = 10 times

  • A 10x ratio may indicate borrowing headroom, depending on other indicators
  • More debt → higher interest expense → lower interest cover, holding EBIT and the borrowing rate constant
13 / Analytical takeaways

Capacity to borrow is not value created

  • An appropriate D/E range should be supported by several indicators — strong interest cover alone does not establish that more debt is desirable
  • If indicators conflict, investigate the cause: a company may comfortably cover today’s interest while operating in an industry whose earnings fall sharply during downturns
  • A target credit rating creates a practical financing constraint: additional borrowing must be assessed alongside its potential effect on the cost of debt
  • Distinguish capacity to borrow from value created by borrowing. These indicators help judge a feasible range; they do not independently demonstrate that additional debt increases business value
14 / The core idea

The Altman Z-score

The Altman Z-score combines financial ratios into a weighted indicator of financial distress. The ratios were selected for their statistical ability to distinguish companies that subsequently failed from those that survived.

💡

It supports an assessment of default risk: it does not establish that bankruptcy will occur.

15 / The inputs

Financial inputs and their meaning

Input Ratio used in the lecture What a weaker ratio signals
Asset utilisation Revenue / total assets Assets generate less revenue
Net working capital (Current assets - current liabilities) / total assets A smaller short-term financial buffer relative to the asset base
Market equity relative to debt Market value of equity / book value of debt Less market-valued equity support relative to debt obligations.
Accumulated profitability Retained earnings / total assets Less accumulated profit retained within the business, potentially reflecting losses or distributions
Operating profitability EBIT / total assets Weaker operating earnings relative to the assets employed
Market value of equity = share price x shared outstanding
  • Market value of equity introduces forward-looking market expectations into an otherwise accounting-based assessment
  • Retained earnings are an accumulated balance, not current-year profit or a cash reserve
  • The model assigns different weights to the ratios.
16 / Interpreting the score

Four zones, five lecture scenarios

Using the provided example:

Z-score Lecture interpretation Lecture scenario
Below 1.8 Severe distress; default expected soon E: 1.11
1.8–2.7 Likely default within two years D: 1.87
2.7–3.0 “Zone of ignorance”: uncertain outcome C: exact score not supplied
Above 3.0 “Safe for now” A: 7.55; B: 4.84
  • These are conditional warnings categories, assuming no substantial improvement in the company’s circumstances
17 / Model limitations

What the score cannot tell you

  • Different versions exist; the version must suit the company being assessed
  • The lecture’s market-equity input applies to publicly traded companies
  • Businesses with few recognised balance-sheet assets, including platform businesses, may require different model versions
  • A score summarises warning signs; it does not explain their causes or determine whether management can reverse them
18 / Analytical takeaways

Using the score well

  • Investigate which ratios drive a weak score: an operating turnaround, liquidity intervention and debt restructuring address different problems
  • Examine deterioration before a threshold is crossed. A company can remain “safe for now” while its financial position materially weakens
  • Use the score alongside the earlier debt-capacity indicators. Agreement across measures strengthens the assessment; conflicting signals warrant investigation
19 / The case

Rusty Hollows Limited: financing an acquisition

Rusty Hollows Limited (RHL) is considering a $50 million acquisition, funded through either:

  • Equity: issue 5 million shares at $10 each
  • Debt: borrow $50 million at 10% interest

The existing business has $100 million in assets, no debt and 10 million shared. Both businesses generate an EBIT return on assets of approximately 17.14% and have the same underlying business risk.

21 / Reading the two routes

Growth is not value per share

Equity financing growth: without additional value per share

  • Assets and profits rise by 50%, but the share count also rises by 50% EPS remains unchanged
  • Business risk and leverage are unchanged, so the required equity return remains 12%
  • The acquisition earns the required return, creating no incremental value for existing shareholders
  • Total equity value increases because new investors contribute capital; this does not itself represent value creation

Debt financing: higher EPS with higher risk

  • Interest reduces total profit relative to equity financing, but no additional shares are issued
  • EPS rises to $1.45 and ROE rises to 14.5%
  • Shareholders bear additional financial risk, requiring a higher return. EPS improvement alone therefore cannot establish value creation
22 / Incorporating financial risk into valuation

Re-levering beta and pricing the share

Using the debt to equity ratio:

βL = βU [1 + (1 − T) D/E] = 1 [1 + 0.7 (50/100)] = 1.35

The higher beta raises the equity hurdle rate:

Re = Rf + βL × MRP = 5% + 1.35 × 7% = 14.45%

By rounding to 14.5%, with zero growth and earnings treated as fully distributable:

P0 = EPS / Re

Equity financing

1.20/0.12 = $10

Debt financing

1.45/0.145 = $10

23 / Decision

EPS says debt; value per share says indifferent

Assessment basis Conclusion
EPS Debt appears preferable: $1.45 versus $1.20
Risk-adjusted value per share Approximately indifferent
  • Acquisition performance should distinguish business expansion from value created per existing share
  • EPS-based incentives can encourage borrowing even when the additional financial risk absorbs the apparent shareholder benefit
  • A financing recommendation requires the earlier debt-capacity checks, including credit rating, interest cover and industry benchmarks, before treating the options as practically equivalent
24 / The core idea

Formula and market-value weights

WACC measures the current weighted cost of debt and equity financing. It connects capital structure to investment decisions: investments create value when their returns exceed the appropriate cost of financing.

WACC = D/(D + E) Rd(1 − T) + E/(D + E) Re
  • D, E: market values of debt and equity
  • Rd: current pre-tax cost of debt
  • T: corporate tax rate
  • Re: shareholders’ required return, or equity hurdle rate

Market values reflect current financing conditions and investor valuations. Book values reflect accounting balances and can produce materially different weights.

26 / Deriving the financing costs

Cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt

Using CAPM, with a 4% risk-free rate, 6.5% market risk premium and an equity beta of 1.2:

Re = 4% + (1.2 × 6.5%) = 11.8%

With a 7% borrowing rate and 30% corporate tax rate:

Rd(1 − T) = 7% × (1 − 0.30) = 4.9%
💡

The lender receives 7%; 4.9% is the company’s cost after the tax benefit

27 / Calculating WACC

The financing hurdle

WACC = (0.25 × 4.9%) + (0.75 × 11.8%) = 10.075% ≈ 10.08%

The company’s financing hurdle is therefore approximately 10.08%, subject to the risk and financing assumption.

💡

Debt’s market value equals its book value here; the calculation does not establish that they must always be equal

28 / When company WACC is an appropriate project hurdle rate

Two assumptions behind using it

Assumption Meaning Why it matters
Same financing weights The investment is evaluated on the basis of maintaining 25% debt and 75% equity financing Different proportions change the weighted financing cost and may change the component costs
Same business risk The investment has comparable underlying risk to the existing business The existing required returns may be unsuitable for an investment with different risk
29 / Analytical takeaways

Linking financing to investment appraisal

  • A company-wide hurdle rate requires scrutiny when the business enters a different activity: an unchanged financing policy does not establish unchanged project risk
  • Book-value weights would incorrectly treat this example 50% debt-funded, understating the importance of the more expensive equity financing
  • Capital structure and investment appraisal are linked: changing financing proportions requires reconsidering both the weights and the required returns, rather than simply substituting more debt at its existing cost
