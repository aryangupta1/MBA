TAKING STOCK
AND STAKEHOLDERS

Three topics on keeping a project aligned while it runs. Stakeholder engagement is influence without authority — mapping who can approve, influence, deliver or obstruct, and building cooperation before it is needed. Tracking with stakeholders separates delivery progress from strategic progress, with a product owner who translates in both directions so feedback becomes an explicit prioritisation decision. Project tracking brings in earned value management: work completed compared with what it cost and with what was scheduled, then a forecast of completion.

00 / Start here

How to master this week

Three topics that build on each other: who the stakeholders are, how to keep them in the loop while the work runs, and how to measure where the work actually stands. A full pass takes about two and a half hours.

  1. Read Stakeholders, blocks 01 to 07

    ~25 min

    Block 02’s map — approve, influence, deliver, obstruct — and block 03’s depth-and-breadth point are the two ideas the rest of the topic leans on. Block 06’s risk table is the one to learn row by row: each risk has its own engagement response, and the scenarios hand you the symptom and ask for the row.

  2. Read Tracking with stakeholders, blocks 08 to 14

    ~25 min

    Start from block 08’s callout: a team can complete every sprint while moving further from the problem that matters. Blocks 09 and 10 show how the product owner protects team focus while keeping direction responsive, and block 12 separates the two failure modes — one threatens the value of the solution, the other the ability to deliver it.

  3. Read Project tracking, blocks 15 to 20

    ~25 min

    Hold one idea from block 15 throughout: in EVM, value means budgeted cost of completed work. Everything else is EV compared with AC for cost and with PV for schedule. Block 17’s grid is the trap — favourable cost efficiency can coexist with delayed delivery.

  4. Fix the eight formulas, then the acronyms

    ~10 min

    Learn them in pairs: CV and CPI both compare EV with AC; SV and SPI both compare EV with PV. Variances benchmark at zero, indices at 1.0. Then EAC = BAC / CPI and ETC = EAC − AC. The Acronyms tab has the twelve expansions if any of the letters blur.

  5. Run the deck cold

    ~25 min

    Seventy-one cards. The symptom cards — a contact resigns, features ship unvalidated, someone asks why they were not consulted — are worth answering out loud before flipping. The formula cards are checkable in a second.

  6. Sit the quiz without notes

    ~15 min

    Each distractor is a real confusion from this week, so read the note on the option you rejected as well as the one you chose. The earned value questions turn on direction: which side of zero or 1.0 is favourable.

  7. Work two scenarios end to end

    ~20 min

    Take one stakeholder scenario and one earned value scenario. The stakeholder ones turn on diagnosing the cause before choosing a response; the earned value ones turn on reading cost and schedule separately. Mark against the checklist, not the walkthrough.

The testYou have it when you can take any project status report and say what it shows about delivery, what it cannot show about strategic alignment, and whether its cost and schedule readings agree.

01 / The core idea

Stakeholder engagement: influence without authority

Stakeholders have an interest in, influence over, or are affected by a project’s outcomes. The relevant network extends beyond sponsors and users to operational teams, external partners and oversight bodies — and stakeholders pursue their own objectives, often outside the project leader’s authority.

💡

Technical delivery alone cannot ensure success. Stakeholder input determines whether the solution addresses the right problem and receives the support needed for implementation.

02 / Mapping the network

Mapping the actual decision network

Two routes feed the map. Formal research — organisational information, company websites and professional networks — identifies an appropriate entry point. The most senior person is not necessarily the right first contact. Conversational discovery then fills in what the formal picture misses: initial contacts reveal reporting lines, team dependencies, decision-makers and potential blockers that formal structures may obscure.

03 / Depth and breadth

Depth across seniority, breadth across functions

Relationships need both depth across seniority levels and breadth across functions. A supportive commercial contact cannot substitute for engineering capacity, legal agreement or marketing commitment.

04 / Cooperation first

Building cooperation before it is needed

  • Relationship investment creates trust before difficult requests or disagreements arise. A first interaction with a senior stakeholder should not be an escalation.
  • Regular contact and an understanding of people’s pressures make constraints easier to discuss openly. Transparency about deadlines and consequences helps others assess urgency, within confidentiality limits.
  • Shared objectives must reach those executing the work. Understanding the commercial intent supports discretionary decisions without constant clarification.
  • Empathy means understanding another team’s incentives and finding mutually beneficial adjustments.
Example — a mutually beneficial adjustment

Additional integration work becomes easier to justify when it prevents a partner’s engineering team from repeating work after a planned upgrade.

05 / Contracts and relationships

Contracts and relationships serve complementary purposes

Mechanism Managerial purpose Limitation
Contractual agreement Resolves substantial disagreements upfront and established minimum commitments and protection Attempting to specify every contingency prolongs negotiation and can constrain commitments to a conservative minimum
Relationship and shared understanding Enables adaptation, cooperation and contributions beyond contractual obligations as benefits become evident Goodwill alone leaves substantial disagreements unresolved until execution
💡

Contractual commitments are a foundation, not a ceiling.

06 / Risk reduction

Engagement as risk reduction

Risk How it threatens delivery Engagement response
Strategic change A partner no longer wants the initiative Maintain senior alignment; contractual commitments provide baseline protection
People turnover Departures remove context, advocacy and decision continuity Spread relationships and understanding across multiple people; support succession and onboarding
Priority change The strategy remains supported, but competing work displaces delivery Keep senior stakeholders informed about timing, consequences and business value
Execution constraints Capacity, budgets or dependencies prevent delivery Engage delivery teams directly and negotiate workable trade-offs
07 / Analytical takeaways

What good engagement looks like

  • A manager should distinguish agreement with the strategy from commitment of resources: endorsement does not guarantee deliver priority.
  • Stakeholder resilience can be assessed by asking whether the project would retain context, sponsorship and momentum if its main contact left.
  • Resistance can reveal a mismatch in incentives or constraints; diagnosing that mismatch is more useful than assuming unwillingness.
  • Engagement quality is demonstrated by stakeholders’ ability to make aligned decisions and sustain delivery through change, rather than by meeting frequency alone.
08 / Two levels of tracking

Tracking delivery and strategic alignment

Tracking level Central question Management response
Tactical Are tasks progressing, and where are blockers or resource constraints? Resolve immediate delivery problems
Strategic Does the work still address stakeholder needs and business objectives? Reassess direction, priorities and expectations

Tracking also communicated tangible progress and value to stakeholders, sustaining confidence in the project.

💡

Delivery progress does not establish strategic progress: a team can complete every sprint while moving further from the problem that matters.

09 / Two concurrent activities

The product owner as translator

While the team executes the current sprint, the product owner obtains stakeholder feedback on relevant outputs from the previous sprint. That feedback is interpreted and weighed against the wider backlog before informing subsequent work.

💡

This creates continuous consultation with controlled incorporation of change, protecting team focus while keeping direction responsive.

10 / Translation both ways

Translation in both directions

The product owner selects which outputs need validation and which stakeholders can provide it — marketing may assess a demonstration; a continuity group may assess public impacts. The managerial value lies in making information usable across different professional perspectives, rather than simply forwarding messages.

11 / Closing the loop

Closing the feedback loop

  • Feedback must lead to an explicit prioritisation decision; gathering opinions alone does not create alignment.
  • Learning about the product’s direction informs future work, while retrospective learning informs how the team works.
  • The broader principle applies beyond Agile: coordinate communication so stakeholders can influence delivery without creating constant, unfiltered interruptions.
12 / Weak consultation

Two distinct consequences of weak consultation

Failure mode Mechanism Warning sign
Project drift Missing feedback allows incorrect assumptions about stakeholder needs to persist Features continue shipping, but their relevance to the intended outcome remains unvalidated
Withdrawal of support Excluded stakeholders challenge decisions, withhold resources or undermine the initiative Influential people question why they were not consulted or reject decisions late
  • The first threatens the value of the solution; the second threatens the ability to deliver it.
  • Consultation therefore belongs at recurring reviews, prioritisation discussions and consequential decision points.
  • For public-facing change, formal community consultation may also be necessary: community sentiment and the political environment can affect project viability.
13 / Rational and emotional

The rider and the elephant

Element Represents Implication for engagement
Rider Rational analysis, planning and direction Provide clear objectives, evidence and understandable next steps
Elephant Emotion, instinct, loyalty and motivation Understand fears and motivations that affect willingness to support change

A convincing plan can still fail if stakeholders feel threatened, confused or emotionally disconnected from the change. Apparent stubbornness may reflect fear or unclear direction; the appropriate response depends on the underlying cause, rather than assuming that more evidence will resolve it.

In managerial terms, rational clarity establishes where the project should go: emotional commitment helps stakeholders act on that direction. Engagement must address both what stakeholders understand and how they experience the proposed change.

14 / Analytical takeaways

What tracking with stakeholders is for

  • A useful tracking system should expose what needs a decision, not merely report activity completed.
  • Feedback has limited value unless it reaches someone with the ability to change priorities.
  • Stakeholder approval is stronger evidence of alignment when people understand the implications and are willing to support implementation.
  • When resistance emerges, the diagnostic question is whether the obstacle is unclear direction, emotional concern or a substantive disagreemeent about the project’s value.
15 / Earned value management

Earned Value Management (EVM)

EVM integrates scope, cost and schedule, comparing work completed with both its actual cost and the work originally scheduled. Reliable tracking requires an approval baseline, regularly updated costs and credible assessments of completed work. “Value” means the budgeted cost of completed work, not revenue, customer benefit or strategic value.

💡

Spending less than planned does not necessarily indicate efficiency: it may simply reflect unfinished work.

16 / Core measures

Measuring current performance

All measures refer to the same reporting date and scope.

Measure Meaning
Planned Value (PV) Budgeted cost of work scheduled to be completed by now. The time-phased budget accommodates uneven spending across the project.
Earned Value (EV) Budgeted cost of work actually completed by now, valued using the original baseline
Actual Cost (AC) Actual direct and indirect costs incurred for the work performed
Budget at Completion (BAC) Total approved budget for the entire project
Rate of Performance (RP) Actual completion proportion / planned completion proportion for the same work
EV = PV x RP

RP is relative to scheduled progress, not necessarily the completion percentage of the whole project.

17 / Variances and indices

Variances and performance indices

Metric Formula Interpretation
Cost variance CV = EV - AC Positive: completed work cost less than budgeted
Negative: cost more
Schedule variance SV = EV - PV Positive: more work completed than scheduled
Negative: less
Cost Performance Index CPI = EV / AC Budgeted work delivered per dollar spent. Above 1 is favourable; below 1 is unfavourable
Schedule Performance Index SPI = EV / PV Work completed relative to work scheduled. Above 1 is ahead; below 1 is behind
  • SV is expressed in dollars of budgeted work, not days or weeks.
  • Indices use 1.0 as the benchmark, whereas variances use zero.
  • Cost and schedule performance must be interpreted separately: favourable cost efficiency can coexist with delayed delivery.
18 / Cost forecasts

Forecasting completion cost

If current cost efficiency continues across the remaining work:

EAC = BAC / CPI ETC = EAC − AC
Measure Meaning
Estimate at Completion (EAC) Forecast total cost of completing the project. It does not replace the approved BAC.
Estimate to Complete (ETC) Forecast additional expenditure required from the reporting date onward.
  • A lower CPI raises EAC because completing the remaining scope requires more spending per unit of budgeted work.
  • ETC is a cost estimate; remaining duration is calculated separately.
19 / Duration forecast

Duration forecast in the lecture’s simplified model

With evenly distributed planned work and an unchanged delivery rate:

Forecast duration = Planned duration / SPI
  • This is a conditional extrapolation, not a guaranteed finish date.
  • The example assumed design, coding and testing sustain the same performance. Changes in productivity, scope or weekly costs would change the forecast.
20 / Analytical takeaways

What earned value is actually for

  • Apparent underspending should be tested against earned work, rather than treated as an available saving.
  • A forecast saving does not establish overall success: the business consequences of delayed delivery must also be assessed.
  • Forecast credibility depends on whether current performance is representative of the remaining work particularly when later stages differ substantially.
  • EVM supports early decisions about resources, scope and deadlines; it’s usefulness lies in enabling corrective action, rather than presenting a precise forecast as certainty.
