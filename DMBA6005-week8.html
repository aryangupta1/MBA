MANAGING
OUR PEOPLE

Two topics on the people behind a project. Service design extends CX to the organisational system that enables it — frontstage and backstage, employee experience, discovery before the Agile backlog, and teams that turn uncertainty into challenge and conflict into competition between ideas. Conflict management separates constructive from destructive conflict and positions from interests, and gives the WIN approach for difficult conversations and a method for written ones.

01 / The core idea

From customer experience to the whole service system

Service design extends CX by designing both the customer experience and the organisational system that enables it. The three lenses differ in how much of that system they take in.

Lens Primary focus
UX A specific interaction or touchpoint
CX The customer’s end-to-end journey
Service design The end-to-end journey plus the people, processes, policies and systems behind it

Internal staff are therefore not simply resources used to deliver CX; their experience is part of the service being designed.

💡

Managerial implication: customer friction is often a symptom of internal organisational friction.

02 / Five characteristics

What makes it service design

Service design is… Meaning
User-centric Built around the experience and needs of people affected by the service
Co-creative Involves relevant stakeholders rather than designing in isolation
Sequenced Considers how interactions unfold over time, like scenes in a movie
Evidenced Makes otherwise intangible services understandable through observable interactions and artefacts
Holistic Considers the complete system, including emotional as well as functional experiences

The relevant stakeholders can extend beyond employees to customers, suppliers, partners, management and other actors affecting service delivery.

03 / Employee experience

Employees as service enablers

Service quality depends partly on whether employees have the information, systems, authority and support required to perform their role effectively. Employee experience includes more than remuneration:

  • frustration or ease when completing work;
  • ability to access required information;
  • perceived ability to contribute;
  • autonomy and empowerment;
  • sense of achievement;
  • quality of interactions with colleagues and management.
Example — software support

A support engineer may genuinely want to solve a customer’s problem but be blocked by poor observability, inaccessible documentation or unclear escalation ownership. Improving those backstage systems improves both the employee and customer experience.

Remove obstacles rather than “make people perform”

The lecture assumes that most employees want to do a good job and are capable of contributing, so the management question changes.

From To
“How do we make the team perform?” “What is preventing capable people from performing?”

The manager, project manager or product owner becomes an obstacle remover:

  • eliminate unnecessary process friction;
  • improve access to information;
  • clarify decision rights;
  • provide appropriate tools;
  • remove organisational barriers to contribution.
💡

Employee motivation stops being something managers try to manufacture and becomes something the organisation enables.

04 / A good service

Desirability, utility and usability

A successful service needs all three.

Dimension Question
Desirability Do people actually want to engage with it?
Utility Does it deliver what it promises?
Usability Can people use it effectively when they need it?

Usability also depends on:

  • frequency — how often an interaction occurs;
  • sequencing — whether steps occur in a sensible order;
  • importance — whether critical interactions receive appropriate attention;
  • information being available at the right moment, not merely existing somewhere.
💡

A technically functional service can still fail if information, systems or decisions appear at the wrong point in the journey.

05 / Before Agile delivery

Discovery defines what should enter the backlog

A new angle on Agile, rather than re-covering Agile itself. Service design can occur before Agile delivery, to understand the complete system before individual features are built.

Early discovery considers:

  • customers and staff;
  • needs and behaviours;
  • barriers to adoption;
  • organisational change requirements;
  • processes and policies;
  • systems and channels;
  • existing products and dependencies.

Service experience vs service model design

Dimension What it covers
Service experience design How customers and staff interact with the service. Journeys, touchpoints, behaviours and desired experiences.
Service model design The organisational architecture enabling those experiences. People, processes, policies, systems, channels and performance measures.

Effective transformation requires both.

06 / Project constraints

Working within scope, cost and schedule

Service design does not remove the traditional constraints of scope, cost and schedule.

Situation Response
Discovery time is limited Conduct enough research to generate informed hypotheses; use internal staff as proxies cautiously; identify assumptions explicitly; plan later validation with actual users
Design and development occur simultaneously Build testing and validation cycles into delivery; periodically test assumptions rather than allowing unvalidated designs to become permanent system behaviour
💡

Key risk: limited discovery is manageable; treating limited discovery as definitive evidence is not.

07 / Team dynamics

The team operates within VUCA

Condition Meaning
Volatility Circumstances change rapidly
Uncertainty Future outcomes are unclear
Complexity Many interconnected factors affect outcomes
Ambiguity Cause, effect and meaning may not yet be clear

The lecture particularly emphasises uncertainty, because its impact can be shaped.

08 / High-performing teams

The string quartet, and a competition of ideas

A string quartet demonstrates a team of highly capable specialists operating with limited formal hierarchy, considerable individual expertise, interdependence, self-governance and constant adaptation. The lecture uses the second violinist as an analogy for organisational health: team success should not be judged solely through the person with the most visible or prestigious role.

💡

The experience of less-visible contributors can reveal whether collaboration is genuinely working.

Strong teams do not require every disagreement to disappear; they require it to become productive rather than personal. Individuals bring anxiety about uncertainty and outcomes, ego or identity and a desire to contribute, and their own perspective on the problem.

09 / Conversation

Conflict, communication and conversation

The lecture proposes four connected elements: conflict → contact → communication → conversation. Productive dialogue requires:

  • respect;
  • active listening;
  • suspending judgement;
  • careful language;
  • encouraging ideas;
  • discussing the problem rather than attacking the person.

Informal personal contact can be valuable, because genuine conversation often reveals problems that formal meetings and reporting systems do not. The outcome is greater psychological participation: employees feel that they can contribute, be heard and exercise their expertise.

💡

Management principle: talk to the person, but debate the idea.

10 / Behavioural change

What behaviour should the service enable?

Service design can go beyond improving transactions to influencing behaviour over time. The design question becomes not only “How can this task be completed?” but:

❓

What behaviour should this service enable or encourage?

Mechanisms can include:

  • nudges;
  • feedback loops;
  • automation;
  • environmental design;
  • progress signals;
  • reducing friction around desirable behaviours.
Example — a finance platform

A finance platform may move beyond displaying balances toward helping customers form better saving behaviours through progress feedback, reminders and appropriately designed choices.

This shifts the service from a functional tool toward an intervention capable of producing behavioural outcomes.

11 / Not an efficiency exercise

Internal service redesign is not efficiency redesign

A common mistake is treating an internal service-design initiative as a traditional process-efficiency exercise.

Approach The question it asks
Efficiency redesign How can this process become faster or cheaper?
Service redesign How can this experience work better for the people participating in it while still achieving organisational goals?

Efficiency still matters, but service design also considers emotional response, perceived effort, confidence, control, clarity, trust and behavioural outcomes. Removing 30 seconds from a process may create little value if users remain anxious or confused. Conversely, an interaction can occasionally take longer while producing a better perceived experience, because the user feels informed and supported.

💡

Approach internal redesign as a design challenge rather than merely a process audit.

12 / Analytical takeaways

What service design asks of managers

  • CX problems often originate backstage. When customers encounter friction, managers should investigate employee workflows, information access, policies and systems rather than immediately redesigning the customer interface.
  • Employee experience is an operating capability. Giving capable staff the tools, autonomy and information to perform improves the organisation’s ability to deliver its strategy.
  • Service design should precede detailed feature delivery where possible: first understand the service system, then translate it into journeys, user stories and an Agile backlog.
  • Managers should not automatically eliminate uncertainty or disagreement. The goal is to turn uncertainty into constructive challenge and interpersonal conflict into competition between ideas.
  • The ultimate test of service redesign is not simply whether a process became cheaper or faster, but whether the whole system enables better behaviour and better experiences for customers and staff while delivering organisational value.
13 / The core idea

Conflict is not inherently negative

Its value depends on whether it generates better decisions or damages working relationships.

Constructive conflict Destructive conflict
Challenges ideas, assumptions and strategies Attacks individuals or their competence
Produces new insights and progress Repeats grievances without resolution
Encourages innovation and critical thinking Creates defensiveness, disengagement and distrust
Strengthens collective problem-solving Undermines collaboration and project delivery

Absence of conflict does not necessarily indicate a healthy team; it may reflect disengagement or reluctance to challenge decisions. The manager’s responsibility is to create conditions where vigorous disagreement is encouraged without permitting personal attacks. Conflict becomes ineffective when discussions repeatedly circle the same issues without producing learning, decisions or progress.

14 / Sources of conflict

Four recurring causes

According to mediator Nina Harding, recurring causes include:

  • Communication breakdowns: unclear channels, inconsistent messaging or declining communication quality.
  • Role ambiguity: uncertainty over responsibilities, ownership and decision-making authority.
  • Information barriers: unclear data ownership or difficulty accessing necessary information.
  • Project pressures: budget, resource or scheduling constraints that develop into interpersonal problems.
15 / Positions and interests

What people say they want, and why they want it

One of the most important distinctions in conflict resolution is between positions — what people say they want — and interests — why they want it.

Positions — above the surface Interests — beneath the surface
Explicit requests, demands or refusals Underlying needs, fears, concerns and motivations
Usually visible in a disagreement Often unspoken because they expose vulnerability
Can appear incompatible May reveal opportunities for mutual benefit
Encourage negotiation over demands Enable more sustainable solutions

Identifying underlying interests

  • Ask “Why is this important?” rather than immediately negotiating demands.
  • Listen for concerns about security, recognition, workload, dependencies or expectations.
  • Avoid becoming absorbed in numerous surface-level complaints when they may share one underlying cause.
  • Seek outcomes that address fundamental needs rather than temporarily suppressing disagreement.
💡

Attack the problem, not the person. Where behaviour is unhelpful, identify its impact and propose an alternative rather than labelling the individual.

16 / Difficult conversations

The WIN approach

Nina Harding’s WIN framework provides a practical way to address problematic behaviour without provoking unnecessary defensiveness.

Step Purpose Example
W — When you… Describe a specific, observable behaviour “When you send multiple separate requests throughout the day…”
I — I feel… Explain the impact on the individual “I find it difficult to maintain focus and prioritise the work.”
N — Next time… Propose a constructive alternative “Next time, could we consolidate requests into one prioritised list?”

The approach works because it:

  • focuses on behaviour rather than character;
  • communicates why the issue matters;
  • offers a clear, future-focused alternative;
  • creates an opportunity for agreement rather than blame.

Language determines how feedback is received

Before addressing conflict:

  • Consider how the other person may interpret the message.
  • Avoid labels, accusations and emotionally charged language.
  • Recognise that the behaviour may be unintentional.
  • Consider the pressures and constraints facing the other person.
  • Frame the discussion around what could work better for both parties.
💡

A constructive message should make behavioural change easier, not force the recipient to defend their identity.

17 / Written communication

Managing conflict in writing

Email and other asynchronous communication can amplify conflict, because tone and intent are easily misinterpreted. Nina Harding recommends:

  1. Slow down: avoid replying immediately when frustrated.
  2. Match the professional tone: respond respectfully, including an appropriate greeting.
  3. Address the substance: acknowledge the other person’s concerns and relevant dependencies.
  4. Identify underlying interests: demonstrate understanding of why the request matters.
  5. Offer a workable alternative: explain constraints and suggest a realistic solution.
  6. Review before sending: ensure the response remains clear, concise, non-blaming and professional.
💡

A useful test: would the message remain reasonable and professional if independently reviewed or presented in a formal dispute?

Example — an unrealistic deadline

Instead of responding defensively, acknowledge the recipient’s dependency, explain the delivery constraint and propose an achievable date.

18 / Culture

Designing a culture that manages conflict

Conflict resolution should be embedded in project culture rather than treated exclusively as a response to disputes. Effective project leadership requires:

Element What it means
Psychological safety People can challenge ideas without fearing personal repercussions
Inclusive participation Quieter contributors have opportunities to question assumptions
Clear responsibilities Ambiguity around ownership and accountability is reduced
Constructive facilitation Disagreement remains focused on the problem and potential solutions
Early intervention Emerging tensions are addressed before they affect trust or delivery

This applies beyond immediate project teams, particularly where multiple stakeholders, disciplines or geographically distributed groups must collaborate.

💡

The project manager’s role is not simply to direct work but to enable productive disagreement, preserve trust and keep the team moving toward shared outcomes.

19 / Analytical takeaways

What effective conflict management looks like

  • Diagnose the source before resolving the dispute. Apparent personality conflicts may originate from unclear ownership, inaccessible information or conflicting dependencies.
  • Negotiate interests rather than positions. Understanding what stakeholders fundamentally need creates more options than debating their initial demands.
  • Use WIN for behaviour-related conflict. Specific observations, communicated impact and actionable alternatives reduce the likelihood of defensive escalation.
  • Treat communication quality as a project risk. Unresolved interpersonal conflict can convert manageable resource or scheduling issues into delivery delays and disengagement.
  • Assess whether disagreement creates progress. Effective conflict management is demonstrated by better decisions, sustained working relationships and the team’s continued ability to collaborate — not simply by the absence of complaints.
