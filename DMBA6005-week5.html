COSTING
AND ESTIMATION

Agile fixes budget and timeline and lets scope move — which is often mistaken for financial freedom. Week 5 works through what it costs to keep that door open, how to choose a financial lens rather than default to one, and what happens to project selection once the criteria that matter stop being financial at all.

The testYou have it when you can argue both sides of a project that came in on time and on budget and still should not have been funded.

01 / The core idea

Flexible scope does not mean flexible cost

Agile commonly fixes budget and timeline while allowing scope to evolve. That trade is often read as financial freedom, and it is not. Iteration keeps the door open for cost to escalate long after the project was approved.

  • Expanding stakeholder requirements
  • Additional sprints
  • Larger or more specialised teams
  • Increasing cloud/infrastructure usage
  • Accumulated technical debt

Cost therefore needs to be tracked and forecast continuously, rather than assessed only when the project is approved.

Agile provides flexibility in what gets delivered, not immunity from financial constraints

02 / The managerial objective

Value rather than activity

The purpose of cost control is not simply staying within the budget; it is ensuring expenditure continues to produce value rather than activity. The distinction matters because the two look identical on a burndown chart.

In technology projects, evolving scope can make a project appear productive while its economics progressively deteriorate. Managers should therefore continually test whether the incremental value of further development justifies its incremental cost.

Is this sprint producing value, or producing activity?

03 / Framing the project

Three conditions, then the numbers

Before comparing financial returns, three conditions should exist.

  1. Need — a legitimate organisational reason for the project
  2. Funds — sufficient financial capacity to undertake it
  3. Management will — meaningful senior commitment to implementing it, not merely approval on paper

The financial case then depends partly on why the project exists at all.

Project driverFinancial implication
ProblemJustify expenditure against the cost/value of solving it
OpportunityDemonstrate the value that can be captured
DirectiveFinancial evaluation may support how to deliver rather than whether to comply
  • Directives may arise from legislation or head-office requirements
  • Technology projects can be harder to justify because novel projects often lack a reliable historical track record
  • A credible financial case therefore improves the likelihood of a project being selected when competing initiatives exist
04 / Choosing the lens

There is no universally correct measure

While NPV, ROI and payback can be seen as possible approaches, there is no universally correct financial measure for every project. The appropriate measure depends on factors including:

  • Project duration
  • Timing of cash flows
  • Capital required upfront
  • Cost of capital
  • Organisational cash availability
  • Broader strategic and implementation considerations

The important skill is therefore not merely calculating a metric, but choosing and interpreting it in the context of the organisation and project.

05 / Net present value

Why cash-flow timing changes attractiveness

NPV becomes particularly useful for longer projects because two investments generating the same nominal total cash benefit can create different economic value depending on when those cash flows occur.

Discount factor = 1 / (1+r)^n

Where r is the discount rate and n is the number of periods into the future. As r or n increases, the present value of a future cash flow falls.

Do not compare long-term projects only on total projected benefits and costs. The cash-flow profile matters as much as the final total

06 / Return on investment

ROI as a short-horizon measure

ROI = (Benefits - Project costs) / Project costs
  • Shows the return generated relative to the amount invested
  • Higher ROI indicates more benefit generated per dollar of project cost
  • Particularly useful as a simple comparison where the investment horizon is sufficiently short that cash-flow timing is less central to the decision

The two measures can legitimately rank the same pair of projects differently, because they are not asking the same question.

07 / Context

The environment can change the decision

Two features of the organisation's financial environment can flip which project is preferable, even when the project forecasts themselves have not moved.

  • Cost of capital — if capital becomes expensive, future cash flows are discounted more heavily, so projects requiring significant upfront investment or delivering benefits later become relatively less attractive
  • Availability of capital — a cash-rich organisation may tolerate a capital-intensive project that another organisation could not realistically fund

Project attractiveness therefore depends not only on expected returns but also on the organisation's financial capacity and constraints. A project should be financially defensible, but financial metrics alone do not determine the correct investment.

Contextualise the project first, then select the financial method that best represents its economics, rather than treating NPV or ROI as universally superior

  • Agile's flexible scope actually increases the need for continuous cost discipline: each additional feature, sprint or capability should still justify its incremental expenditure
  • A project's financial attractiveness is partly a function of its cash-flow timing, not merely the total dollars eventually earned
  • NPV and ROI can legitimately favour different alternatives because they answer different questions; the managerial task is deciding which question matters in the project's context
  • Cost of capital and available cash can change which project is economically preferable even when the underlying forecasts remain unchanged
  • A strong project pitch should establish need + funding capacity + managerial commitment, then demonstrate how the selected financial measure supports the investment decision
08 / The limit of the numbers

Why financial metrics are insufficient

NPV and ROI capture financial attractiveness, but projects also differ in strategic alignment, customer impact, technical feasibility, risk, resource availability and innovation potential.

This matters most in non-profits, public organisations and innovation portfolios, where financial return may not be the primary objective. Even in for-profit organisations, selecting the project with the highest financial return can be strategically inferior if it poorly supports organisational priorities.

Project selection should optimise the organisation's objectives, not merely the project's standalone financial result

09 / Weighted scoring

Making competing priorities explicit

  1. Identify the criteria relevant to project selection.
  2. Assign each criterion a weight based on its importance.
  3. Score each project independently against each criterion.
  4. Multiply each score by its weighting.
  5. Sum the weighted scores to rank the alternatives.
Weighted project score = Σ (criterion score × criterion weight)

Weights should total 100%, and a highly weighted criterion has greater influence on the final decision.

  • Forces managers to articulate what actually matters before choosing between projects
  • Makes trade-offs transparent rather than leaving them implicit
  • Provides a defensible explanation of why one project was selected
  • Can combine financial and non-financial criteria within the same framework

Risk: the numerical result can create false objectivity. The ranking is only as defensible as the weights and scores used to produce it

10 / Balanced scorecard

Causal links, not more KPIs

The balanced scorecard evaluates initiatives across interconnected dimensions: financial outcomes, customer value, internal processes, learning, growth and innovation, and organisational vision and strategy.

Its greater value is not merely producing additional KPIs, but identifying causal relationships between strategic objectives.

Improving internal systems → enables a more personalised customer experience → supports customer value → contributes to profitability
  • Balanced scorecards can become overly elaborate and bureaucratic
  • Large frameworks may generate extensive KPIs without necessarily improving the underlying decision
  • They may therefore fit large formal organisations better than highly Agile, people-centred environments

Examine the interdependencies and causal links between strategic outcomes rather than treating each KPI independently

11 / Portfolio level

Risk balanced across projects, not within one

Organisations should not assess every project as though it must independently represent the safest or highest-return option. A portfolio can deliberately contain both:

  • lower-risk, incremental projects producing predictable returns; and
  • higher-risk experiments offering disproportionate upside.

The appropriate mix depends on the organisation's risk appetite, strategic objectives and operating environment. A startup may tolerate considerably more experimentation than a government organisation whose priorities include reliability, compliance and continuity.

12 / Strategic randomness

Why deliberate exploration is necessary

If an organisation continually directs resources toward the opportunities already known to work, it becomes increasingly efficient at exploiting existing value — and increasingly exposed.

  • existing opportunities may disappear;
  • customer preferences may change;
  • technology may shift;
  • competitors may disrupt the existing model;
  • the organisation may become dependent on a narrow source of revenue.

Strategic randomness means deliberately creating space for controlled experimentation, divergent thinking and initiatives whose outcomes cannot be confidently predicted. The objective is not randomness for its own sake, but a portfolio in which most resources exploit known opportunities, a smaller proportion explores uncertain ones, failure among exploratory projects is expected, and occasional large successes compensate for unsuccessful experiments.

If experimentation is expected to have a low success rate, individual failures do not demonstrate that the strategy has failed

13 / Judging failure

Project failure is not portfolio failure

Suppose ten experimental initiatives are funded: nine generate little or no commercial return, and one identifies a major new business opportunity.

Evaluating each initiative independently would make the portfolio appear badly managed. Evaluating the combined portfolio outcome may show that the nine unsuccessful experiments were effectively the cost of discovering the one outsized winner.

Project failure ≠ portfolio failure

The management danger is that organisations unintentionally eliminate innovation by terminating experimentation after several unsuccessful attempts — funding only initiatives with predictable outcomes, and progressively removing the very uncertainty from which breakthrough opportunities emerge.

  • Too much exploration consumes resources without generating sufficient reliable cash flow

How much uncertainty can the organisation afford while preserving enough exploration to create its next source of growth?

15 / Concentration risk

The gorilla client

A gorilla client is a customer responsible for a disproportionately large share of the organisation's business. Commercially attractive in the short term, it creates strategic vulnerability:

  • bargaining power shifts toward the customer;
  • managers may become reluctant to challenge the client;
  • resources become increasingly tailored to one relationship;
  • loss of the client can threaten the entire organisation.

This is another form of over-exploitation: the company becomes extremely efficient around one existing source of value while neglecting alternatives.

Diversification creates resilience even where concentration appears financially attractive today

16 / Permission to fail

Incentives shape exploration

Strategic randomness cannot exist if employees are penalised whenever an experiment fails. If performance systems reward only predictable delivery, KPI achievement and short-term financial returns, employees rationally avoid uncertain initiatives.

An organisation claiming to value innovation therefore needs mechanisms that distinguish disciplined experiments that fail from poorly managed projects. Failure should not automatically be rewarded, but intelligent experimentation and learning must be protected.

17 / Defining success

Outputs versus outcomes

Traditional project success emphasises schedule, cost and scope. These remain important measures of project execution, but they do not establish whether the project created value. A project can meet all three constraints and still fail if:

  • customers reject the solution;
  • the wrong problem was solved;
  • stakeholders gain little value;
  • the delivery process damages the team.

Conversely, a project may exceed its original budget or schedule yet create significant strategic or customer value. True project success is primarily about outcomes, not outputs — value delivered, user and stakeholder satisfaction, strategic contribution, sustainability of the outcome, and the health and capability of the delivery team.

Triple constraint asks: “Did we deliver the project as planned?”
Strategic evaluation asks: “Was it the right project, and did it create the intended value?”
  • Financial optimisation at the individual-project level can produce a strategically fragile portfolio; organisations need to optimise the portfolio, not every experiment
  • Exploration and exploitation are complementary capabilities: exploitation funds today's business, while exploration protects tomorrow's
  • A high failure rate can be rational where experiments are deliberately bounded and the potential winners have asymmetric upside
  • Weighted scoring helps expose managerial priorities, but assigning numbers does not remove judgement — it merely makes that judgement visible
  • Project success should ultimately be assessed through value and outcomes, with cost, scope and schedule treated as execution measures rather than the complete definition of success
