01 / The core idea

Project risk cuts both ways

💡

Project risk is an uncertain event or condition that could positively or negatively affect project outcomes.

Effective risk management aims to:

  • Identify threats before they become costly surprises.
  • Reduce uncertainty around delivery, cost and customer requirements.
  • Prepare responses rather than simply reacting when problems occur.
  • Identify opportunities that could increase project value.

Categories of project risk

Risk category Source of uncertainty Potential impact
MarketCompetitor disruption, changing demandReduced revenue or strategic relevance
FinancialBudget overruns, funding shortagesReduced profitability or project cancellation
TechnologyUnproven technology, integration failureRework, delays or infeasibility
PeopleStaff departures, skill shortages, team conflictLost expertise and reduced delivery capacity
ProcessIneffective workflows, poor governanceQuality failures and missed risks
Cyber/dataBreaches, outages, data lossFinancial, operational and reputational damage
RegulatoryLegal or compliance failuresPenalties, restrictions or mandatory remediation

Process risk has an additional dimension: the method used to identify and analyse risks can itself determine which risks are discovered. An ineffective assessment process creates blind spots.

02 / Leadership and management

Leading risk and managing it

The lecturer distinguishes two complementary perspectives.

Leadership — Chris Management — Dirk
Encourages experimentation and innovationControls and mitigates exposure
Accepts uncertainty to pursue opportunitiesIdentifies potential failures and responses
Creates willingness to take calculated risksKeeps risk within acceptable boundaries
💡

Strategic tension: eliminating risk can eliminate innovation, while pursuing innovation without controls can threaten project viability.

The greatest risk: lack of imagination

The lecturer argues that failing to imagine what could go wrong is itself a major project risk.

  • Risks may remain invisible because existing assumptions are never challenged.
  • Individual problems may appear manageable but become serious when their combined impact is considered.
  • Assessing the project from alternative perspectives can reveal overlooked threats.

Managerial implication: risk identification requires creative thinking and systems-level analysis, not merely completing a checklist.

03 / Threats and opportunities

Negative and positive risk

Best-practice risk management considers both threats and opportunities.

Negative risk — threat Positive risk — opportunity
An event could reduce project valueAn event could increase project value
Objective: prevent or reduce lossesObjective: capture or enhance benefits
Example: an integration failure delays launchExample: unexpected demand accelerates adoption
Example — AI financial advice platform

A sudden surge in users following media exposure is a positive risk because it creates growth potential. However, without sufficient infrastructure capacity or operational support, the company may fail to capture that opportunity.

💡

A positive risk still requires planning, capacity and an appropriate response.

Black swan events and resilience

Black swan events are highly unexpected, consequential events that challenge established assumptions. Their specific timing or form may be impossible to predict, but organisations can prepare for disruption more generally through:

  • contingency planning;
  • operational resilience;
  • response procedures;
  • organisational readiness to adapt.

The objective is not perfect prediction, but the capacity to respond when assumptions fail.

04 / Probability and impact

Two dimensions, assessed together

The lecture identifies two central dimensions:

  • Probability: likelihood that a risk event will occur.
  • Impact: consequences for project cost, schedule, quality, objectives or value.

A risk’s importance depends on both dimensions, rather than either in isolation. A common simplified prioritisation measure is:

Risk exposure = Probability × Impact

[Added context — the formula is a common quantitative expression of the lecture’s two dimensions; the transcript does not prescribe a particular calculation method.]

This provides an initial way to compare risks, although it does not fully capture dependencies or unusually severe consequences.

05 / Continuous risk management

Risk management runs the length of the project

Risk management must operate throughout the project, not only during initial planning.

What a risk management plan should establish

  • methodology and risk categories;
  • roles, responsibilities and risk ownership;
  • probability and impact assessments;
  • budget and schedule implications;
  • stakeholder risk tolerances;
  • response strategies and monitoring arrangements.

The plan must remain a living document, updated as new risks emerge and existing responses are evaluated.

06 / Capacity and appetite

Risk capacity and risk appetite

Concept Meaning Management question
Risk capacityMaximum exposure the organisation can objectively absorbHow much risk can the organisation withstand?
Risk appetiteExposure leadership is willing to accept to achieve objectivesHow much risk does the organisation choose to take?

Risk capacity is influenced by financial strength, operational resilience and legal obligations. Risk appetite reflects strategy, organisational culture, leadership preferences and stakeholder expectations.

Example — capacity versus appetite

A financially strong technology company may have the capacity to fund several experimental products but choose a low risk appetite for systems handling sensitive customer information.

💡

Having the capacity to absorb a loss does not mean the organisation is willing to accept it.

07 / Risk at scale

Project, program and portfolio risk

Level Focus Distinctive risk consideration
ProjectDelivering an individual initiativeLocal cost, schedule, technical and people risks
ProgramCoordinating related projects to achieve shared benefitsInterdependencies and cascading failures
PortfolioManaging projects and programs against strategic objectivesAggregate risk, diversification and capital allocation

Program risk: a delay in one project can affect others through shared resources, dependencies or linked deliverables. Portfolio risk: projects need not be directly related. Management can balance initiatives with different risk-return profiles to reduce overall organisational exposure.

08 / Modern Portfolio Theory

Selecting projects as a portfolio

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) provides a financial framework for balancing expected return and risk across a portfolio. Applied conceptually to project selection:

  • Different projects contribute different expected benefits and exposures.
  • Projects whose outcomes are not closely correlated can diversify risk.
  • A portfolio can combine experimental, high-potential initiatives with more predictable projects.
  • Project selection should consider the combined portfolio risk, not only each project’s individual attractiveness.

Diversification benefits depend on how risks move together; projects exposed to the same underlying disruption may not provide meaningful diversification.

💡

The highest-return individual projects do not necessarily produce the most resilient or strategically desirable portfolio.

09 / Cyber risk

Cyber risk and regulatory obligations

Cyber incidents can create several simultaneous exposures:

  • operational disruption;
  • loss or compromise of customer data;
  • regulatory and legal consequences;
  • reputational damage and declining customer trust.

The lecturer emphasises prompt incident response.

Risk management must distinguish between voluntary best practices and mandatory legal obligations. The lecture highlights Australian data-breach notification and ransomware/cyber incident reporting requirements. However, these involve different legal thresholds, reporting triggers and deadlines; they should not be treated as interchangeable.

10 / Beyond initial delivery

Risk does not end at launch

In digital products, risk management continues after release because the service evolves through ongoing improvements and changing user requirements. CI/CD supports this by making iterative delivery and validation part of the operating model, rather than treating launch as the final project outcome.

The broader principle is designing for adaptability:

  • anticipate future changes in requirements and operating conditions;
  • maintain the ability to improve and recover services;
  • preserve product relevance and value beyond initial delivery.
💡

A project that meets its launch deadline but cannot adapt economically to future conditions may still destroy long-term value.

11 / Analytical takeaways

What understanding risk asks of a manager

  • Risk management is a strategic activity, not merely a control function. Organisations must decide which uncertainties create worthwhile opportunities and which threaten their objectives.
  • The quality of risk identification matters as much as the assessment itself. Managers should question assumptions, examine interacting risks and actively seek alternative perspectives.
  • Assess risk against both appetite and capacity. A project may be financially survivable yet inconsistent with the organisation’s chosen exposure or stakeholder obligations.
  • Risk becomes systemic at scale. Program managers must control cascading dependencies, while portfolio managers should evaluate aggregate exposure and diversification.
  • Risk management effectiveness depends on continuous adaptation. The critical question is whether identified risks have owners, credible responses and ongoing monitoring — and whether the organisation can respond to surprises without undermining long-term value.
12 / Eight techniques

Risk identification techniques

Risk identification requires structured analysis and creative thinking to uncover threats and opportunities that conventional planning may overlook. The lecture introduces eight techniques, each offering a different perspective on uncertainty.

13 / Brainstorming vs Delphi

Taking the group pressure out

Brainstorming can generate ideas quickly but is vulnerable to groupthink, dominant personalities and premature agreement. The Delphi Technique reduces these influences through:

  1. Individual consultation with subject-matter experts.
  2. Anonymous consolidation of their responses.
  3. Distribution of the combined findings to participants.
  4. Further rounds of feedback and refinement.

The emphasis is on evaluating ideas independently of who proposed them, rather than simply reaching majority agreement. A minority opinion may identify a risk that the wider group has overlooked.

💡

Delphi is particularly useful when expert status, organisational hierarchy or group pressure could distort long-term risk assessments.

14 / SWIFT

Structured “What If”

SWIFT explores how an initial disruption can create subsequent problems. The notes work it through for a SaaS platform.

💡

Repeated questioning reveals second-order consequences, rather than treating the initial event as an isolated risk.

15 / Scenarios and simulation

Scenario analysis and Monte Carlo simulation

Scenario analysis considers how different conditions could develop and influence project outcomes over time.

  • Near-term events may create longer-term consequences.
  • Uncertainty can increase as the planning horizon extends.
  • Alternative scenarios help managers prepare for different outcomes.

Monte Carlo simulation extends probabilistic scenario analysis by repeatedly sampling uncertain inputs to generate a distribution of possible results. [Added context — mathematical interpretation] For example, instead of assuming a fixed project completion date, a simulation can estimate the probability of meeting different deadlines based on uncertainty in task durations.

The managerial value is understanding the range and likelihood of outcomes, rather than relying on one forecast.

16 / Working backwards

Fault Tree Analysis and reverse brainstorming

The lecture connects FTA with reverse brainstorming: starting with an undesirable outcome and exploring how it could occur.

💡

Key distinction: reverse brainstorming creatively identifies ways a service could fail; formal FTA structures the causal relationships between failures.

17 / Risk register

From identified risk to owned risk

A risk register converts identified risks into documented, assigned and monitorable items.

Field Purpose
Risk ID and descriptionIdentifies the potential event
Category and root causeExplains the source of uncertainty
Probability and impactSupports risk prioritisation
TriggersIndicates when the risk may be developing
Planned responseSpecifies how the exposure will be managed
Risk ownerEstablishes accountability for monitoring and action
💡

Risk ownership is critical: identifying a risk without assigning responsibility creates documentation rather than effective management.

The register should remain dynamic, with risks reassessed as conditions, evidence and project priorities change.

18 / Heat map

The probability–impact matrix

A heat map plots risks against their likelihood of occurring and severity of consequences.

Risk level Typical management response
ExtremeImmediate intervention; eliminate or fundamentally redesign unacceptable exposure
HighPrioritised treatment and close monitoring
ModeratePlanned controls and periodic review
LowAccept or monitor where appropriate

A heat map provides a quick view of which risks require attention and how exposure changes over time.

💡

Important: risk ratings must be evaluated against organisational risk appetite; a low estimated probability does not automatically make a catastrophic risk acceptable.

19 / Tracking over time

Top Ten tracking and expert judgement

The Top Ten approach periodically ranks the most important project risks. Unlike a static priority list, it also considers how long each risk has remained unresolved.

Risk A

Highest priority, identified this week.

Risk B

Moderate priority, unresolved for six weeks.

Risk B may deserve increased attention because persistent exposure can indicate ineffective mitigation or neglected responsibilities. The technique helps detect risks that remain consistently problematic without becoming the single highest priority.

Expert judgement

Expert judgement supplements quantitative assessments through the experience of people familiar with comparable projects, technologies or operating environments. It is particularly valuable when historical data is insufficient or risks are difficult to quantify. However, expert conclusions should still be challenged and revisited when new evidence becomes available.

20 / Responding to threats

Negative risk response strategies

Once risks have been identified and prioritised, project managers select an appropriate treatment strategy.

Strategy Meaning Technology example
AvoidChange the plan to eliminate the threatRemove an experimental dependency
AcceptAcknowledge exposure without taking additional preventive actionAccept a minor, low-impact UI defect
TransferShift specified financial or contractual consequences to another partyPurchase cyber insurance
MitigateReduce the probability or impactIntroduce redundancy, testing or safeguards

Risk acceptance is appropriate where the expected benefit of additional treatment does not justify its cost, subject to organisational risk tolerance.

💡

Risk transfer does not necessarily eliminate accountability: outsourcing a service may transfer contractual responsibility while leaving customer trust, operational and regulatory exposure with the organisation.

21 / Analytical takeaways

Using the tools well

  • Use multiple identification techniques. Checklists detect known failures, while SWIFT, scenario analysis and reverse brainstorming help uncover unexpected dependencies and consequences.
  • Reduce bias when uncertainty is high. Delphi can reveal valuable minority perspectives that dominant individuals or groupthink might otherwise suppress.
  • Risk registers must drive action. Every material risk requires ownership, indicators, a response and ongoing reassessment.
  • Prioritisation should be dynamic. Heat maps identify current exposure, while Top Ten tracking highlights persistent problems that static rankings may overlook.
  • Choose responses based on exposure, treatment cost and risk appetite. The objective is not zero risk, but controlled exposure consistent with project and organisational objectives.
