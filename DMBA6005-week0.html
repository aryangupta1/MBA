The $RUs digital innovation case

A Sydney financial services firm wants younger clients, and has three digital options on the table. The whole of Week 0 is the argument that you cannot choose between them until you know which problem you actually have — plus how Shadow Boxing, this course's decision-first teaching method, works.

00 / Start here

How to master this week

Week 0 is one case and one teaching method, so the whole pass fits inside about an hour and a quarter. The route below gets the diagnose-before-you-choose move into my hands rather than just into my notes.

  1. Read sections 01 to 05 in order

    ~15 min

    The five case sections build a single argument, so reading them out of order breaks it: the firm’s strength, why it meets clients too late, the four candidate problems, what each option buys and risks, then the decision rule. Slow right down on section 03 — the four diagnoses are the hinge the rest of the page turns on. Leave section 06 on Shadow Boxing until the end; it is how the course teaches, not part of the case.

  2. Fix the four diagnoses in place

    ~10 min

    Twelve of the fourteen terms belong to the $RUs case, and the four problem types are the ones worth memorising word for word. Engagement and conversion are the pair that trip people up — one is a prospect who knows the firm but does not find it relevant, the other is a prospect who is interested but never signs. The Acronyms tab holds a single entry and takes seconds, so clear it while you are here.

  3. Drill symptom to option, not definition

    ~10 min

    Most of the twenty-eight cards give a symptom and ask which initiative the guidance points to, which is the shape the exam-style question takes. Push hardest on the three cards that describe a live complaint — enquiries that never convert, prospects who see the firm as for retirees, existing clients frustrated by access — and say the diagnosis out loud before you name the option.

  4. Test the diagnosis under pressure

    ~12 min

    Twelve questions, ten on the case and two on Shadow Boxing. The distractors are built from the confusions this week actually produces — pairing an option with the wrong risk, or answering awareness with a mobile app — so read every note, including on the options you did not pick. If you miss one, the fault is almost always the diagnosis rather than the option.

  5. Argue four judgement calls through

    ~20 min

    Nothing here is calculated; each scenario is a decision you have to defend in prose. The two that matter most are the app proposal with no diagnosis behind it and the pair of conflicting reports from the advisers and the marketing lead, because both force you to say which problem you are backing and what would prove you wrong. Use the hints one at a time and stop as soon as one is enough.

  6. Run the diagnosis from memory

    ~10 min

    Close the page and write out the four candidate problems, then the initiative each one points to and the risk that comes with it. Finish by stating the closing rule in your own words — a clear customer problem, testable with minimal risk — and the sequence Shadow Boxing runs in, since committing before you see the expert is the whole point of the method.

The testThe week has landed when someone can hand me a single symptom from $RUs — a prospect who has never heard of the firm, or one who enquires and goes quiet — and I can name the diagnosis, the initiative it points to and the risk I am taking on, without opening the page.

01 / The firm

Who $RUs is, and what it relies on

$RUs is an established Sydney-based financial services firm with a strong reputation for complex, long-term financial advice — particularly for clients approaching retirement.

Services offered What the current model rests on
Superannuation advice Older and retired clients
Wealth management Referrals from existing members
Tax structuring Personalised, boutique-style service
Trusts Relationships developed later in a client’s financial life
Intergenerational wealth transfer
Domestic and international investment planning
🔑

This model has produced strong service quality, but it may restrict the company’s future growth.

02 / The strategic problem

Arriving too late in the client’s life

Many potential clients only contact $RUs when they are close to retirement. The firm may therefore be missing people in their 30s and 40s, when important financial decisions are often being made.

  • establishing investment strategies
  • creating trusts
  • planning intergenerational wealth transfers
  • structuring tax arrangements
  • investing in domestic or international markets

The digital capability gap

The CEO recognised that $RUs may be falling behind digitally, but the company does not yet have a clear digital strategy. Three possible directions are on the table.

  1. Online Advertising
  2. Social Media Engagement
  3. Mobile Application
❓

Which digital initiative would create the most meaningful connection with younger potential clients?

03 / Diagnosis first

Four candidate problems, not one

The three options address different parts of the customer journey, so $RUs must first determine which problem it actually has.

🔑

Without diagnosing the correct problem, the company could invest in technology that does address its real strategic need.

04 / Option assessment

What each initiative buys, and what it risks

Each option has a distinct main purpose. Setting the purpose beside the risk is what makes them comparable.

Option Benefits Risks
Online advertising Fast visibility, targeted campaigns, measurable enquiries May generate clicks without trust or suitable clients
Social media Builds credibility, educates younger clients, supports long-term engagement Requires consistent content and may take time to produce results
Mobile app Improves convenience and digital service for existing clients Expensive to build and may not attract new clients
05 / The decision rule

Match the initiative to the diagnosis

The key issue is identifying whether $RUs needs to improve awareness, engagement, or the client experience. Each answer points to a different initiative.

🔑

$RUs should not adopt technology simply to appear modern. The best option is the one that solves a clear customer problem and can be tested with minimal risk.

06 / How this course teaches

Shadow Boxing

Shadow Boxing is an interactive learning approach that places students in realistic project scenarios where they must make decisions, experience the consequences, compare their thinking with industry experts, and discuss outcomes with peers. Students participate in the decision rather than being told the “correct” answer.

What it is for

  • Apply theoretical concepts to realistic problems
  • Experience the uncertainty found in real projects
  • Understand that multiple valid solutions may exist
  • Reflect on how experienced professionals approach the same situation
  • Learn through discussion with peers
🔑

Shadow boxing is a learning activity, not an assessment. Your choices do not affect your marks — they only change the learning path you experience.

07 / Takeaways

What to carry out of Week 0

  • $RUs’ strength — deep, personalised advice to older clients — is also what limits its growth.
  • The firm meets clients too late: the decisions it advises on are made in people’s 30s and 40s.
  • Three options are on the table, and they solve three different problems, not one.
  • Diagnose before choosing: awareness, engagement, conversion or service experience.
  • Online advertising buys visibility; social media buys trust; a mobile app buys service quality.
  • The best option solves a clear customer problem and can be tested with minimal risk.
  • Shadow Boxing teaches by making you decide first and compare with an expert afterwards.
