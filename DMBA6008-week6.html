EQUITY HURDLE RATE
AND VALUATION APPLICATIONS

What shareholders must be paid for the risk they bear, and what a business is worth when there is no share price to read it from. Week 6 prices market risk with beta and the CAPM, shows how debt turns a business beta into a higher equity hurdle rate, then values two companies the market cannot price directly — an early-stage venture worked backwards from its exit value, and a private company benchmarked against listed comparables and discounted for being private.

01 / The core idea

Diversifiable risk versus market risk

CAPM assumes investors hold well-diversified portfolios. Investors therefore receive no additional return for risks that can be diversified away — firm and industry-specific shocks can be offset by holding investments exposed differently to the same event.

Investors instead require compensation for market risk: risks that broadly affect companies and cannot easily be removed through diversification. The notes give recession, major geopolitical disruption and pandemic as examples.

💡

CAPM = Capital asset pricing model

02 / Managerial implication

Which risk sets the required return

The relevant risk for determining shareholders’ required return is therefore systematic / market exposure, not every risk facing the company. A company exposed more strongly to market movements must offer investors a higher expected return.

  • Priced: systematic / market exposure.
  • Not priced: risks the investor can diversify away.
  • Stronger exposure to market movements → a higher expected return must be offered.
03 / Measuring market risk

What beta measures

Beta (β) measures how the return on an individual company’s shares moves relative to the overall market. The notes read it against a benchmark of 1.0.

Beta Interpretation Risk implication
β = 1.0 Moves approximately in line with market Average market exposure
β > 1.0 Moves more than market Greater market risk
β < 1.0 Moves less than market Lower market risk

β = 1.2

A 1% market movement is associated with roughly a 1.2% movement in the stock in the same direction. The company therefore has approximately 20% greater sensitivity to market movements.

04 / Estimating and reading beta

Where beta comes from and what it reflects

Beta is estimated from historical stock returns relative to market returns — approximately five years of monthly share-price data.

The number has an economic reading. Cyclical businesses are more likely to have higher betas because their demand and cash flows respond strongly to economic conditions. More defensive businesses tend to have lower betas because demand remains more stable through economic cycles.

Tech example

A highly cyclical semiconductor company may experience amplified gains during an investment boom and amplified losses during a downturn → higher beta and therefore a higher required equity return.

💡

Beta captures the company’s historical sensitivity to broad market conditions

05 / The model

CAPM turns market risk into a hurdle rate

CAPM converts market risk into the required return to shareholders, or equity hurdle rate.

re = rf + βi(MRP)

Where:

Variable Meaning Logic
re Required return on equity Shareholders’ hurdle rate
rf Risk-free rate Compensation for time value of money
MRP Market risk premium Extra return investors require for holding shares rather than low-risk assets
βi Individual stock beta Scales the market premium for the company’s market risk
06 / The components

Risk-free rate, market risk premium and beta

The risk-free rate rf is the minimum return required even without equity risk. It is typically proxied by a government bond yield, reflecting government borrowing as relatively low risk.

The market risk premium is the historical excess return earned by shares relative to low-risk government bonds. Australian estimates have historically been around 6–7%. It represents compensation for accepting equity-market risk.

MRP = Rmarket -Rf

Beta adjusts the market premium to reflect the particular company’s sensitivity to market movements: higher β → greater required return → higher equity hurdle rate.

07 / Analytical takeaways

Risk alone can make an investment less attractive

The notes close on what diversification, beta and CAPM change about the way a project is judged.

  • Diversification changes which risks should be priced. Investors are compensated for market risk, not risks they can cheaply eliminate themselves.
  • Beta is economically important because it links the company’s exposure to recessions and other broad shocks directly to its required shareholder return.
  • A rising beta increases the equity hurdle rate even if the company’s expected cash flows have not changed. Risk alone can therefore make an investment less attractive.
  • CAPM forces managers to ask whether expected returns adequately compensate shareholders for the company’s market exposure, rather than simply whether a project is profitable in absolute terms.
❓

Is that return sufficient for the systematic risk shareholders must bear?

08 / The core idea

Capital structure changes equity risk

The notes separate two betas. Unlevered beta (βU) measures the company’s market risk assuming no debt financing — it reflects the underlying business and assets before financial leverage is introduced. Levered beta (βL) measures equity risk after debt is included in the capital structure.

Debt introduces financial risk because interest obligations remain even when operating profit falls, so shareholder returns become more volatile.

💡

Higher debt → greater ROE volatility → higher leveraged beta → higher equity hurdle rate

09 / Why leverage magnifies returns

Same EBIT, different return to shareholders

The underlying business can generate the same EBIT under different financing structures, but the return to shareholders changes, because debt reduces the equity base and introduces fixed interest costs. Leverage can therefore increase ROE in favourable conditions even though absolute profit is lower.

This does not represent free value creation: shareholders achieve the higher return by bearing more financial risk. The notes show the other side of that bargain with one operating shock — EBIT falling from $30m to $20m.

Capital structure ROE before shock ROE after shock Change
No debt 21% 14% -7pp
Levered 35% 21% -14pp
💡

A high ROE can partly reflect leverage rather than superior operating performance

10 / The conversion

Converting unlevered beta into levered beta

The relationship is:

βL = βU [1 + (1 − T) D/E]

Where:

Variable Meaning
βL Levered equity beta
βU Unlevered beta
D / E Debt-to-equity ratio
T Corporate tax rate
11 / Interpretation

Business risk plus financial risk

Higher D/E means a higher levered beta, because shareholders absorb more financial risk. The (1 − T) term reflects the notion that interest is tax deductible, reducing the effective impact of debt.

The formula separates the two kinds of risk: business risk, captured through unlevered beta, and financial risk, added through leverage.

12 / Effect on the hurdle rate

The equity hurdle rate rises with leverage

Use the levered beta when determining the return required by shareholders of a leveraged company. The notes work one example from the inputs below and step the debt-to-equity ratio up.

Given

Risk-free rate

Core insight

  • Increasing debt can magnify shareholder returns, but investors recognise the extra risk.
  • They therefore demand a higher required return, which raises the equity hurdle rate.
  • More leverage is therefore not automatically beneficial simply because ROE rises.
13 / Analytical takeaways

Reading ROE with the leverage in view

The notes close with four observations and one open question.

  • ROE must be interpreted alongside leverage: a higher ROE may reflect a smaller equity base and greater financial risk rather than better operating performance.
  • Unlevered beta isolates the company’s underlying business risk; levered beta shows the risk actually borne by shareholders under the chosen capital structure.
  • Increasing debt can make equity appear more profitable in good conditions while making shareholder returns more vulnerable to operating shocks.
  • Capital structure therefore affects valuation indirectly through the required return: more leverage → higher beta → higher cost of equity.
❓

Does the additional return sufficiently compensate shareholders for the additional financial risk?

14 / The core idea

Why early-stage valuation is different

Early stage ventures sit in a particular position. They may have:

  • A functioning business and proof of concept
  • Some revenue/profit potential
  • Insufficient history or stability to raise debt
  • Founders who have exhausted bootstrapping, family funding and grants

The next major funding source may therefore be venture capital (VC) — equity investment rather than lending.

15 / Required return

Uncertainty sets the hurdle

VC investors face substantial uncertainty around:

  • Successful commercialisation
  • Market adoption
  • Competitive technologies
  • Execution by the founding team

Their required return can therefore be far above the conventional equity hurdle rate derived from the CAPM.

💡

Higher uncertainty → higher required return → heavier discounting of future value → lower valuation today

16 / Step 1: value at exit

The model works backwards from exit

The venture capital valuation model works backwards from the VC’s expected exit value. The first step is to set the assumptions and project what the business could be worth when the VC exits.

Input Example
Investment horizon 5 years
Forecast EBIT at exit $30m
Exit multiple 8x EBIT
VC required return 60% p.a.
Funding required $8m
Existing shares 100,000

The projected enterprise value at exit is:

EV5 = EBIT5 × Exit Multiple EV5 = $30m × 8 = $240m
  • The 8x EBIT multiple reflects what investors believe a comparable business could be worth at exit
  • Possible exits include the sale of the VC stake, an IPO, or acquisition by a strategic buyer
17 / Step 2: discount to today

Discount exit value back to today

PV = Exit Value / (1 + r)n

Where r is the VC required annual return and n is the investment horizon.

PV = $240m / (1.60)5 = $22.888m

Interpretation

The extreme reduction is driven by the very high risk-adjusted hurdle rate.

💡

The forecast $30m EBIT and resulting $240m exit value assume the venture receives funding required to scale. Without capital, the forecast operating outcome may not be achievable

18 / Step 3: the VC stake

Determine the VC ownership stake

For instance, if the VC contributes $8m into a business valued at $22.888m after funding:

VC Ownership = Investment / Post-funding Value = 8 / 22.888 = 34.95%

This dilution can appear severe, but the relevant comparison is not percentage ownership alone.

💡

Founder trade-off: a smaller percentage of a substantially more valuable business may be preferable to retaining a larger percentage of a venture that cannot fund its growth

19 / Pre-funding value

Pre-funding value and notional share price

Pre funding valuation

Pre-funding value = Post funding value - New Investment $28.888m - $8m = $14.888m

This represents the value attributable to the existing owners immediately before the VC investment.

Notional share price

For example, with 100,000 existing shares:

Share Price = Pre-funding Value / Existing Shares = $14.888m / 100,000 = $148.88 per share

This is a notional private-company share price, not an observable market price, because the company is not publicly traded.

20 / Analytical takeaways

An exit-value exercise worked backwards

  • Early-stage valuation is fundamentally an exit-value exercise worked backwards: estimate what the business could be worth at exit, then discount aggressively for risk
  • The required return is a major valuation driver. At very high VC hurdle rates, even a large future enterprise value can translate into a relatively modest present valuation
  • Founders should focus on the value created by funding, not dilution in isolation: capital may be necessary to hire, commercialise and reach the projected exit value at all
❓

What is a credible exit value, how risky is achieving it and what ownership stake is required today to earn the target return?

💡

The valuation depends heavily on two assumptions: the exit multiple and the required VC return; small changes in either can materially alter founder dilution and implied value

21 / The core idea

Why private-company valuation is harder

A private company is not listed on a securities exchange, so valuers do not have the same depth of observable market information that is available for listed firms. Valuation therefore relies heavily on benchmarking against comparable public companies with similar industry exposure, risk characteristics and cash flow characteristics.

Those public-company benchmarks usually cannot be applied directly. Private firms may warrant valuation discounts because of:

  • Lower liquidity — ownership stakes may take longer to sell
  • Smaller scale
  • Narrower geographical reach
  • Lower product or customer diversification
  • Dependence on founders, key management or specific technologies
💡

Private-company valuation combines market evidence with substantial expert judgement — partly science, partly art.

22 / Enterprise value and equity value

Where the comparable valuation begins

The comparable valuation begins with enterprise value.

EV = Market Capitalisation + Net Debt

Where:

Net Debt = Debt - Cash

Rearranging:

Equity Value = EV - Net Cash
  • Enterprise value (EV) represents the value of the operating assets/business
  • Subtracting net debt isolates the value attributable to equity holders
23 / Comparable-company valuation

Establish a benchmark, then apply it

Establish an industry benchmark

First, identify relevant listed companies in the same sector, then calculate or observe their:

Enterprise Value / EBIT

Example average benchmark:

EV/EBIT = 16 x

This multiple now becomes the starting benchmark for valuing private companies.

Apply the multiple

Worked example
Private company EBIT
$20m
Estimated enterprise value
16 x $20m, EV = $320m
Net debt
$30m

Equity value = $320m - $30m

Equity value = $290m

💡

The listed company benchmark implies a $290m equity valuation before adjusting for the disadvantages of being privately held.

24 / Capitalisation rate

An alternative expression of the multiple

The 16x EBIT multiple can also be expressed as a capitalisation rate.

Cap Rate = 1/16 = 6.25%

Therefore:

EV = EBIT / Cap Rate EV = $20m / 0.0625 = $320m
  • This is mathematically another way of expressing the same valuation relationship
  • The cap-rate terminology is particularly common in sectors such as property
25 / Private company discount

Why the benchmark is adjusted, and by how much

Why the benchmark is adjusted

A $290m equity value comes from listed-company comparables, but the private company may carry additional risks that those firms do not. Examples include:

  • Difficulty selling an ownership stake
  • Limited product diversity
  • Smaller customer base
  • Dependence on key executives or founders
  • Reliance on a specific technology

Applying the discount

Assuming a 40% private-company discount:

Adjusted Equity Value = $290m × (1 − 0.40) Adjusted Equity Value = $174m
💡

The 40% is not mechanically generated by the valuation model. It reflects the valuer’s judgement based on the company’s characteristics and previous comparable transactions/valuations.

26 / Notional share value

From adjusted equity value to a price per share

With 5 million private-company shares outstanding:

Share Value = Adjusted Equity Value / Shares Outstanding = $174m / 5m $34.80 per share

This is a notional private-company share price rather than an exchange-traded market price. It reflects both:

  1. Public-company comparable evidence
  2. Adjustments for private-company-specific risk
27 / Analytical takeaways

Evidence, comparability and judgement

The notes close with three observations and one question to ask of any private-company valuation.

  • Private company valuation usually begins with observable public-company evidence, then adjusts that evidence for differences between the target and its listed peers.
  • Comparable multiples are only as useful as the comparability of the firms selected; differences in scale, risk, product depth and cash flows can materially distort the result.
  • The private-company discount can have a major valuation impact and is inherently judgemental, making the assumptions behind it as important as the headline valuation.
❓

Are the selected public comparables genuinely similar, and what private-company risks justify the discount being applied?

💡

Private company valuation is less about finding a single objectively correct number and more about constructing a defensible valuation range from market benchmarks plus justified adjustments.
