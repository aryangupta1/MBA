DMBA 6005 · Agile Project Development · Week 2

Agile, customer experience and winning projects

Three strands. What makes an experience valuable rather than merely usable, where Agile came from and what each Scrum role is actually for, and why winning starts before anyone writes the bid.

Pre-live session 60 key terms 84 flashcards
01 / The core idea

Customer experience beyond the product

Customer experience (CX) looks beyond the product itself to the customer’s entire experience with an organisation. Value is created across multiple touch points — every interaction between the customer and the organisation. A strong CX therefore considers the full relationship, not merely whether the core product works.

Product vs experience

A customer may purchase a specific product, but their perception of value is shaped by the surrounding experience:

Product focus Experience focus
What is delivered? How is the customer treated throughout?
Individual transaction Ongoing relationship
Product functionality Total value across touch points
Immediate sale Long-term customer value

Tech example

For SaaS platforms, value is not only the software features. It also includes onboarding, account setup, support, billing, upgrades, reliability and renewal.

03 / The evolution

Three waves of customer experience

CX has developed through three broad waves. Each wave brought a main focus, and each carried its own limitation or development requirement.

04 / The measure

Limitation of CX metrics

Measures such as NPS can be useful signals. However, once organisations pressure customers or employees to achieve particular scores, the measure can lose meaning. Managers should therefore treat CX metrics as evidence for investigation, not as the objective itself.

💡

Improving the score is not necessarily the same as improving the customer experience.

05 / The levels

From UI to customer experience

Digital experiences operate at several levels.

Level Focus
Design The problem and proposed solution
UI How the user directly interacts with the digital product
UX How effectively and easily the user experiences that interaction
CX The broader relationship across all organisational touchpoints

CX is therefore broader than UX.

💡

A hotel booking platform may have excellent UX, but the overall CX can still be poor if refunds are difficult, customer support is slow or billing is unclear.

06 / The horizon

CX as long-term value creation

Organisations should not evaluate customers solely through the profitability of a single transaction. A valuable experience can generate:

  • Repeat purchases
  • Retention
  • Ongoing service revenue
  • Recommendations
  • Deeper customer relationships
❓

How do we create enough value that the customer chooses to continue the relationship?

07 / The enabler

Role of digital technology

The combination of mobile, cloud computing, AI and digital channels enables more sophisticated customer experiences. These technologies can make interactions:

  • More personalised
  • More accessible
  • More responsive
  • More integrated across touchpoints

However, technology is an enabler of CX rather than the objective itself. A sophisticated digital interface has little value if it does not solve an important customer problem.

08 / The method

Why agile supports better CX

Rather than developing a complete solution before showing customers or stakeholders:

Frequent feedback reduces the risk of investing significant time and resources into a solution that customers ultimately do not value. This makes agile particularly useful where customer needs are uncertain or evolve during development.

09 / Analytical takeaways

What to carry out of this topic

  • CX is broader than the product: value comes from the complete customer journey.
  • Optimise touchpoints collectively, rather than perfecting one interaction in isolation.
  • Do not confuse CX metrics with CX itself — measures such as NPS are indicators, not outcomes.
  • Long-term relationships matter more than individual transactions when assessing customer value.
  • Digital technologies should improve meaningful customer outcomes, not simply add functionality.
  • Managers should continually ask: Are we building what the customer actually values, and how quickly would we discover if we are wrong?
10 / The origin

Why Agile emerged

Agile developed largely in response to poor software project outcomes under highly sequential, plan-driven approaches. Traditional waterfall development typically ran through a fixed order of stages, on the assumption that requirements could be sufficiently understood before development began.

Why this struggled in digital projects

In complex and technology-driven environments:

  • Customers often cannot fully specify what they need upfront
  • Requirements become clearer once users interact with prototypes
  • Teams inevitably discover mistakes and new information during delivery
  • Technologies and market conditions can change while the project is underway
  • Extensive upfront design becomes expensive if assumptions later prove wrong
🔑

The core problem is uncertainty.

11 / The values

The Agile Manifesto

The manifesto sets out four values. Each is written as a matter of emphasis between two things that both exist in a project, not as a choice between one and the other.

Greater emphasis Rather than over-relying on
Individuals & interactions Processes & tools
Working software Comprehensive documentation
Customer collaboration Contract negotiation
Responding to change Following a plan
💡

The right hand elements still have value. However, agile argues that the left hand elements deserve greater emphasis.

Central philosophy

Agile is fundamentally a set of principles, not a particular meeting structure or project-management tool. Therefore:

Agile ≠ Scrum Agile = Philosophy / principles Scrum = One framework for implementing those principles
12 / The framework

Scrum as an Agile implementation

Scrum introduces enough structure to make adaptability manageable. Teams are empowered to adapt, but operate within clear roles, priorities and short delivery cycles.

Scrum is both:

  • Adaptive at the project level
  • Structured within each delivery cycle
💡

Flexibility without chaos.

13 / The roles

Scrum roles and their strategic purpose

Three roles carry the framework. Each holds a different core accountability, and each matters for a different structural reason.

Role Core accountability Significance
Product Owner Product vision, value and priorities Connects external stakeholders and customer needs with internal delivery
Development Team Determines how work is delivered Uses autonomy and cross-functional capability rather than command-and-control
Scrum Master Enables scrum and removes impediments Provides process governance without becoming the team’s hierarchical manager
14 / Boundary spanning

The Product Owner

The Product Owner acts as an interpreter between the organisation and delivery team. The role is to:

  • Engage management, marketing and other stakeholders
  • Maintain the product vision
  • Prioritising competing requirements
  • Manage and refine the product backlog
  • Help determine what enters upcoming sprints
❓

What should we build next to maximise value?

15 / Autonomous delivery

The Development Team

The team is:

  • Cross functional
  • Self organising
  • Collectively accountable for delivery
  • Responsible for determining how backlog items are completed

Management defines desired outcomes and priorities without micromanaging execution.

🔑

Decision authority should sit close to the knowledge required to make the decision.

16 / Process governance

The Scrum Master

The scrum master is primarily a facilitator and coach, not a traditional project manager. Their roles includes:

  • Removing impediments
  • Protecting the team’s ability to work
  • Reinforcing Agile/Scrum practices
  • Limiting disruptive external interference
  • Maintaining transparency and continuous improvement
🔑

Self management still requires governance.

17 / Bounded flexibility

Sprints

A sprint creates a temporary period of focus and stability, commonly framed in the course as around two weeks. Adaptation is not banned — it is moved to a particular moment in the cycle.

💡

Welcome change, but don’t allow continuous change to create chaos.

18 / Prioritisation

Product backlog and sprint backlog

  • Product backlog — the broader prioritised set of potential work
  • Sprint backlog — the subset selected for the current sprint
💡

Forced Prioritisation

Not everything valuable can or should be done simultaneously.

19 / Analytical takeaways

What the week is arguing

  • Agile emerged because complex projects contain uncertainty that cannot be designed away upfront
  • Waterfall is weakest where requirements and technology evolve significantly during delivery
  • Agile converts uncertainty into short learning cycles rather than treating change as project failure
  • Scrum combines team autonomy with enough structure to prevent chaos
  • Product Owner, Scrum Master and delivery roles distribute accountability rather than recreating traditional hierarchy
  • Sprints create stability inside an adaptive system — arguably the most important conceptual point beyond the mechanics of Scru,
  • For managers, the key challenge is deciding what should remain stable long enough for execution and what should remain flexible enough for learning
20 / The premise

Winning starts before the bid

Large project bids should be treated as calculated risks, not isolated application exercises. A strong proposal develops four elements together:

Idea + Budget + Timeline + Stakeholders

Considering them simultaneously helps expose:

  • Feasibility gaps
  • Unrealistic assumptions
  • Missing stakeholders
  • Funding shortfalls
  • Delivery risks
💡

A compelling bid must be both ambitious and practically deliverable.

21 / The four elements

Integrated project planning

Project components should not be designed independently.

Element Key question
Idea What are we trying to achieve?
Budget What resources will delivery require?
Timeline When and in what sequence can it realistically happen?
Stakeholders Who must support, influence or help deliver it?
22 / Sequencing

Stakeholder engagement before approval

Stakeholder engagement should begin before funding or formal approval is secured. This matters because:

  • Trust cannot be created instantly
  • Stakeholders may reshape the proposed solution
  • Consultation can uncover risks before commitments are made
  • Genuine involvement creates stronger project legitimacy
23 / Cultural engagement

Cultural engagement and protocol

When projects involve First Nations or other culturally significant communities, engagement must go beyond ordinary consultation. Key principles include:

  • Establishing relationships early
  • Respecting appropriate cultural protocols
  • Bring cultural expertise into decision-making
  • Maintaining ongoing dialogue rather than one off consultation
  • Prioritise shared understanding and process, rather than forcing predetermined outcomes
24 / Uncertainty

Transparency under uncertainty

Large bids require specificity even when important details remain uncertain. Rather than pretending all answers are known:

  • Explain how the project will be developed
  • Identify what still requires consultation
  • Seek feedback
  • Translate that feedback into resources, scope and implementation decisions.

Some risks remain genuinely unpredictable, such as major external cost shocks. The objective is therefore not to eliminate uncertainty, but to distinguish between two kinds of it.

25 / Oversight

Governance of large projects

Typical governance in large projects typically may involve:

  • Elected representatives
  • Senior executives
  • CEO or executive sponsors
  • Project control groups
  • Formal reporting and oversight

Importantly, project circumstances may change while governance obligations remain fixed. Teams may still be expected to provide updates and meet decision milestones even when scope, cost or implementation details evolve.

26 / The sponsor

The importance of sponsor buy-in

Strong sponsors can:

  • Champion the project internally
  • Influence resource allocation
  • Resolve organisational barriers
  • Strengthen legitimacy
  • Maintain momentum through uncertainty
27 / Influence

“Soft power” in winning projects

Project approval is partly a formal process and partly a relationship-building process. Effective soft power includes:

  • Building relationships well before submission
  • Developing an evidence-based case
  • Using clear visual communication
  • Holding targeted one-on-one discussions
  • Seeking advice from influential stakeholders
  • Incorporating their perspectives into the proposal
💡

Create shared ownership before the formal decision occurs.

By the time the bid reaches the approval stage, key stakeholders should already understand, influence and ideally support the project.

28 / Takeaways

Analytical takeaways

  • A winning bid demonstrates both strategic ambition and delivery credibility
  • Planning should integrate scope, budget, timeline and stakeholder, rather than treating them separately
  • Stakeholder engagement is part of project design, not merely communication after approval
  • Strong governance provides accountability, while strong relationships create commitment
  • Sponsor buy-in is built before funding formal decisions
❓

Have we built enough evidence, feasibility and stakeholder ownership that others are willing to back the project?
