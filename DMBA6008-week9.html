01 / The core idea

Both sides of the balance sheet

Corporate restructuring asks how the asset side and financing side of the balance sheet can be changed to improve company value.

❓

Is the current combination of assets, equity and debt the best structure for creating shareholder value?

Balance sheet area Restructuring mechanism Core question
AssetsAsset salesAre these assets still earning sufficient returns to justify ownership?
AssetsDemergersCould the business create more value as separate entities?
AssetsESG investment / CapExWhat investment is required to create sustainable shareholder, environmental and social value
EquityShare buybacksShould surplus cash be returned by repurchasing shares and reducing equity?
DebtFinancial RestructuringIs the level of debt appropriate for minimising financing costs and maximising firm value?
02 / Asset restructuring

Asset sales, demergers and ESG investment

Asset sales

An asset should not automatically remain within a company simply because the company already owns it:

  • Assets may become candidates for sale when they fail to generate the required return
  • Another owner may be able to use the asset more effectively or extract greater value from it
  • Selling converts the asset into cash, allowing capital to be redeployed elsewhere
💡

Ownership should be based on the asset’s ability to create value, not historical attachment to it

Demergers

A demerger separates part of an existing company into a distinct entity rather than selling it for cash. The separated business may have its own securities exchange listing, a separate board, separate management, and a more focused strategy and capital allocation process. The objective is to unlock value where businesses may be worth more separately than within the existing corporate structure.

MechanismWhat happens
Asset saleAsset exchanged for cash
DemergerOwnership is reorganised into separate corporate entities
03 / ESG and financial restructuring

ESG investment, buybacks and the level of debt

ESG investment

Restructuring also considers environmental, societal and governance (ESG) investment:

  • ESG CapEx may increase or reshape the company’s asset base
  • These investments are considered not only for shareholder value but also for their impact on the environment and society
  • ESG requirements may influence whether existing assets should be retained, upgraded, sold or separated
❓

Does the asset portfolio remain appropriate given the changing economic, environmental and social requirements?

Share buybacks

A share buyback (equity repurchase) reverses the usual capital-raising process. The company uses cash to buy back some of its outstanding shares, thereby reducing the amount of equity on the balance sheet.

💡

The key issue is whether returning capital through a buyback creates more value than retaining the cash or investing it elsewhere

Debt and capital structure

Financial restructuring also considers whether the company has the optimal level of debt. The objective is to ensure the financing structure supports the lowest appropriate weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and therefore maximises enterprise value.

04 / Analytical takeaways

A capital-allocation problem

  • Corporate restructuring is fundamentally a capital-allocation problem: management must continually test whether assets and financing remain in their highest-value configuration
  • Asset sales and demergers address value trapped on the asset side, while buybacks and debt restructuring address inefficiencies in the financing structure
  • A restructuring decision should compare the value of the current structure with credible alternatives, rather than assuming existing ownership or financing arrangements should continue
  • ESG adds a broader constraint to restructuring: management must consider how investment decisions affect both long-term shareholder value and environmental/social outcomes
05 / What a demerger is

One business becomes two

A demerger separates part of an existing company into a new, independent entity rather than selling it for cash. The new entity typically has its own security-exchange listing, board, management team, and operating and financing structure.

The original parent may still retain an ownership interest. Existing shareholders effectively move from owning one combined business to holding interests in two separate valued businesses.

Value after demerger > value before demerger → shareholder value created
06 / How a demerger creates value

Higher cash flows or lower risk

From an NPV perspective, a demerger creates value if separation produces:

  1. Higher expected cash flows, and/or
  2. Lower risk / cost of capital, increasing the present value of those cash flows

The restructuring itself does not automatically create value. Value must come from an improvement in the underlying cash-flow or risk characteristics of the businesses.

Source of valueMechanism
TransparencyInvestors can directly observe each business’s performance rather than relying on aggregated or segment reporting
Management focusSeparate management teams can concentrate on one business rather than managing a portfolio of unrelated operations
Removal of cross-subsidiesCosts, capital and weak performance can no longer be hidden or subsidised by stronger divisions
Clearer risk profileInvestors and lenders can assess each entity’s risks independently
Market reratingGreater transparency and clearer cash flows may improve how equity or debt is valued
Investor accessSeparation may make the remaining parent investable for investors previously restricted by the activities of another division

For example

Separating an alcohol division from a supermarket group may allow ESG-focused funds to invest in the remaining consumer-staples business where their mandates previously prevented investment in the combined group.

07 / Rerating and financing

Separation can change the cost of finance

Separation can also affect the financing side of each business:

  • Clearer assets and cash flows reduce uncertainty
  • Credit-rating agencies can assess each entity independently
  • A less risky entity may receive an improved credit rating
  • This may reduce its required return or financing cost
❓

Is the conglomerate structure hiding the true performance or risk of otherwise valuable businesses?

08 / Asset sales

Selling for cash, then redeploying it

An asset sale permanently removes an asset, division or business unit from the company in exchange for cash. Unlike a demerger:

  • The parent generally retains no ownership or control over the sold business
  • Cash is received immediately
  • Management must decide how that cash can be redeployed most productively

Possible uses include investing in higher-value opportunities, repaying debt, paying dividends, and funding growth in strategically stronger businesses.

💡

The objective is not simply to sell a weak asset, but to redeploy the capital into a use with greater NPV

Book value vs economic value

Asset restructuring should be based on future cash-flow value, not the accounting value recorded on the balance sheet. An asset can have:

Book value > PV of future cash flows

It is therefore destroying shareholder value despite appearing valuable in the accounts. This can create cross-subsidisation, where a strongly performing division masks the poor economics of another division.

09 / Asset restructuring example

$1bn of book assets, $871.9m of economic value

Both Asset A and Asset B have a book value of $500m, but their future cash flows differ.

Asset AAsset B
Book value$500m$500m
Annual cash flow for 5 years$150m$80m
Hurdle rate10%10%
PV of cash flows~$568.6m*$303.3m
Value creation+$68.6m−$196.7m

Asset A is creating value, while Asset B is not earning the required return.

Combined economic value: 568.6+303.3 = 871.9m

The business therefore has $1bn of book assets but only $871.9m of economic value.

10 / Selling and redeploying the asset

Sell above economic value, reinvest at a positive NPV

Assume Asset B can be sold for $400m. This is attractive because:

Sale Price (400m) > Asset B Economic Value (303.3m)

A buyer may rationally pay more because the asset fits its strategy better, produces greater synergies within its portfolio, or can be financed at a lower cost.

The $400m is then invested into Asset C, which produces annual cash flows of $110m and has a present value of approximately $417m.

NPVc = 417 - 400 = +17m New combined value: 568.6 + 417 = 985.6m Value increase from restructuring: 985.6-871.9 = 113.7m
💡

The value uplift comes from both selling Asset B for more than its standalone economic value and reallocating that capital into a positive-NPV asset

11 / Analytical takeaways

Separation and sale both need a cash-flow case

  • Demergers create value only when separation improves cash flows or reduces the required return; simply creating two listed entities is not enough
  • Transparency matters because conglomerate structures can hide weak performance, cross-subsidies and differences in risk between divisions
  • For asset sales, book value is largely irrelevant to the decision; management should compare the asset’s economic value, achievable sale price and alternative use of the proceeds
  • Senior management should continually test whether each business remains the highest-value owner and use of its capital
  • The final decision — reinvest, repay debt or return cash to shareholders — should be based on whichever alternative produces the highest incremental NPV
12 / What a share buyback does

The reverse of issuing shares

A share buyback (equity repurchase) occurs when a company uses cash to repurchase its own shares from shareholders, either directly or through the market.

  • Cash leaves the company and goes to shareholders
  • Repurchased shares are cancelled, reducing shares outstanding and equity
  • It is effectively the reverse of issuing shares to raise capital
💡

A buyback therefore changes the financing structure of the company, but does not automatically create economic value

13 / Why companies undertake buybacks

Six motivations, six cautions

MotivationLogicKey caution
Return excess cashNo sufficiently attractive investment opportunities exist, so cash is returned to shareholdersHolding cash may destroy opportunity value if shareholders can invest it better elsewhere
Avoid increasing dividendsA buyback returns cash without creating expectations of a permanently higher dividendBuybacks are more discretionary than recurring dividends
Increase EPS/ROEFewer shares increase EPS; lower equity can increase ROEThese can be mechanical accounting improvements, not value creation
Signal undervaluationManagement may repurchase shares if it believes the market price is too lowA higher share price caused by the repurchase itself does not prove intrinsic value increased.
Offset employee-share dilutionRepurchases can counter additional shares issued through executive remunerationFocus may be on preserving EPS rather than improving economics
Improve shareholder valueSurplus cash is returned when management cannot reinvest it above the required returnValue depends on the alternative use of the cash
💡

A higher EPS or ROE does not necessarily mean shareholder value has increased.

14 / When does a buyback actually create value?

It depends on what funds it

❓

What is funding the buyback, what changes inside the business, and does the remaining company generate returns above the return investors require?

Selling an equally productive asset

Suppose a company sells an asset that is earning exactly the return shareholders require and uses the proceeds to buy back shares. The company becomes smaller — fewer assets, lower profits, fewer shares, less equity. However, nothing fundamentally improves. The remaining assets are still producing the same return relative to shareholder expectations.

💡

Result: no economic value is created. The buyback changes the size of the company, but not the quality of the underlying business.

15 / Selling an underperforming asset

The value comes from the sale

The situation changes when the buyback is funded by selling an asset that is generating an inadequate return. After the sale:

  • The weak asset is removed
  • The stronger asset remains
  • Overall asset quality improves
  • Returns to the remaining shareholders increase

In this situation, shareholder value can rise. However, the key point is that the buyback itself is not the real source of value creation. The buyback is simply the mechanism used to return the sale proceeds to shareholders.

💡

The value comes from selling an underperforming asset and concentrating capital in the better-performing part of the business

16 / Funding the buyback with debt

Better ratios, more risk

A company can also borrow money and use the cash to repurchase equity. Unlike the previous examples:

  • The company’s operating assets remain unchanged
  • Debt increases
  • Equity decreases
  • Leverage increases

This can make EPS and ROE rise, partly because there are fewer shares and less equity. But the company is now riskier. More debt can lead to:

  • Greater fixed financial obligations
  • Greater financial risk for shareholders
  • A higher levered beta
  • A higher required return on equity

Debt also provides a benefit because interest is tax-deductible. The value impact therefore depends on the trade-off between the tax benefits of debt, and higher financial risk and a higher required shareholder return.

💡

Accounting metrics can improve while economic value declines

17 / What management should assess

A capital-allocation decision

A buyback should be treated as a capital-allocation decision, not simply as a way to improve financial ratios. Management should ask:

  • Does the company actually have surplus capital?
  • Are there positive-NPV investments available instead?
  • Is the buyback funded from excess cash, an asset sale or additional borrowing?
  • If an asset is sold, was it underperforming?
  • If debt is raised, how much does financial risk increase?
  • Does the resulting business earn more than its post-restructuring cost of capital?
  • Would reinvestment, debt repayment or dividends create more value?
💡

The correct choice is the use of capital that generates the highest economic value, not necessarily the highest EPS

18 / Valuation mechanics

Required return, share price and the value test

Required return on equity

Re = RF + BEta x Market Risk Premium
  • Re = required return on equity
  • Rf = risk-free rate
  • Beta = equity risk
  • Market risk premium = additional return investors require for market risk

Higher leverage can increase beta and therefore increase the return shareholders require.

Simplified share valuation

Share price = EPS / (Re-g)
  • EPS = earnings per share
  • Re = required return on equity
  • g = long-term growth rate

This explains why higher EPS does not guarantee a higher share price. If the required return rises enough, the valuation can still fail.

Core valuation-creation test

Return earned > Required return
💡

If the company earns only the required return, it is not creating additional value

19 / Analytical takeaways

Buybacks do not inherently create value

  • Share buybacks do not inherently create value. Their effect depends on what funds the transaction and what the company looks like afterwards
  • An increase in EPS or ROE can be purely mechanical and should not be treated as proof of economic improvement
  • Selling an underperforming asset can create value because it improves the quality of the remaining asset portfolio; the buyback is secondary
  • Debt-funded buybacks create a trade-off between the tax benefits of debt and greater financial risk
❓

Does the restructuring cause the business to generate returns above its new required return, relative to alternative uses of the capital?

20 / Capital structure as a restructuring decision

Seeking the lowest sustainable WACC

From a restructuring perspective, management should periodically reassess the mix of debt and equity used to finance the business. The objective is to find the capital structure that produces the lowest sustainable WACC, because:

Lower WACC → lower financing cost → higher firm value

This makes capital structure an ongoing value-management decision rather than a one-off financing choice.

21 / Why adding debt can initially create value

Cheaper finance and a tax deduction

Introducing some debt can reduce WACC because debt may be a cheaper source of finance and interest is tax deductible. As leverage increases:

  • Equity represents a smaller proportion of total financing
  • The tax benefit of debt can reduce the overall financing cost
  • WACC may initially decline

This creates an incentive to introduce debt into an otherwise all-equity business.

22 / Why too much debt destroys the benefit

Shareholders and lenders both demand more

The benefit does not continue indefinitely. As debt increases:

  • Shareholders face greater financial risk
  • Levered beta rises
  • Shareholders demand a higher return
  • Lenders also begin to perceive greater default risk
  • The company’s credit rating may deteriorate
  • Lenders require a larger credit spread / credit risk premium

Eventually, the additional cost and risk of debt outweigh its financing and tax benefits.

23 / Credit ratings and the cost of debt

Debt gets dearer as reliance on it grows

The cost of debt is not fixed as leverage changes. As the company becomes more leveraged, lenders may downgrade its creditworthiness. For example:

  • Low leverage may support a high credit rating and a small credit spread
  • Increasing leverage can lead to progressively lower ratings
  • High leverage can result in a substantial premium being added to the company’s borrowing rate
💡

Credit spread = additional return lenders require for taking company-specific credit risk

24 / Financial distress costs

Costs beyond the interest rate

The WACC calculation captures higher borrowing costs and higher shareholder risk, but excessive leverage can create broader financial distress costs. These may include:

  • Customers becoming reluctant to commit to the company
  • Key employees leaving
  • Suppliers tightening or withdrawing credit
  • Reduced strategic flexibility

These effects can damage operating cash flows before the company actually defaults. This means the true cost of excessive debt may be greater than the observable interest rate alone suggests.

25 / Management implication

Raise debt, or reduce it

Boards and senior management should periodically reassess whether the existing capital structure is still appropriate.

❓

What combination of debt and equity minimises the company’s sustainable cost of financing while keeping financial risk acceptable?

A restructuring may therefore involve either:

  • Increasing debt when the business is under-leveraged, or
  • Reducing debt when leverage has moved beyond the value-maximising range

The optimal capital structure is where the marginal benefits of additional debt are balanced by the additional risk and financing cost it creates.

WACC

WACC = Equity weight x Cost of equity + Debt weight x After-tax cost of debt

The debt component is adjusted for the tax deductibility of interest. As leverage changes, both the cost of equity and cost of debt can change, so WACC must be reassessed rather than assuming financing costs remain constant.

26 / Analytical takeaways

An ongoing restructuring decision

  • Capital structure should be treated as an ongoing restructuring decision, because the value-maximising debt/equity mix can change over time
  • Debt can initially lower WACC, but increasing leverage eventually raises both shareholder risk and lender risk
  • Credit ratings provide an important transmission mechanism: greater leverage can cause wider credit spreads and more expensive debt
  • Excessive leverage has costs beyond interest, including potential damage to customers, employees, suppliers and strategic flexibility
💡

The objective is not maximum debt or minimum tax, but the lowest sustainable WACC consistent with an acceptable level of financial risk

27 / ESG as business as usual

Part of normal business management

ESG — environmental, social and governance — should increasingly be treated as part of normal business management rather than as a specialist activity. Management and boards need to consider:

  • Environmental: emissions, water use, raw materials and exposure to environmental change
  • Social: employees, human rights, modern slavery and supply-chain practices
  • Governance: board structure, remuneration, ethics and how the organisation is managed
❓

Does an ESG initiative increase future cash flows or reduce business risk?

💡

ESG therefore fits within the same valuation framework as any other strategic investment.

28 / The financial case for ESG

Four channels to value

ESG can affect value through four broad channels:

Grow and preserve revenue

ESG investment can protect existing revenues or create new ones through lower-emission products and services, adapting products to changing consumer preferences, accessing new markets, increasing market share, and potentially supporting premium pricing.

The important point is that these benefits normally require investment first. ESG-related growth does not occur simply because a company adopts an ESG objective.

💡

CapEx today may protect or increase future operating cash flows

29 / Improve operating efficiency

Lower operating and financing costs

ESG initiatives can also reduce costs through:

  • Alternative energy sources
  • Recycling
  • Lower water consumption
  • More efficient production and distribution
  • Potentially lower borrowing costs

For example, reducing exposure to environmental risks may make the company cheaper to insure, while achieving certain ESG targets may support access to cheaper debt.

💡

Lower operating and financing costs increase the cash flows available to investors

30 / Intangible value

Hard to measure is not the same as worthless

Some ESG benefits are strategically important but harder to quantify. These include:

  • Stronger brand value
  • Greater customer loyalty
  • Improved ability to attract and retain talent
  • Positioning for emerging markets and technologies
  • Alignment with future societal expectations

These effects may be longer-term and less certain than direct cost savings, but can still protect future revenues and competitive advantage.

💡

Difficulty measuring an ESG benefit does not mean the benefit has no economic value

31 / ESG and business resilience

Higher cash flows and lower risk

ESG investment can reduce exposure to risks that could damage future cash flows. Examples include:

  • Improving supply-chain reliability
  • Reducing exposure to volatile or scarce raw materials
  • Developing substitutes for resource-intensive inputs
  • Reducing stranded asset risk
  • Maintaining access to investors and capital markets

A stranded asset is an asset whose economic usefulness or value deteriorates because of changes such as environmental regulation, climate exposure or technological transition. Greater resilience may also reduce perceived investor risk.

Lower exposure to market and environmental risk → potentially lower beta / required return → lower cost of capital → higher valuation
32 / ESG CapEx through the Three Horizons

Horizon 1 — protect and improve the current business

The type of financial justification used for ESG investment should depend on how certain and how distant the expected benefits are. Horizon 1 investments are relatively predictable and can usually be assessed using conventional ROI or discounted cash flow methods.

Horizon 1 CapExWhat it isExamples
Compliance-drivenInvestment required to meet existing or expected regulation. It may not be discretionary, but it protects the company’s ability to continue operatingEmission controls; water-treatment upgrades; waste-disposal systems
Efficiency-enhancingInvestment that reduces operating costs while also improving environmental outcomesEnergy-efficient machinery; smart-building upgrades; logistics optimisation
💡

Relatively measurable cost savings and risk reduction make traditional investment appraisal appropriate

33 / Horizon 2

Build emerging capabilities and resilience

Horizon 2 combines expected financial returns with a stronger strategic rationale because outcomes are less certain.

Horizon 2 CapExWhat it isExamples
Growth-oriented ESGInvestment designed to create capabilities for emerging ESG-related markets. It may offer substantial future returns but involves uncertainty over market adoption and timingRenewable-energy capacity; electric-vehicle infrastructure; new products or packaging with lower environmental impact
Risk-mitigationInvestment designed to protect future cash flows and long-term viabilityFlood-proofing facilities; ethical supply-chain systems; modern-slavery monitoring; replacing vulnerable raw-material inputs
💡

Investment is justified partly through expected returns and partly through avoiding future downside risk

34 / Horizon 3

Future-proof the business

Horizon 3 ESG investments have the greatest uncertainty and are better viewed through an innovation and strategic-option lens than through short-term ROI alone. These are discretionary investments intended to position the company for future technologies, markets or societal expectations. Examples:

  • Corporate venture investments in ESG technologies
  • Circular-economy business models
  • Biodiversity initiatives
  • Emerging technologies from startups and research institutions

These investments have highly uncertain financial outcomes today, but investors may still expect management to invest in them to future-proof the company and its industry position.

35 / Choosing the right financial lens

One hurdle does not fit every horizon

The further an ESG investment moves from Horizon 1 to Horizon 3, the less useful a purely short-term financial test becomes.

🚨

The mistake would be to apply the same investment hurdle and degree of certainty to every ESG initiative

36 / Analytical takeaways

The same finance framework, a broader case

  • ESG does not require a completely different finance framework: value still comes from increasing cash flows and/or reducing risk
  • ESG can affect revenue, operating costs, financing costs, resilience and intangible assets such as brand and talent
  • The strongest business case is often broader than immediate ROI because ESG investment can protect future cash flows and prevent stranded assets
  • ESG CapEx should be evaluated differently across the Three Horizons: certainty declines as the investment moves from operational improvement toward innovation
💡

Management should ultimately ask whether ESG investment strengthens the company’s long-term cash-generating ability, resilience and cost of capital
