BUSINESS
EVALUATION

What a business is worth to the people who own it. Week 5 follows the cash from the assets through reinvestment and the lenders’ prior claim to the residual that belongs to equity, prices that residual first with a single growing perpetuity and then with a year-by-year discounted cash flow closed off by a terminal value, and finishes with the dashboard of historical ratios that turns a revenue forecast into future statements, cash flows and an equity value.

How to master this week

Four topics that build on one another: the cash-flow pathway defines what is being valued, the Gordon Growth Model prices it in one line, the DCF stretches that into a year-by-year forecast, and the driver dashboard is where the forecast actually comes from. 01 / The core idea

Valuation focuses on future distributable cash

Business valuation ultimately requires estimating the future cash flows available to investors and discounting them to present value. The complication is that cash generated by operating assets is not automatically available to shareholders. Three things sit between the two.

  1. Reinvest The business must reinvest to maintain or grow its asset base.
  2. Debt holders have a prior claim.
  3. Residual Only the residual belongs to equity holders.
❓

How much survives reinvestment and prior claims?

02 / From operating cash to free cash flow

Reinvestment comes before distribution

Cash generated by assets must partly be retained to support future operations. The notes name two forms of reinvestment and the reason each one needs cash.

Reinvestment Why cash is required
Working capital Inventory, receivables and other short-term operating requirements
Capital expenditure (CapEx) Replace depreciating assets or acquire assets needed for growth

Free Cash Flow to the Firm’s Investors (FCFF)

FCFF = Cash profit generated by assets - Working capital investment - CapEx

FCFF represents the cash available after operating reinvestment but before satisfying investors’ claims. Both debt and equity investors have claims on this cash, but debt holders rank first.

03 / From FCFF to cash available to owners

Debt holders have the first claim

Before shareholders receive cash, the firm must satisfy its debt obligations. Two payments come out of FCFF ahead of anything for equity.

  • Interest payments — return paid to lenders
  • Debt repayments — repayment of borrowed principal

Free cash flow to equity holders

Free cash flow available to equity holders = FCFF - Interest - Debt repayments Free Cash Flow to Equity ~= Dividends
04 / The valuation implication

Profit generation is not enough

A company can generate substantial operating cash but still produce relatively little cash for its owners. The notes give three conditions under which that happens.

  • Working-capital requirements are high
  • Maintaining or growing the asset base requires substantial CapEx
  • Debt servicing absorbs significant cash
💡

Valuation requires forecasting the entire cash-flow pathway, not simply revenue or accounting profit.

05 / Forecasting equity cash flow

What a realistic valuation has to forecast

A realistic valuation therefore requires forecasts of the following.

  1. Operating performance and cash generation
  2. Future working-capital requirements
  3. CapEx required for maintenance and growth
  4. Interest obligations
  5. Debt repayments
💡

The resulting residual cash flow is what ultimately supports the value of equity.

06 / Analytical takeaways

Growth, debt and assumptions

The notes close with three observations about what the pathway means for anyone valuing a business.

  • Growth can consume cash before it creates value. Higher revenue may require more working capital and CapEx, reducing cash currently available to owners.
  • Debt creates a hierarchy of claims: lenders are serviced before shareholders, making financing obligations directly relevant to residual equity cash flow.
  • When valuing a business, management assumptions about reinvestment needs and debt obligations can be just as consequential as assumptions about future profitability.
💡

The valuation model needs to estimate the future cash available for distribution to equity holders, because this is the cash flow attributable to the owners.

07 / The core idea

A growing perpetuity priced today

The Gordon Growth Model (GGM) provides a quick estimate of equity value when dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate indefinitely. It converts the stream of future dividends available to shareholders into a present share price.

P₀ = D₁ / (Rₑ − g)
Symbol Meaning
P₀ Share price today
D₁ Expected dividend per share next period
Rₑ Shareholders’ required return / equity hurdle rate
g Expected perpetual dividend growth rate
💡

Valuation uses the next-period dividend D₁, not today’s dividend, because present value is based on future cash flows.

08 / Building the valuation

Three inputs, in order

The notes build the valuation in three moves: get from the company’s total dividends to a per-share figure, roll that figure forward one period, then apply the return shareholders require.

Convert total dividends into dividend per share

DPS₀ = Total dividends / Shares outstanding

Estimate next year’s dividend

D₁ = D₀(1 + g)

Apply the shareholder hurdle rate

Rₑ represents the return shareholders require for bearing the company’s equity risk.

💡

Higher perceived risk → higher required return → lower valuation.

09 / The drivers

What drives the valuation?

Each of the three inputs pushes the estimated value in a known direction.

Variable Increase causes Why
D₁ Higher value More cash available to shareholders
g Higher value Future dividends compound faster
Rₑ Lower value Investors discount risky future cash flows more heavily
💡

The denominator Rₑ − g makes valuation particularly sensitive to assumptions about growth and required return.

10 / Managerial interpretation

Value depends on cash, growth and risk

The GGM links three fundamental drivers of equity value.

Value ← Cash distribution + Growth - Risk

Management therefore creates shareholder value by:

  • Increasing sustainable cash available to owners
  • Creating credible long-term growth
  • Managing risk sufficiently to reduce investors’ required return
11 / The limits

A simple model, not a detailed forecast

GGM requires only three estimates but assumes that dividends continue growing at a constant rate into perpetuity. Its usefulness is therefore as a quick valuation approximation rather than a detailed representation of every future business cash flow.

💡

The GGM can provide a useful valuation sense-check, but its result is only as credible as the assumptions underlying future cash flow, growth and risk.

12 / Analytical takeaways

What to carry out of the model

  • Share price is the present value of future shareholder cash flows, not simply a multiple of current profit.
  • Growth only creates a higher valuation because it increases the future cash available to shareholders; the market simultaneously prices the risk required to generate that growth.
  • Small changes in Rₑ or g can materially change estimated value because both sit in the denominator — these assumptions deserve more scrutiny than arithmetic.
❓

Are our assumptions about sustainable dividend growth and shareholder-required return economically defensible?

13 / From GGM to a DCF

Why forecast year by year

The Gordon Growth Model assumes cash flows grow at a constant rate forever. A more realistic valuation explicitly forecasts free cash flow to equity (FCFE) year by year for the period in which management can make reasonable estimates. After that explicit forecast period, the remaining value is captured through a terminal value.

Equity Value = PV (Explicit FCFE) + PV (Terminal Value)

The valuation therefore separates into two periods:

  1. Forecast period detailed annual cash-flow estimates
  2. Continuing period simplified long-term growth assumption
14 / Present value of explicit FCFE

Discount each year’s cash flow separately

If shareholders require a return of Rₑ, each year’s FCFE is discounted on its own and the results are summed:

PV(FCFE) = Σ from t=1 to n of FCFEₜ / (1 + Rₑ)ᵗ
💡

Later cash flows contribute less to today’s value because they are further away and therefore discounted for longer.

15 / Terminal value

Why terminal value is required

The business does not cease to exist after Year 5, yet detailed forecasting becomes increasingly unreliable as uncertainty around technology, competition and markets increases. Rather than forecast every future year individually, the valuation assumes a stable perpetual growth rate beyond the explicit forecast period.

Terminal value using the Gordon Growth Model

At the end of Year 5:

TV₅ = FCFE₆ / (Rₑ − g)

where:

FCFE₆ = FCFE₅(1 + g)

Worked example

Using FCFE₅ = $90m, g = 3% and Rₑ = 15%:

FCFE₆ = 90(1.03) = $92.7m TV₅ = 92.7 / (0.15 − 0.03) = $772.5m

Choosing long-term growth

The terminal growth assumption should be conservative.

💡

A company cannot sustainably grow materially faster than the overall economy forever; otherwise it would become implausibly large relative to the company.

16 / Discounting terminal value

Bringing a Year 5 value back to today

A terminal value of $772.5m is a Year 5 value, not today’s value. It has to be discounted back over the five years of the explicit forecast period.

PV(TV) = TV₅ / (1 + Rₑ)⁵ PV(TV) = 772.5 / 1.15⁵ = $384.1m
⛔

Common mistake: calculating terminal value correctly but forgetting to discount it back from the end of the forecast period.

17 / Equity value and share price

Adding the two present values

Total equity value is the present value of the explicit FCFE plus the present value of the terminal value, as the equity-value formula states. It represents the present value of all expected future FCFE into perpetuity.

Converting equity value into share price

Assuming 100 million shares outstanding and an equity value of $633.8m:

Share Price = Equity Value / Shares Outstanding Share Price = 633.8m / 100m = $6.34
18 / DCF valuation structure

The whole model in one line

Putting the pieces together, the price per share is the discounted explicit cash flows plus the discounted terminal value, divided by the shares on issue:

P₀ = [ Σ from t=1 to n of FCFEₜ / (1 + Rₑ)ᵗ + TVₙ / (1 + Rₑ)ⁿ ] / Shares Outstanding

with:

TVₙ = FCFEₙ₊₁ / (Rₑ − g)

The valuation therefore depends on three major sets of assumptions:

Driver Effect on valuation
Higher forecast FCFE Increase in value
Higher terminal growth g Increase in value
Higher required return Rₑ Decrease in value
19 / Analytical takeaways

What to carry out of the DCF

  • A DCF is more realistic than a simple perpetual-growth model because it allows near-term cash flows to behave differently before settling into long-run growth.
  • The explicit forecast horizon should reflect how far management can credibly forecast: uncertainty rises rapidly in businesses exposed to technological disruption and competitive change.
  • Terminal value can represent a large proportion of total valuation.
  • The next major valuation challenge is not the discounting mechanics but how to forecast FCFE credibly from the underlying operations of the business.
❓

Are the forecast cash flows, terminal growth rate and required return economically defensible rather than merely mathematically convenient?

20 / The starting point

The financial statements provide the starting point

A detailed valuation begins with the company’s latest balance sheet and profit and loss statement. The objective is not simply to analyse historical performance. Historical relationships are converted into a dashboard of financial drivers that can be projected forward to construct the future:

  • Revenue and operating profit
  • Assets and working capital
  • Debt and interest
  • Retained earnings and equity
  • Free cash flow available to equity holders
💡

Ratios such as asset utilisation and working capital now become forecasting assumptions inside the valuation model, rather than merely performance measures.

21 / The dashboard

Historical financial driver dashboard

Nine relationships are read off the latest statements. Each has a lecture calculation, a current level and a role in the forecast — the ratios become the mechanics connecting forecast revenue to future financial statements and cash flows.

22 / Profitability drivers

How the operating cost margin reaches value

The operating cost margin determines:

EBITDA = Revenue - Operating Costs

Therefore the direction of the margin sets the direction of profitability, all else equal.

Operating cost margin Effect Direction
Decrease Increase in EBITDA and future cash generation EBITDA up
Increase Decrease in profitability and valuation, all else equal Value down
23 / Asset and reinvestment drivers

Growth has to be paid for in assets

Asset utilisation connects revenue forecasts to the non-current asset base:

Asset utilisation = Revenue / Net Non-current assets

This relationship matters because forecast revenue growth may require additional assets and therefore investment, affecting the future cash available to shareholders. Working capital ratios perform the same role for current assets and liabilities:

Revenue growth → Working Capital requirements → Cash reinvestment
24 / Financing and distribution

Financing and distribution drivers

Debt and lease liabilities are treated together for valuation purposes because lease liabilities behave similarly to debt in economic substance and generate interest expense. The financing relationships affect:

  • Future interest expense
  • Debt obligations
  • Cash remaining for equity holders
Retained Earnings = NPAT - Dividends

Retained earnings increase equity and therefore help determine the company’s future financial position.

25 / Using the dashboard

Baseline, not forecast

The latest year’s ratios provide the baseline, not necessarily the final forecast. For each driver, the analyst must decide whether to:

  • Maintain the historical relationship
  • Adjust it based on expectations about the company’s future operations

For example:

Forecast Revenue→Forecast Costs, Assets, Liabilities→Forecast Profit→Forecast Cash Flow→Equity Value
💡

The valuation depends not simply on projecting revenue growth, but on understanding what operating resources, costs and financing are required to produce that growth.

26 / Analytical takeaways

What to carry out of the topic

  • The key transition in a comprehensive valuation is from historical ratios to forward-looking assumptions; the ratios become the mechanics connecting forecast revenue to future financial statements and cash flows.
  • Revenue growth is not automatically value creation: if it requires disproportionately more operating costs, working capital or non-current assets, much of the additional cash may be absorbed by reinvestment.
  • Historical ratios are a starting point rather than truth. The analytical task is deciding which relationships are structurally persistent and which should change based on the firm’s strategy and outlook.
❓

What operational and financing assumptions must be true for the forecast cash flows — and therefore the valuation — to be credible?
