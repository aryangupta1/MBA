01 / The premise

Project management starts before execution

Project management is not only about delivering work efficiently. It also involves determining whether the proposed project is worth undertaking and whether it addresses the correct problem.

Question Focus
Are we doing the right thing? Problem relevance, user needs and strategic value
Are we doing it the right way? Execution quality, resources, time, cost and delivery

A team can deliver a project on time and within budget while still creating little value if the original problem was poorly defined.

Project development vs project management

  • Project Development — identifies the problem, explores opportunities and validates the proposed concept.
  • Project Management — coordinates the resources and activities required to deliver it.
🔑

Strong projects combine both rather than treating execution as the starting point.

02 / The failure mode

Solution fixation

Teams often jump directly from an initial problem statement to a familiar solution — a new application, an AI feature, a marketing campaign, a major system replacement. Committing to an implementation before understanding the underlying need is solution fixation.

Consequences

  • Resources are spent solving the wrong problem
  • Important stakeholder perspectives are missed
  • Assumptions become embedded in the project
  • Late changes create greater cost and disruption
  • The output may be technically successful but strategically irrelevant
🔑

Early exploration may appear slower, but it reduces expensive rework later.

03 / The method

Design thinking in projects

Design thinking provides a structured but creative way to investigate uncertain problems. Its major characteristics are:

  • User-centred — begins with user needs and experiences
  • Interdisciplinary — combines different professional perspectives
  • Experiential — learns through observation, testing and interaction
  • Iterative — ideas are repeatedly refined rather than developed once
  • Open-ended — teams delay judgement while exploring possibilities

Creativity here does not mean artistic ability. It means being able to reframe problems, question assumptions and generate alternative approaches.

Frameworks and judgement

Frameworks provide principles and structure, but they do not automatically determine the correct solution. Managers must still apply judgement, contextual knowledge, stakeholder insight and creative problem solving.

04 / The two modes

Divergent and convergent thinking

Design thinking alternates between expanding and narrowing the project's focus.

05 / The first framework

The Double Diamond

The Double Diamond divides project development into two connected question: what is the right problem to solve, and what is the right way to solve it?

Stage Thinking pattern Core purpose
Discover Diverge Investigate users, stakeholders, context and possible problems
Define Converge Select and clearly frame the most important problem
Design / Develop Diverge Generate and prototype alternative solutions
Deliver Converge Select, refine and implement the strongest solution

The model is iterative

Although the stages appear sequential, teams frequently move backwards and forwards. Testing a solution may reveal that the problem was defined incorrectly, that requirements were missed, that stakeholder priorities have changed, or that the concept is not technically or commercially viable.

Iteration is therefore a normal learning process rather than evidence that the project has failed.

06 / The extension

The Triple Diamond and technology projects

The Triple Diamond extends design thinking beyond concept selection into development, real-world validation and scaled implementation. It better represents technology projects, where a promising prototype must still become a reliable, integrated and adoptable system.

The purpose of Discovery is to avoid approving development based only on assumptions or enthusiasm. Once validated, the concept is converted into a functional solution, and continues evolving as the team learns about technical constraints and user expectations.

07 / The word itself

Agile and agility

The term Agile has two related meanings in project environments.

Meaning Explanation
Agile project management A formal approach based on incremental delivery, feedback and continuous prioritisation
Operational agility The broader ability to adapt when evidence, requirements or conditions change

Agile delivery supports the Triple Diamond because development and validation continue through repeated cycles rather than a single final release.

🔑

Agile execution does not compensate for poor problem selection. A team can efficiently build the wrong product in short iterations.

08 / Worked example

An AI customer support project

A SaaS company may initially propose an AI chatbot because competitors have introduced one. Applying the Double and Triple Diamond changes what gets built.

  1. Discover — Research reveals customers are frustrated mainly poor visibility into unresolved support cases
  2. Define — The core problem becomes lack of timely status information, not the absence of a chatbot
  3. Design — Alternatives include proactive notifications, a case-tracking portal, improved triage or an AI assistant
  4. Concept validation — Users test lightweight prototypes before major investment
  5. Development — The selected approach is build through short Agile iterations
  6. Validation — A pilot measures resolution time, customer satisfaction and support workload
  7. Rollout — The system is progressively released with monitoring and user support
🔑

The process shifts the project from “build an AI chatbot” to “improve the support experience using the most appropriate solution”.

09 / Managerial implications

The questions a project leader should ask

  • What evidence demonstrates that this is the real problem?
  • Whose needs are represented in the problem definition?
  • Have alternative explanations and solutions been explored?
  • What assumptions must be tested before major development?
  • What would invalidate the proposed concept?
  • How will feedback influence development decisions?
  • What evidence is required before rollout?
  • How will adoption and long-term value be measured?
10 / The engagement

StellarCX and $RUs

The course runs a continuing Shadow Boxing simulation in which the StellarCX consulting team advises the client, $RUs. Rather than receiving a perfectly defined project, the team must respond to incomplete information, competing priorities, organisational politics, uncertain client expectations, limited budgets and timelines, and changing project directions.

The client

$RUs is a successful Sydney-based financial services company led by Murray and his business partner. It is financially successful and well managed, supported by a strong, stable income stream, and has established credibility with its existing client base — while facing uncertainty about its future growth.

Its core challenge is to attract and engage younger clients. That is not simply a marketing campaign; StellarCX must identify a solution that responds to younger customers' needs, strengthens long-term engagement, supports future growth, can scale across the business, and may use AI where it creates genuine value.

🔑

AI is a possible enabler, not automatically the correct solution.

11 / The team

The Gold Team, and what pulls it off course

Chris Gold leads one of StellarCX's consulting teams. The team includes different disciplines, motivations and working styles — differences that can improve the project, but may also create tension.

Team member Role or perspective Potential contribution or tension
Chris Gold Project manager and product owner Values engagement and quality; must lead through ambiguity and competing priorities
Dirk Senior contributor Strong preference for practical work over meetings and organisational initiatives
Ivy Experienced project manager Brings experience coordinating people, timelines and deliverables
Andrew Machine-learning specialist Interested in career progression and securing a visible, successful AI project
Jeremy New technical team member Brings technical capability but is still learning the team and organisational context
Annie Designer Represents the design perspective and may support user-centred problem exploration

Project risks

Risk Why it matters
Technology bias The team may choose AI because it appears innovative rather than because it solves the client's problem
Personal agendas Career goals may influence recommendations and resource decisions
Premature execution Pressure to show progress may cause the team to act before defining the problem
Poor collaboration Different disciplines and work preferences may create conflict or silos
Client assumption bias The team may accept $RUs's view of the problem without validating younger customers' needs
Weak engagement A technically productive team may still perform poorly if members are disconnected or unheard
12 / Your role

Adviser, not deliverer

The student acts as an adviser to the StellarCX team, deciding:

  • What the team should do next
  • When it should accept or challenge the client's direction
  • What information is still missing
  • Which stakeholders should be consulted
  • Whether AI is appropriate
  • How competing views should be managed
  • When the project has enough evidence to proceed

The objective is not only to deliver a solution, but to make defensible project decisions under uncertainty.

13 / Why it matters

Reading the environment before designing the solution

Before designing a solution, StellarCX must understand the environment in which $RUs operates. Context analysis helps the team identify:

  • The real customer problem
  • Market opportunities and threats
  • Regulatory constraints
  • Changing customer expectations
  • Competitor capabilities
  • Whether the proposed solution fits $RUs’ strengths
🔑

Without this analysis, the team risks delivering an innovative solution that is irrelevant non-compliant or commercially unviable.

14 / The market

The Australian financial planning market

The financial planning industry is shifting from serving mainly older, wealthy clients towards supporting younger people with broader and more immediate financial needs.

Traditional market

Financial advisers have historically focused on:

  • Retirement planning
  • Investment portfolio management
  • Superannuation
  • Tax structuring
  • Inheritance or wealth preservation

These services are commonly targeted at clients who already hold significant assets.

Emerging market

Younger clients require advice across different stages of wealth creation, including:

  • Budgeting and debt management
  • HECS repayment
  • First-home purchasing
  • Career and education decisions
  • Starting a business
  • Insurance
  • Ethical investing
  • Digital assets
  • Early retirement planning

The opportunity is to build relationships before clients become wealthy, rather than waiting until they approach retirement.

15 / The segment

The younger client segment

Millennial and Gen Z clients are not simply younger versions of $RUs existing customers. Their financial circumstances, expectations and behaviours are different.

Younger client expectation Implication for $RUs
Digital convenience Advice should be accessible through modern digital channels
Personalisation Generic financial plans may feel irrelevant
Transparency Fees, incentives and recommendations must be clearly explained
Ethical alignment Clients may consider sustainability and conflicts of interest
Immediate relevance Services must address current pressures, not only retirement
Ongoing engagement Clients may expect regular, responsive interaction
Simple communication Complex financial language must be made understandable

The core customer tension

Younger Australians may recognise the value of financial advice but face a set of constraints.

16 / The competition

Competitive environment

Banks and major financial institutions, and independent boutique firms such as $RUs, bring different advantages and different limits.

Strategic position for $RUs

Its stronger position is likely to combine:

🔑

Personalised independent advice with a modern, convenient and scalable customer experience

17 / Trust

Trust and industry reputation

Trust is a central issue in financial planning because clients must disclose sensitive information and rely on advice with long-term consequences. Past industry scandals exposed:

  • Conflicts of interest
  • Inappropriate product recommendations
  • Commission-driven behaviour
  • Weak separation between advice and product sales

These issues contributed to tighter regulation and greater public scrutiny.

Younger clients and trust

Younger customers may be especially sceptical of:

  • Hidden commissions
  • Unclear fees
  • Sales-oriented recommendations
  • Adviser relationships with product providers
  • Claims of independence that are not supported by practice

For $RUs, trust must be demonstrated through:

  • Transparent pricing
  • Clear explanations
  • Visible independence
  • Ethical data use
  • Consistent communication
  • Evidence that recommendations reflect the client’s interests
18 / Regulation

Regulatory context

Financial advice operates within a highly regulated environment overseen by bodies including the Australian Securities and Investments Commission. Any new service or technology must consider:

  • Financial advice obligations
  • Privacy and customer-data protection
  • Disclose requirements
  • Conflicts of interest
  • Record-keeping
  • Cybersecurity
  • Accountability for recommendations

Implications for AI

AI may improve personalisation, accessibility or internal efficiency, but it also introduces risks.

AI opportunity Associated risk
Personalised financial education Advice may be inaccurate or unsuitable
Automated customer support Customers may mistake general information for personal advice
Client segmentation Data use may appear intrusive or unfair
Adviser productivity Staff may over-rely on automated outputs
Scalable engagement Reduced human interaction may weaken trust
🔑

AI should therefore support qualified advisers rather than operate as an uncontrolled substitute for professional judgement.

19 / Wealth over time

Superannuation and intergenerational wealth

Superannuation remains important to younger clients because decisions made early can influence long-term financial security. Its complexity arises from frequent changes involving:

  • Contribution limits
  • Taxation
  • Concessions
  • Access rules
  • Estate planning treatment

The intergenerational opportunity

As wealth transfers from older generations to their children, younger clients may need support with:

  • Inherited property
  • Superannuation death benefits
  • Trusts
  • Tax implications
  • Family financial planning
  • Preparing for future responsibilities

This creates an opportunity for $RUs to form relationships across generations. Rather than advising only the existing older client, $RUs could engage the wider family before wealth is transferred.

20 / The challenge

The strategic challenge for $RUs

$RUs wants to become relevant to clients under 40 while maintaining its established strengths. This requires more than:

  • Refreshed branding
  • Social media activity
  • A new website
  • Adding AI for promotional value

The broader transformation must integrate:

  1. Customer insight — understanding younger client’s actual needs and barriers
  2. Service innovation — developing advice suited to earlier life stages
  3. Digital transformation — improving accessibility and convenience
  4. Trust design — strengthening transparency and independence
  5. Regulatory alignment — ensuring new services remain compliant
  6. Scalability — serving more clients without removing the personalised experience
21 / The opportunity

A potential market opportunity

A promising strategic position is to help younger professionals manage both:

  • Present financial pressures — such as debt, housing and budgeting
  • Future wealth opportunities — such as superannuation, inheritance and family assets

This could allow $RUs to move from being viewed primarily as a retirement adviser to becoming a long-term financial partner across life stages. However, this remains a hypothesis that StellarCX must test through customer research.

22 / What it means for the project

Implications for the StellarCX project

StellarCX should not begin by deciding what technology to build. The discovery process should first investigate:

  • Why younger clients do or do not seek advice
  • Which financial problems they value most
  • How much they are willing to pay
  • Which channels they trust
  • What prevents them from engaging with $RUs
  • Whether existing clients can introduce younger family members
  • Where digital tools or AI would meaningfully improve the experience

The project should then evaluate possible solutions based on:

  • Customer desirability
  • Technical feasibility
  • Commercial viability
  • Regulatory acceptability
  • Strategic fit with $RUs

Key analytical takeaways

  • The financial advice market is shifting toward younger, digitally fluent and trust-sensitive clients
  • Younger clients require broader support than traditional retirement and investment advice
  • $RUs’s advantage lies in personalised and independent service, but its digital capability may limit growth
  • Trust, transparency and regulatory compliance are essential parts of the customer experience
  • Intergenerational wealth transfer provides a potential pathway to earlier client engagement
  • AI is a possible enabler, but not the starting point.
  • StellarCX must validate whether the core opportunity is marketing, service design, technology, pricing, intergenerational engagement, or a combination of these.

24 / Takeaways

What to carry out of Week 1

  • Project success begins with problem selection and definition, not scheduling.
  • Efficient execution cannot rescue an irrelevant project.
  • Divergent thinking expands understanding; convergent thinking creates direction.
  • The Double Diamond connects problem discovery with solution development.
  • The Triple Diamond extends this logic through development, validation and rollout.
  • Iteration reduces uncertainty by exposing assumptions before they become expensive.
  • The $RUs problem is initially broad and requires further discovery.
  • StellarCX must avoid treating AI as the project objective.
  • Team members' motivations and working styles will influence decisions.
  • Effective project leadership requires managing both the work and the people performing it.
  • The central managerial challenge is to ensure teams are building the right solution and building it effectively.
