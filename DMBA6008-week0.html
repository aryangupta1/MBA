Fundamentals of financial management

Three statements, one business. The balance sheet says what the firm owns and owes, the profit and loss statement says how it traded, and the cash flow statement says whether any of it turned into money. Then a fourth topic turns all three into a judgement: what the returns are, what risk sits behind them, and whether the performance can last.

01 / The equation

What a balance sheet is made of

The balance sheet is based on the accounting equation, where the left side must balance with the right side.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Each category answers a different question. Assets are resources used to generate profits; liabilities are external claims on them; equity is what is left for shareholders.

Both sides split again by time: current items expire or settle within 12 months, non-current beyond 12 months.

02 / Why start here

Why the balance sheet is the starting point

A business must first invest in assets before it can earn revenue, and different industries need different assets.

💡

Revenue is generated because assets exist

What qualifies as an asset

An asset is a resource the business controls and expects to yield future benefits. To qualify:

  • There has been an investment or cost to obtain or control it
  • It is expected to generate future economic benefits
  • Those benefits can be measured with reasonable reliability

Nearly every activity lands here

Sell assets Assets decrease / change
Make sales Cash or Accounts Receivable increase
Pay expenses Cash decreases
Incur unpaid expenses Liabilities increase
Earn profit Retained Earnings (Equity) increase
Make a loss Retained Earnings (Equity) decrease

Equity = Assets − Liabilities

Equity represents the net worth of a company — what is left for the owners.

Financial distress

Signals of financial distress include:

  • Assets become less than liabilities (negative equity)
  • Asset values have been significantly written down
  • The business may still have enough cash to pay short-term obligations, but unless performance improves it has a higher risk of defaulting on future obligations
03 / Current assets

Short-term assets and working capital

Current assets are expected to be converted into cash, sold or consumed within 12 months — also called short-term assets. They support day-to-day operations and liquidity.

💡

Non-current assets may also be called fixed assets or long-term assets

Current asset What it represents Main management concern
Cash Money available to pay short-term obligations Too little creates liquidity risk; too much may be idle
Receivables Money owed by customers Slow payment, bad debts and cash flow pressure
Inventory Goods held for resale or use within 12 months Obsolescence, excess stock and write-downs
Prepayments Goods or services paid for in advance Cash is tied up before the benefit is received

Cash

Cash pays staff, suppliers, operating expenses and unexpected costs. Excessive cash signals inefficient capital allocation, because it earns a low return compared with investment in growth.

Receivables

Receivables — accounts receivable or trade debtors — are amounts owed by customers already supplied under credit terms. Revenue may be recorded well before the cash arrives, while wages, suppliers and rent still have to be paid. Credit terms win sales and market share, at the cost of slow payment, bad debts and cash flow pressure.

Inventory

Inventory is goods held for resale — finished goods, raw materials, supplies, work in progress, and tools consumed within 12 months. Service businesses may carry work in progress for services not yet billed. It is recorded at cost, not expected selling price, and written down to net realisable value if worth less — through low demand, damage, obsolescence or price reductions.

Prepayments

Prepayments arise when a business pays before receiving the benefit — prepaid rent or insurance, an annual software subscription. As the benefit is consumed, the asset falls and an expense is recognised.

$120,000 of insurance, paid up front
At payment date
$120,000 / $0
After six months
$60,000 / $60,000
After twelve months
$0 / $120,000

Prepayment remaining / Expense recognised

A company prepaying a 12-month cloud software licence records the unused portion as a prepayment and recognises the expense gradually over the contract period.

Inventory intensity by business model
Woolworth's inventory
~17% of total assets
Qantas' inventory
~2% of total assets

Same line item, very different weight

A supermarket must have products immediately available for sale; most of an airline's assets are long-term assets such as aircraft.

The current asset trade-off

The goal is not to minimise or maximise current assets. It is to determine the optimal level that supports operations, protects liquidity, avoids unnecessary financing costs, and generates an acceptable return on capital.

💡

The core management is balancing: Operational safety against capital efficiency

Warning signs

Warning sign Possible interpretation
Cash rising significantly The business may lack profitable investment opportunities
Inventory rising faster than sales Products may be difficult to sell
Receivables rising faster than revenue Customers may be paying more slowly
High bad debt expense Credit controls may be weak
Large prepayments Cash may be unnecessarily tied up
Frequent short-term borrowing Working capital may be poorly managed
💡

Does the business hold the right type and amount of current assets for its operating model

04 / Non-current assets

The engine room, and how it wears out

Non-current assets generate benefits beyond 12 months. They are the engine room, providing the long-term operating capacity that generates revenue: land, buildings, plant and equipment, and intangibles.

Land is not depreciated, because it does not wear out, and is recorded separately from buildings, which are depreciated. Plant and equipment is depreciated as its benefits reduce through use, age or obsolescence.

Depreciation and carrying value

Depreciation allocates an asset’s cost across the periods it helps generate revenue. It hits both statements: an expense reducing profit, and a reduced carrying value on the balance sheet.

💡

Depreciation does not necessarily represent a cash payment in the current period. The cash outflow usually occurred when the asset was purchased

Accumulated depreciation is the total depreciation recognised over the life of an asset to date. The remaining balance is called the book value or carrying value.

Carrying Value = Original Asset Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
Carrying value
Original equipment cost
$100 Million
Accumulated depreciation
$40 Million

Carrying value = $60 Million

The carrying value is an accounting amount. It may not equal the asset's current market value.

Useful life, same $100 Million asset
5-year life
$20 Million p.a.
10-year life
$10 Million p.a.

Extending 5 → 10 years cuts depreciation by $10 Million

This increases reported profit, even though the asset itself has not become more productive.

Useful life is an estimate

Useful life is the period the business expects to use the asset, not its maximum physical life. Review it at least annually, and revise for usage, new technology, changed strategy or earlier replacement.

Estimate Annual depreciation Effect on reported profit Effect on carrying value
Longer useful life Lower Higher Higher
Shorter useful life Higher Lower Lower
💡

An unusually long useful-life estimate may create an illusion of stronger profitability and financial position

Capital expenditure versus routine expense

Expenditure is capitalised when it increases future benefits — extending life, adding capacity, improving performance. It then increases the asset balance and is depreciated. Routine repairs preserve existing condition and are expensed.

Expenditure type Accounting treatment Example
Capital expenditure Added to the asset balance Major server upgrade that extends useful life
Routine repair Expensed immediately Replacing a failed server component
Capacity improvement Usually capitalised Adding production machinery
Regular maintenance Expensed immediately Routine equipment servicing

Depreciating assets without replacing them signals underinvestment and heavy capital expenditure later. Stay-in-business capex maintains current operations and earnings; growth capex expands earning capacity.

Methods may differ by asset, provided they reflect how benefits are consumed. The method changes the timing of the expense, not the original cost — so ask whether it matches usage, is consistent, and matches industry practice.

💡

Are the businesses non-current assets capable of supporting future earnings, and are management's depreciation and investment policies presenting their condition fairly

05 / Intangibles

Assets without physical substance

Intangible assets are non-current assets without physical substance that are identifiable, controlled by the business and expected to provide measurable benefit. Recognition requires probable future benefits, reliably measurable cost, and identifiability — separable, or arising from contractual or legal rights.

Category Examples Source of value
Patents Exclusive rights over an invention Prevent competitors from using protected technology
Copyrights Rights over software, media or written content Enables control and commercialisation
Trademarks and brands Purchased brand names or logos Supports recognition, loyalty and pricing power
Licenses Broadcasting or operating licenses Provides legal permission to operate
Customer-related assets Purchased customer lists or contracts Supports future sales and relationships
Development assets Qualifying software or product development Expected to generate future revenue
Goodwill Arises when another business is acquired Represents benefits not separately identifiable

What the balance sheet leaves out

Some valuable resources cannot be recognised — skilled employees, market knowledge, customer loyalty, market share, internally built reputation — because the business may not control them, they may not be separable or reliably measurable, or they were built internally rather than acquired.

How intangibles arise

  1. Separate acquisition: buying a software license, patent, trademark or customer list. The purchasing price normally provides a measurable basis for recognition.
  2. Business acquisition: customer contracts, brand names, publishing rights, proprietary technology and licenses identified when one company acquires another. Goodwill may also arise, but is treated as a separate category.
  3. Internal development: new software, a product under development, a pharmaceutical product or a technology platform. Recognition depends on whether the expenditure is expected to generate identifiable future economic benefits.

Research versus development

Research is expensed while the commercial outcome is uncertain. Development may be capitalised once it is sufficiently connected to probable future benefits.

Amortisation, indefinite lives and impairment

Amortisation is depreciation applied to non-physical assets. Finite-life intangibles are amortised over the remaining legal protection, the contract term, or the expected period of usefulness.

Indefinite-life intangibles — some acquired brands, airport landing slots, certain licenses — are not amortised, but must be impairment-tested at least annually.

Impairment occurs when carrying value exceeds recoverable economic value — through technological change, weak performance, lost customers, regulation, competition or failed projects. Carrying value falls, an expense is recognised, and profit and equity decline.

Warning signs

Warning sign Possible interpretation
Rapid growth in capitalised development costs Expenses may be shifted onto the balance sheet
Long amortisation periods Profit may be overstated in the short term
Repeated project failures without impairment Asset values may be overstated
Large intangible balance after acquisitions Future earnings depend heavily on acquired assets
No impairment despite declining performance Management assumptions may be overly optimistic

Comparability suffers: companies look asset-light because internally generated capabilities are excluded from the balance sheet entirely.

💡

Do the recognised intangible assets genuinely represent future economic benefits, and are amortisation and impairment policies presenting their value fairly?

06 / Goodwill

The premium paid for a business

Goodwill is an unidentifiable intangible asset created when one business acquires another — the amount paid above the fair value of the acquired identifiable net assets.

Goodwill = Purchase Price − Fair value of identifiable net assets

A buyer pays the premium for expected growth, customers, skilled employees, market position, synergies or technology — benefits that cannot be recognised separately.

A software company acquires a SaaS platform
Purchase price
$120 Million
Fair value of identifiable net assets
$100 Million

Goodwill recognised = $20 million

The additional $20 million may reflect combining the companies' platforms, accessing the acquired customer base, gaining specialist engineering talent and reducing duplicated operating costs.

Goodwill versus identifiable intangibles
Identifiable
can be sold or licensed
Goodwill
cannot be sold separately

Goodwill arises only from acquiring a business

Identifiable intangibles such as patents, software and trademarks may arise without acquiring a whole business; goodwill represents broader acquisition benefits.

💡

Goodwill is recorded on the buyer's balance sheet, not the acquired company's original balance sheet

Goodwill has an indefinite life, so it is not amortised and stays on the balance sheet unless impaired — but must be impairment-tested at least annually, when benefits fail to appear, synergies are missed, or key customers and staff leave.

A large goodwill balance means growth by acquisition at substantial premiums — not automatically bad, but its value rests on management delivering. Goodwill is management’s expectation about an acquisition’s future value.

💡

Did the business create enough value from the acquisition to justify the premium it paid?

07 / Asset quality

Considerations with assets

The line between an asset and an expense is the most consequential judgement on the balance sheet. An asset spans more than one period and has its cost recognised over time; an expense is consumed now and reduces profit immediately.

Management may prefer the asset treatment, since expenses cut profit now while assets keep it higher. The test is whether future benefits can be identified and measured reliably.

Expenditure Typical treatment Reason
Staff training Expense Future benefits are uncertain and difficult to measure
Research Expense Commercial success is not yet sufficiently certain
Qualifying software development Asset Future economic benefits may be identifiable and measurable
Routine maintenance Expense Maintains rather than improves future benefits
💡

This reflects conservatism in financial reporting: uncertain asset values are not recorded simply because management believes they are valuable

Control, not legal ownership

Legal ownership is not required — what matters is substance over legal form. An asset may be recognised when the business controls its use, takes the benefits, bears the risks, and can exclude others.

Revaluation

A non-current asset may be revalued upward: the asset and equity both rise through a revaluation reserve. A later fall reduces the reserve first, and any excess becomes an expense.

Where assets get overstated

Warning sign Possible interpretation
Rapid growth in capitalised costs Expenses may be shifted onto the balance sheet
Weak cash flow despite high reported profit Asset recognition may be supporting profit
Assets with uncertain future benefits Carrying values may be overstated
Frequent upward revaluations Management may be strengthening the balance sheet through judgement
Delayed write-downs Asset values may no longer be recoverable
❓

Do the recorded assets genuinely represent controlled future economic benefits, or are assumptions and accounting choices making the business appear stronger than it is?

08 / Liabilities

Obligations and how they are financed

Liabilities are present obligations from past transactions requiring a future transfer of resources. Some are known precisely; others need estimates of amount or timing.

Classification Settlement period Examples
Current liabilities Within 12 months Payables, accrued expenses, short-term borrowings
Non-current liabilities Beyond 12 months Long-term loans, lease obligations, long-term provisions

Accounts payable are owed to suppliers for goods already received, delaying payment often interest-free. Large payables signal either strong bargaining power or trouble paying.

Borrowings — overdrafts, credit lines, short-term loans, the current portion of long-term debt, long-term loans and leases — carry interest and repayment.

Revenue received in advance — unearned or deferred revenue, a contract liability — arises when a customer pays before delivery: the cash is banked now, the revenue recognised as the service is delivered.

Accrued expenses are costs incurred but unpaid — wages, interest, utilities, contractors — under accrual accounting, which records expenses when incurred rather than when paid. Employee entitlements such as leave and bonuses need estimation.

Provisions are obligations where the amount or timing is uncertain — warranties, redundancies, restructuring costs, litigation, refunds, environmental obligations — based on management's best estimate of the expected future cost.

Estimating a warranty provision
Annual sales
$100 million
Historical claim rate
3% of sales

Warranty provision = $100 million × 3% = $3 million

The company records a $3 million liability even though the exact claims and payment dates are unknown.

Lower liabilities

Unlike assets, which management may prefer to overstate, liabilities may be omitted or understated.

Provision estimates rest on assumptions: claim history, legal outcomes, failure rates, turnover, restructuring plans. Liabilities should match the business model.

Liability feature Possible interpretation
High supplier payables Strong bargaining power or payment difficulty
High revenue in advance Strong customer demand but future service obligations
High borrowings Greater financial risk and interest costs
Large provisions Significant expected future costs
Low provisions despite known risks Liabilities may be understated
❓

Are the liabilities complete, reasonably estimated and manageable given the company's cash flow and business model?

09 / Equity

The owners' residual claim

Equity represents the owners' residual claim on a business after liabilities are deducted from assets. It reflects the business's net worth from an accounting perspective.

Equity = Assets − Liabilities
Category Meaning
Issued capital Funds contributed by owners or shareholders
Retained earnings Accumulated profits kept in the business after dividends
Reserves Equity movements not recognised through the income statement

Issued capital is the owners’ initial investment plus later contributions — owner’s equity in a small business, issued capital or share capital in a company.

Retained earnings are accumulated after-tax profits that have not been distributed to owners.

Closing retained earnings = Opening retained earnings + Profit − Dividends

Profit raises retained earnings; losses and dividends reduce them. Retention funds growth without new debt or equity, so management balances retention against dividends — a high-growth technology company retains most profits, a mature one distributes more.

Reserves

Reserves record equity changes that bypass the income statement — most often revaluation and foreign currency translation reserves.

Data centre revalued upward
Recorded at
$20 million
Assessed value
$25 million

Asset +$5 million · Revaluation reserve +$5 million · Profit: no immediate effect

The gain remains unrealised until the asset is sold.

The same asset falls to $18 million
Revaluation reserve removed
$5 million
Additional expense recognised
$2 million

Reserve absorbs the fall first, then profit

Because the original value was $20 million and it went up to $25 million, moving back down to $20 million uses the $5 million revaluation, and anything beyond that is an additional expense — which reduces profit and retained earnings.

A foreign currency translation reserve arises when a subsidiary’s statements are converted into the parent’s currency, shifting equity though no currency was sold.

❓

Is equity growing because the business is generating and retaining profits, or because of owner contributions and accounting revaluations?

10 / Summing up

Reading the balance sheet as a system

Observation Possible interpretation
High-quality assets and manageable liabilities Strong financial position
Large assets funded mainly by debt Greater financial risk
Rising receivables and weak cash Revenue may not be converting into cash
Large deferred revenue and strong cash Customer-funded operations with future service obligations
Growing equity through retained earnings Profits are being retained in the business
Growing equity mainly through new capital Growth depends on additional shareholder funding

The questions to carry forward

  • Financial analysis starts with the balance sheet because it explains what the business owns, owes, and how its assets are financed.
  • Assets are the foundation of revenue generation, and almost every transaction ultimately affects the balance sheet.
  • Does the asset structure fit the business model, and are the assets capable of generating future benefits?
  • Are liabilities complete, manageable and favourably structured, and how dependent is the business on borrowing?
  • Is equity growing through retained profits, owner contributions or revaluations?
  • Can the business meet its short and long term obligations?
  • A strong balance sheet is not simply one with more assets or fewer liabilities.
❓

Do the quality of the assets, the structure of the liabilities and the source of equity support sustainable future performance

01 / Purpose

What the P&L actually reports

The profit and loss statement (P&L) reports trading performance over a specific period — a month, quarter or year. The balance sheet shows position at a date; the P&L measures performance over time.

❓

Did the organisation generate enough revenue to cover the expenses incurred in earning that revenue?

Revenue is economic benefit from business activity — sale of goods, services, interest, dividends, rent, royalties. Expenses are the costs of producing, delivering and operating: salaries, advertising, rent, electricity, interest, insurance, repairs, depreciation and cost of goods sold.

The P&L and the balance sheet are one system

Revenue does not mean cash has been received: a cash sale increases cash, while a sale made on invoice creates accounts receivable.

Profit and loss effect Balance sheet effect
Cash revenue is earned Cash increases
Credit revenue is earned Accounts receivable increases
Inventory is sold Inventory decreases
An unpaid expense is recognised A liability increases
Profit is generated Retained earnings and equity increase
Technology retail example
Sales revenue
$1300
Cost of goods sold
($1000)

Profit = $300

A laptop bought for $1000 cash, held as inventory, then sold. Inventory falls $1000, cash or receivables rise $1300, and the $300 profit lifts equity through retained earnings.

Revenue may be recorded before customers pay, and depreciation with no cash at all — so a profitable organisation may still have cash flow difficulties.

Profit = Revenue − Expenses
02 / Timing

Accrual accounting and the timing problem

Profit depends not only on which revenues and expenses are recorded but when. Cash accounting records transactions when cash moves; accrual accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when resources are consumed or obligations incurred.

Cash-basis profit = Cash received from revenue − Cash paid for expenses Accrual profit = Revenue earned − Expenses incurred

Cash accounting distorts performance by recording revenue and its costs in different periods: delaying a supplier payment lifts cash-basis profit though the resource was already consumed.

Issue Cash accounting Accrual accounting
Revenue recognised When cash is received When revenue is earned
Expense recognised When cash is paid When the resource is used or cost incurred
Main focus Cash movements Economic activity
Credit sales Recorded when customer pays Recorded when the sale or service occurs
Unpaid expenses Recorded when paid Recorded when incurred
Measuring performance Misleading across periods Better reflects the period

The matching principle

Accrual accounting supports the matching principle: expenses belong in the same period as the revenue they helped generate. That stops costs being shifted between periods, and stops a business looking more profitable simply for paying its bills late.

03 / Expenses

Assumptions behind expenses

Some expenses come from clear external transactions, such as a wage payment. Others need management to estimate how much was consumed, which period benefited, and how a total cost should be spread.

Period expense = Estimated total cost × Proportion allocated to the period

Repairs and maintenance

Repairs occur irregularly though the asset supports revenue continuously — a period with no repair payment still consumed maintenance-related resources.

Depreciation of non-current assets

Vehicles, machinery and computer equipment are consumed through use, wear, age and obsolescence. Depreciation is an expense even though no cash moves when it is recorded.

Advertising

Advertising may be bought in one period but earn revenue across several, and management may not know whether it produced a sale, how long the benefit lasts, or which period should carry the cost.

Servers useful for four years
Purchase cost
$120,000
Assumed useful life
4 years

Annual depreciation = $30,000

Obsolescence after three years
Purchase cost
$120,000
Assumed useful life
3 years

Annual depreciation = $40,000

The assumption changes reported profit even though the original cash payment remains the same.

Insurance, accrued interest, materials consumed, salaries and prepaid services all need apportioning. The objective is to measure resources used during the period, not payments made.

04 / Revenue

Assumptions behind revenue

Revenue is recognised when earned, not when cash arrives. The earning point blurs when customers may return goods, when part of the sale covers future services, or when work completes gradually over several periods.

Cash received before revenue is earned

Where a customer pays before delivery, cash is recorded as an asset and unearned revenue as a liability — the obligation to deliver in the future.

Cash received = Revenue earned + Unearned revenue

Sales with refund rights

A retailer sells a device for $5000 with a full money-back guarantee, and history shows about 10% of similar sales are refunded.

Item Amount
Cash received $5000
Revenue recognised $4500
Estimated refund liability $500

If the return period expires with no refund, the remaining liability becomes revenue. An underestimated refund rate overstates both revenue and profit.

Products bundled with future services

Enterprise hardware plus a two-year support package sells for a combined $8000, support valued at $800. Hardware revenue of $7200 is recognised immediately; the $800 starts as a liability. Allocating too little to future services inflates current revenue.

Support revenue recognised = $800 / 2 = $400

Long-term contracts

Long-term project revenue may be recognised progressively rather than on completion, with completion estimated from costs incurred.

Percentage completed = Costs incurred / Total estimated costs Revenue recognised = Contract value × Percentage completed

On a $1 million contract with $900,000 estimated costs and $450,000 incurred, the project is $450,000 / $900,000 = 50% complete. Management still judges:

  • total project costs
  • work completed
  • future cost overruns
  • whether the customer obligation has genuinely been satisfied

Understated cost estimates make a project look further advanced and pull revenue forward. More broadly, discretion allows distortion — underestimating refunds, booking deposits as revenue, undervaluing future service obligations, overstating completion, or moving future revenue into the current period.

Analytical questions

  • Has cash been received before the related product or service was delivered?
  • Are refund and return estimates based on reliable historical data?
  • How has revenue been allocated between products and future services?
  • Have long-term project estimates changed materially?
  • Is revenue growth supported by cash collection and completed obligations?
  • Could current profit be overstated through early revenue recognitio
05 / Judgement

Why profit is sometimes called an "opinion"

Accrual profit depends partly on estimates. Different reasonable assumptions about depreciation, maintenance, advertising or revenue allocation produce different profit figures for the same business — not arbitrary, but a measurement based on informed judgement.

Assumptions should be reasonable, consistently applied, evidence-backed and revisited when circumstances change — so look past the profit figure to the assumptions behind the major expenses.

Under accrual accounting, profit depends partly on management's judgement about when and how much expense should be recognised
💡

The credibility of reported profit therefore depends on the quality and consistency of the assumptions behind it.

Questions to ask

  • What assumptions were used to allocate expenses across periods?
  • Have useful-life or depreciation estimates changed?
  • Are costs being postponed to make current profit appear stronger?
  • Are advertising and maintenance costs recognised consistently?
  • Would different reasonable assumptions materially change reported profit?
06 / Gross profit

Gross profit and gross margin

Cost of goods sold (COGS), or cost of sales, is the cost directly tied to producing, purchasing, delivering or selling. It excludes broader operating costs such as corporate administration, HR and some marketing.

Business type Likely direct costs
Retailer Inventory purchased for resale
Manufacturer Raw materials, production labour and supplies
Airline Fuel and pilot wages
SaaS company Cloud hosting and direct service-delivery costs
Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of goods sold Gross margin = Gross profit / Revenue × 100
Hardware company
Revenue
$10 million
Direct production costs
$6 million

Gross profit = $4 million · margin 40%

The company retains 40 cents of gross profit from each $1 of revenue before paying its other operating expenses.

That $4 million must still cover marketing, administration, R&D, interest, tax and shareholder profit. Gross profit measures product profitability before broader operating costs, so a high gross margin is not automatically enough.

Direct and Indirect Cost Judgement

Determining COGS is not always straightforward. Costs sit in COGS when closely linked to producing or delivering revenue. Judgement is needed for supervisors, customer support, product research, advertising, security, purchasing and marketing, whose costs may not trace to a particular sale.

Scope for manipulation

Management can lift reported gross profit by shifting costs from COGS into operating expenses. Reclassifying direct customer-service costs as administration cuts COGS and raises gross margin while total profit is unchanged — product economics look better without any real improvement.

Direction Possible causes
Falling margin Rising input, production or delivery costs; an inability to increase selling prices; greater discounting; increased competition; an unfavourable product mix; weaker bargaining power with suppliers. A persistent decline may be an early warning of future pressure on profit.
Rising margin Higher prices; lower supplier or production costs; economies of scale; improved operational efficiency; a shift towards higher-margin products. Analysts should confirm the increase is not caused by expense reclassification.

A product may generate substantial revenue but still destroy value if its gross margin is too low.

07 / Cost categories

OPEX, CAPEX and non-cash charges

Beyond COGS sit the costs of running the wider business. Operating expenditure (OPEX) supports normal operations — head-office rent, admin salaries, HR and IT, insurance, advertising, utilities, commissions, interest, tax, depreciation, amortisation and impairment. Capital expenditure (CAPEX) acquires or improves non-current assets whose benefits span more than one period.

Buying servers for $240,000 and paying $30,000 a year to maintain them treats the purchase as CAPEX, the maintenance as OPEX, and the server cost as depreciation over its useful life.

Cost type Behaviour Examples
Fixed cost Generally unchanged within a relevant activity range Rent, insurance, CEO salary
Variable cost Changes with production or sales volume Sales commissions, electricity usage
Mixed or step cost Stable initially but rises when activity passes a threshold Supervisors, IT capacity, support staff

The classification of a cost as fixed or variable therefore depends on the time period and level of activity being analysed.

Non-cash charges

Depreciation covers tangible assets, amortisation intangibles such as licences. Both are non-cash expenses, because the cash moved when the asset was acquired. Extending an estimated useful life cuts annual depreciation and lifts current profit.

An impairment charge is recognised when an asset no longer delivers its original economic benefit. The asset falls, an expense hits the P&L, and equity decreases. Management may be slow to recognise it, because impairment reflects on earlier investment decisions.

08 / Levels of profit

Revenue down to the bottom line

"Profit" can refer to several figures within the P&L. Each removes a different category of cost and therefore answers a different analytical question, and no single measure should be assessed in isolation.

EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — is widely used to compare operating performance between firms, and is sometimes read as operating cash profit because D&A are non-cash. It is not cash flow: it ignores receivables, payables, capital expenditure, interest and tax, so strong EBITDA can coexist with cash pressure.

EBIT, or operating profit, includes depreciation and amortisation and so counts the cost of consuming operating assets. It measures performance before financing and tax, which makes it useful for comparing companies with similar operations but different debt.

Item Company A Company B
Assets $200m $200m
EBIT $20m $20m
Interest expense $0 $6m
Profit before tax $20m $14m

Both generate the same EBIT from the same assets, so operating performance is similar; B reports lower profit before tax purely because it uses debt. Net profit after tax (NPAT) is the bottom line after all recognised expenses.

Abnormal and extraordinary items

Abnormal items arise from business operations but are not expected to continue regularly — restructuring costs, redundancy payments, major organisational changes. 09 / Comprehensive income

Beyond trading profit

The statement of comprehensive income extends the P&L: NPAT plus other gains or losses that change equity without being trading profit — generally from movements in asset values or exchange rates rather than completed transactions.

Comprehensive income = NPAT + Other comprehensive income

A data centre carried at $40 million and independently revalued at $50 million adds $10 million to the asset and $10 million to the asset revaluation reserve. It is not normal profit: the property has not been sold, no cash arrived, and the value could fall again.

A gain is unrealised when the asset has moved in value but not been sold. So comprehensive income can be high while sales, costs and trading profit are unchanged.

Other comprehensive income may be estimated, volatile, reversible and unrelated to cash generation — less telling about financial strength than NPAT and operating cash flow.

10 / Analysis

Analysing a profit and loss statement

The aim is to understand where profit came from and whether it is sustainable. Vertical analysis states each item as a percentage of revenue: COGS at 71% of revenue means about 71 cents of every sales dollar goes to direct cost.

Expense percentage = Expense / Revenue × 100 Profit margin = Profit measure / Revenue × 100

A single year says little, so track revenue growth, COGS percentage, gross margin, operating expenses, EBITDA and EBIT margins, interest expense and NPAT margin over several periods.

Revenue rising but margins failing Growth may be costly or driven by discounting

11 / Framework

What to carry out of the P&L

A strong analysis works through seven questions in order.

  1. Recognition: Has revenue genuinely been earned?
  2. Cost adequacy: Are expenses sufficient to support current and future performance?
  3. Margins: Are gross, operating and net margins improving?
  4. Trends: How has the cost structure changed over time?
  5. Benchmarks: How does performance compare with similar firms?
  6. Assumptions: Could accounting estimates be overstating profit?
  7. Sustainability: Is profit driven by genuine operations or temporary cost reductions and one-off items?

Low costs are not the goal. Employees, technology, marketing, maintenance and materials generate future revenue, so over-cutting lifts short-term profit while weakening capability, asset condition and future growth.

  • The P&L measures whether activities are profitable over a defined period; the balance sheet records the accumulated consequences.
  • Reported profit does not necessarily represent cash available to the organisation.
  • Accrual accounting separates operating performance from cash collection and payment timing.
  • Gross margin allows profitability to be compared across firms and periods of different sizes.
  • Cost classification involves management judgement and can distort gross-margin comparisons.
  • Depreciation, amortisation and impairment are expenses but do not normally involve cash outflows.
  • EBITDA is useful for comparisons but should not be mistaken for actual cash flow.
  • Comprehensive income measures broader changes in wealth, not only trading performance.
  • Reported profit is a structured accounting estimate, not a purely objective cash figure.
Has revenue been appropriately recognised? Are expenses adequate to support sustainable performance?
01 / Why the statement exists

Profit does not equal cash flow

The cash flow statement explains how cash moved in and out over a period. It exists because profit is not cash flow: revenue may be recognised before customers pay, expenses before suppliers are paid, and non-cash expenses such as depreciation, bad debts and provisions cut profit without cutting cash.

Category Meaning Typical cash flows
Operating activities (CFOA) Cash generated or used by normal business operations Customer receipts, supplier payments and operating expenses
Investing activities (CFIA) Cash used to buy or received from selling long-term assets Property, equipment and other non-current assets
Financing activities (CFFA) Cash raised from or returned to lenders and owners Borrowings, debt repayments, equity contributions and dividends
Net change in cash = CFOA + CFIA + CFFA

A business can open and close the year on the same cash balance after a great deal of activity — a SaaS company might collect subscriptions, invest in cloud infrastructure, then repay debt or pay dividends. The unchanged balance does not mean nothing happened.

What it lets you assess

  • Whether operations are genuinely generating cash
  • Whether investment spending supports maintenance or growth
  • Whether the business depends heavily on debt or shareholder funding
  • How cash is being returned to lenders and owners
  • Why cash balances changed despite reported profits
💡

The central issue is not simply how much cash the business holds, but where the cash came from and how management used it.

02 / Operating cash flow

Does the core business generate cash?

Operating cash flow measures cash from day-to-day operations — whether the core business funds itself without leaning on borrowing or new shareholder money.

Operating cash flow = Cash received from customers − cash paid to suppliers and employers − interest paid + interest received − income tax paid

A healthy business normally produces positive operating cash flow, judged relative to its size, assets and growth. A decline may be fine if assets were sold, operations discontinued, or cash temporarily put into working capital. It is a warning when assets and revenue rise, operating cash flow falls or lags, and debt increasingly funds operations.

Revenue is not cash receipts

Revenue is recognised when earned; cash only when received. Revenue above receipts means customers have extended terms — a persistent gap signals weak credit policy, slow payment, rising receivables and more reliance on debt. Receipts above revenue means customers pay in advance: a SaaS company collects annually but recognises gradually, the unearned amount sitting as a liability. Receipts may also include GST, which is not revenue.

Reconciling profit with operating cash flow

A useful health test compares profit after tax against operating cash flow; the two differ because profit carries accruals and non-cash items.

Profit after tax + Non-cash expenses ± Working capital movements = Operating cash flow

Non-cash expenses — depreciation, impairment, bad debts, provisions — cut profit but not cash, so they are added back. Working capital, by contrast, absorbs operating cash.

Working capital movement Effect on operating cash flow
Increase in inventory Decreases cash flow
Increase in receivables Decreases cash flow
Reduction in payables Decreases cash flow

Large amounts of cash tied up in inventory or unpaid customer accounts may indicate poor working-capital management.

💡

The key issue is not only whether a business reports a profit, but whether that profit is being converted into operating cash.

03 / Investing

Is the asset base being future-proofed?

Investing cash flow shows cash spent on or received from non-current assets — whether the business is maintaining its asset base, expanding capacity, or buying assets that may generate future revenue.

Investing activity Cash-flow effect
Purchase of property, plant and equipment Outflow
Purchase of development of intangible assets Outflow
Acquisition of another business Outflow
Property development expenditure Outflow
Sale of non-current assets or businesses Inflow

Spending on long-term assets is capital expenditure (CAPEX). Negative investing cash flow is not a concern in itself — it may mean replacement, expansion or new technology. Delaying investment lifts short-term operating cash flow but weakens the future if equipment becomes unreliable, technology dates, efficiency slips, or demand cannot be met.

A business can report an accounting loss while generating healthy cash, because depreciation and impairment cut profit without a cash payment. An impairment records a fall in accounting value — it does not mean the asset was sold or cash received.

Asset replacement ratio

Asset replacement ratio = Capital expenditure / Depreciation Expense
Result over several years Possible interpretation
Approximately 1.0 Assets may be replaced at roughly the rate they depreciate Holding steady
Consistently above 1.0 Possible expansion or major asset renewal Investing
Consistently below 1.0 Possible underinvestment or ageing assets Warning

The ratio should be assessed over approximately three to four years, as investment expenditure can vary significantly between individual periods.

💡

The central issue is whether the business is using current cash flows to future-proof its asset base, rather than improving short-term cash flow by postponing necessary investment.

04 / Financing

Where the money comes from, and who gets it back

Financing cash flow shows cash received from or paid to the providers of finance — how funds are raised, how debt and equity are managed, and whether financing decisions are adding risk.

Financing activity Cash-flow effect
New long-term borrowings Inflow
Repayment of long-term debt principal Outflow
Issue of shares Inflow
Share buybacks Outflow
Dividends paid Outflow

Under the course framing, short-term borrowings and interest payments sit in operating rather than financing cash flow. Financing records the principal borrowed or repaid; interest is separate because it is the cost of servicing debt. Dividends also reduce retained earnings, leaving less internal funding.

Dividends should be covered by sustainable operating cash flow. Repeatedly funding them through new borrowing, new equity, asset sales or drawing down reserves creates the appearance of strong returns while weakening the business.

Financing example
Operating cash flow
+$200m
Investing cash flow
$0m
Financing cash flow
−$230m
Dividends paid
$350m

$120m inflow − $350m dividends = −$230m

The dividend was funded by $200m from operating cash flow, $120m from new debt or equity, and $30m from existing cash reserves.

Questions to ask
  • Are dividends covered by operating cash flow?
  • Is debt being raised to fund shareholder distributions?
  • Are cash reserves being depleted?
  • Is the business returning cash instead of investing in long-term assets?
  • Is the financing strategy sustainable?

A high dividend is not automatically evidence of strong performance.

💡

The key question is where the cash used to pay it came from.

06 / Free cash flow

What is left for the financiers

Free cash flow is what remains after operations generate cash and long-term assets are paid for — the cash available to financiers for debt repayment, dividends and buybacks. It is not a statement line item, so it must be calculated. It matters because operating cash cannot all be distributed: some must replace depreciating assets and fund growth.

Free cash flow = Cash flow from operating activities + Cash flow from investing activities FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Net Investment Expenditure $3.1bn CFOA + (−$1.4bn CFIA) = $1.7bn FCF
Business CFOA CFIA FCF Dividend Interpretation
Woolworths $3.1bn −$1.4bn $1.7bn $0.5bn Comfortably covered
Qantas $2.7bn −$2.1bn $0.6bn $0.3bn Covered while investing

Positive FCF suggests dividends and debt repayments can be funded internally. Negative FCF is not automatically a problem where it reflects investment for growth, but persistent negative FCF needs investigation. Where FCF exceeds dividends, distributions are internally supported; where it falls short, debt, equity or reserves are filling the gap, and repeated shortfalls suggest an unsustainable dividend policy. A ten-year trend shows whether it holds across conditions.

07 / Practice 1

Diagnosing financial health

Positive profit is not strong performance. Profitability must be compared against prior periods, similar businesses, industry benchmarks and the risk of the model. This firm reports profit after tax of $14.8m, a gross margin near 19.5% and interest cover around 3.7 times — positive, but unjudgeable without comparison.

Asset growth is not always positive

Receivables and inventory increased by $16m while payables decreased by $10m, so working capital absorbed $26m of cash.

Cash absorbed by working capital = $16m + $10m = $26m

That may mean customers are slower to pay, inventory is accumulating, suppliers are giving less credit, or the firm pays faster than it collects. Funding recurring working capital with long-term debt is a warning sign — long-term borrowing is propping up the operating cycle rather than expansion.

Investment in non-current assets

Property, plant and equipment rose $10m against $10m of depreciation, leaving net book value unchanged at $115m — assets replaced at roughly the rate they are consumed, rather than capacity expanded.

09 / Operating cash cycle

How long cash is tied up before it comes back

The operating cash cycle measures how long cash sits in inventory and receivables before collection, and so how well working capital is managed. A longer cycle needs more cash to run the business.

Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities Operating cash cycle = Days inventory + Days receivables Days inventory = Inventory / (Cost of goods sold / 365) Days receivables = Accounts receivable / (Revenue / 365) Days payables = Accounts payable / (Cost of goods sold / 365) Funding gap = Operating cash cycle − Days payables

Days inventory is the average hold before sale; days receivables the average wait for payment. In services, work in progress behaves like inventory, since time is committed before payment. Average balances may represent the period better than point-in-time ones. Suppliers finance part of the cycle: higher days payable keeps cash longer, lower days payable demands more internal cash or debt.

Measure Previous period Current movement / result
Days inventory 47 days Increased by 12 days
Days receivables 38 days Increased by 17 days
Operating cash cycle 85 days Increased by 29 days
Days payables 35 days Fell to 11 days
Funding gap 50 days Increased to 102 days +52 days

The business now needs about 52 additional days of financing, because inventory sat longer, customers paid later and suppliers gave less credit. A widening funding gap forces extra borrowing, use of reserves, renegotiated supplier terms, better collections or lower inventory — and borrowing adds interest, hitting both profit and cash flow.

10 / Takeaways

What to carry out of Cash Flow

  • Profit does not equal cash flow — the statement tests whether accounting transactions produced an actual movement of cash.
  • Net change in cash = CFOA + CFIA + CFFA; an unchanged closing balance does not mean nothing happened.
  • A healthy business would normally produce positive operating cash flow, assessed relative to its size, assets and growth.
  • Negative investing cash flow is not automatically a warning sign — it may be investment in future growth.
  • Compare capital expenditure with depreciation over three to four years to judge asset replacement.
  • A high dividend is not evidence of strong performance; the question is where the cash to pay it came from.
  • Free cash flow = CFOA + CFIA, and dividends should sit inside it.
  • In Practice 2, $24.8m of cash earnings became −$1.2m of operating cash flow because working capital absorbed $26m, and the $10m dividend was ultimately funded by $23.2m of new long-term debt.
  • A lengthening operating cash cycle alongside falling supplier credit forces the business to fund the difference itself.
01 / The return chain

Raw numbers cannot judge performance

Raw revenue, profit and asset values cannot determine financial performance on their own because they ignore the resources required to generate those outcomes. Returns analysis links the income statement and the balance sheet to assess how effectively the business converts its asset base into profit.

Measure Formula What it diagnoses
Asset utilisation Revenue / Assets How effectively assets generate revenue
Profit margin Profit / Revenue How effectively revenue is converted into profit
Return on assets (ROA) Profit / Assets Overall effectiveness of assets in generating profit
ROA = Asset Utilisation x Profit Margin (Revenue / Assets) × (Profit / Revenue) = Profit / Assets

The second line is why the decomposition holds: revenue cancels, leaving profit over assets.

💡

ROA tells us the outcome; asset utilisation and margin help explain why that outcome occurred.

02 / Two drivers

How a business improves its return on assets

A business can improve its return on assets through two broad mechanisms.

  • Make assets work harder — generate more revenue from the existing asset base, reflected in higher asset utilisation.
  • Earn more from each dollar of revenue — improve pricing, product mix or cost efficiency, reflected in a higher profit margin.
💡

A high-margin, asset intensive business may produce the same ROA as a lower-margin business with very efficient asset utilisation.

Why decomposition matters

A software company may have strong margins because incremental software sales have relatively low costs, but weak asset utilisation after a large acquisition increases its asset base. Even if profit grows, ROA may fail if the additional assets do not generate enough incremental revenue and profit.

That is what makes the decomposition useful for diagnosing whether performance problems originate primary from asset productivity or the profitability of sales.

Interpreting business performance

A larger company is not necessarily a better-performing company simple because it has more assets, higher revenue or greater absolute profit.

❓

How effectively does the business generate earnings from the resources committed to it?

  • ROA integrates profitability and asset efficiency into one measure.
  • Use asset utilisation and profit margin to diagnose the drivers behind ROA, rather than treating ratios independently.
  • When ROA changes, ask whether asset utilisation changed, whether profit margin changed, and which factor is driving the change.
  • Ask whether newly acquired or invested assets are generating sufficient incremental returns.
03 / Return on equity

The owners’ return, and what financing does to it

Return on Equity (ROE) measures the return generated on the owners’ investment in the business.

ROE = Profit / Equity

Profits generated for shareholders can either be distributed as dividends or retained and reinvested in the business.

ROE should not be interpreted without considering how the business is financed. A business can increase ROE by financing more of its assets with debt rather than equity, leaving less equity supporting the same business:

Profit / ↓Equity → ↑ROE

A higher ROE therefore does not automatically mean better underlying business performance — the higher return may partly result from financial leverage rather than superior profitability or asset efficiency.

The risk-return trade off

Higher Debt Effect
Less equity required Can increase ROE
Debt must be services Creates fixed financial obligations
Poor trading conditions Greater difficulty meeting interest and repayments
Greater leverage Higher financial risk to owners
💡

Leverage can amplify owners’ returns, but it also increases the risk being taken to generate those returns.

04 / Two kinds of risk

Where the risk comes from

Financial risk arises from the financing structure of the business, particularly the use of debt. Debt creates obligations that remain even when business performance deteriorates. Greater financial risk can mean:

  • Greater volatility in returns/profits to owners
  • Greater pressure on cash flows
  • Difficulty servicing interest when revenue or profit fails
  • Difficulty repaying debt under adverse trading conditions
❓

What level of financial risk is required to generate that ROE?

Business risk

Business risk arises from the underlying operations and industry of the business rather than from its financing choices. It reflects the stability and predictability of earnings, and a business with relatively stable earnings has lower business risk because its future cash flows are more predictable. Potential sources identified include:

  • Uncertain or emerging markets
  • Entrepreneurial/new ventures
  • Exchange-rate exposure
  • Commodity-price exposure
  • Variability in customer demand or industry conditions
05 / Reading both together

Business risk against financial risk

These risks need to be considered together.

Risk Driven by Core question
Business risk Operations and industry How predictable are earnings and cash flows?
Financial risk Debt and financing structure Can the business meet its financial obligations?

A business with stable cash flows and low business risk may be better positioned to carry debt, because it has greater certainty about its ability to service that debt. Conversely, a business with highly uncertain earnings may have less capacity to safely take on substantial financial leverage.

What the ROA–ROE difference reveals

ROA assesses returns generated from the assets of the business, whereas ROE assesses returns generated for equity owners. A company can therefore have a relatively weaker ROA and a relatively stronger ROE if debt financing increases the return attributable to the smaller equity base. That difference is a warning sign to investigate leverage and financial risk, rather than assuming the higher ROE represents superior performance.

  • Higher ROE must be interpreted alongside financial leverage.
  • Debt can improve owners’ returns without improving the underlying productivity of the business.
  • Higher expected returns generally need to be considered against the additional risk taken to achieve them.
  • Assess business risk and financial risk together when deciding whether debt levels are sustainable.
  • When comparing businesses, ask whether high ROE comes from genuinely strong performance or greater leverage, how stable earnings and cash flows are, whether debt could still be serviced if trading conditions deteriorated, and whether the level of debt is appropriate given the underlying business risk.
06 / Cash committed to operations

Working capital and operating cash

Working capital represents funds committed to the day-to-day operations of the business.

Working Capital = Short-term Assets - Short-term Liabilities

Working capital is necessary to operate, but excessive amounts can tie up cash that could otherwise be invested elsewhere. The key analytical issue is therefore not simply how much working capital exists, but how efficiently it is being managed and converted back into cash.

Cash flow from operations

Cash flow from operating activities (CFOA) shows the cash generated by the core operations of the business. Unlike accounting profit, CFOA reflects actual cash movement. A useful performance ratio is:

CFOA Margin = CFOA / Revenue

This shows how much operating cash is generated from each dollar of revenue. A lower ratio may indicate that revenue growth is not translating efficiently into cash.

CFOA margin of 10%

$0.10 of operating cash per $1 of revenue

CFOA margin of 6%

$0.06 of operating cash per $1 of revenue

07 / Profit versus cash

A profitable business can still be short of cash

A profitable business can still experience cash pressure, so profit and CFOA must be compared. Strong working capital management should generally support good conversion of profit into operating cash flow.

Accounting Profit vs CFOA

If CFOA << Profit, this is a signal to investigate where cash is being absorbed.

Working capital as a cash-flow diagnosis

A business can report increasing revenue and profit whilst simultaneously consuming cash if more funds become tied up in operating assets. Where profit is strong but CFOA is weak, the next analytical step is to examine the business’s working capital cycle and potential funding gap.

💡

Profit measures economic performance; operating cash flow reveals whether that performance is actually producing cash.

Why this matters for management

Poor cash conversion can restrict a firm’s ability to fund new investments, repay debt, distribute dividends, respond to unexpected pressures and finance continued growth internally. Profitable growth can still create financial strain if the additional sales require increasingly large amounts of working capital.

  • Working capital is cash committed to operations and therefore has an opportunity cost.
  • CFOA / revenue indicates how effectively sales are converting into operating cash.
  • Compare profit with CFOA to assess the quality of reported earnings and cash conversion.
  • Strong profit with weak CFOA should trigger investigation of inventory, receivables and other working-capital demands.
  • A manager should ask whether revenue growth is producing proportionate cash flow, why CFOA is materially different from profit, whether working capital is absorbing increasing amounts of cash, and whether growth is creating a larger funding requirement.
08 / The right question

Ratios are diagnostic tools, not answers

Financial ratios are most useful as diagnostic tools, but not as the final answers. The goal is to use them to identify where performance looks strong or weak and then ask what may be causing it. A dashboard of ratios can summarise performance, but a favourable ratio may hide a future weakness, while an unfavourable ratio may have a reasonable explanation.

What is the ratio ? → Why is it at that level ? → Is it sustainable? → What risks sit behind it?

Key diagnostic questions

Area What the ratio may suggest The right question
Asset utilisation Assets are generating revenue efficiently Are assets being maintained and replaced adequately, or is performance being boosted by an ageing/depreciated asset base
Profit margin Strong conversion of revenue into profit Is the margin sustainable, or is it being achieved by cutting important spending such as employees, innovation or future capability?
Return on assets Strong return from the asset base Is the higher return justified once the risk of the business is considered
Debt / Leverage Debt is being used to finance assets Is the level of debt excessive relative to the stability and diversification of cash flows
Business risk Earnings may be more less predictable How stable are cash flows, markets, management capability and access to future opportunities?
Return on equity Shareholders are receiving a strong return Is the return high enough for the risk shareholders are taking?
Cash / working capital Profit may or may not be converted well into cash Is cash being trapped in inventory or receivables? Is there a funding gap?
Supplier credit Payables may help finance operations Is the business overly dependent on supplier credit that could be reduced or withdrawn?

Sustainability matters more than the current number

A strong current ratio can sometimes be achieved at the expense of future performance.

  • High asset utilisation may reflect underinvestment in replacement assets
  • High profit margins may result from postponing discretionary but strategically important spending
  • High ROE may be driven by debt rather than stronger operating performance
  • Strong cash flow may partly depend on stretching supplier payments
❓

Is current financial performance being achieved in a way that can be sustained?

09 / Return against risk

Risk changes the meaning of a return

Returns cannot be assessed independently of risk. A higher ROA or ROE is only attractive if the return is sufficient compensation for business risk, financial risk and the uncertainty of future cash flows. This introduces the concept of a required return.

If the shareholders require a 20% return for the level of risk they are taking but the business generates only 19%, the return may look high in isolation but may still be insufficient relative to risk.

Actual Return should be assessed against Required Return

Some risks cannot be reduced to a ratio

Business risk is partly qualitative and there is no single calculation that captures it completely. Analysts also need to consider the stability of earnings and cash flows, diversification, access to markets, the quality and capability of management, industry conditions and exposure to uncertainty. Good financial analysis therefore combines quantitative ratios with qualitative judgement.

10 / The diagnostic process

What the analysis is actually for

  1. Observe the ratio: identify unusual strengths, weaknesses or trends.
  2. Diagnose the driver: determine what operational, financing or working-capital factors explain it.
  3. Assess risk: consider whether the result comes with additional business or financial riskl.
  4. Test sustainability: ask whether current performance can continue without damaging future capability.
  5. Compare with required performance: determine whether the return is sufficient given the resources and risks involved.

The purpose of financial analysis is not simply to identify the company with the highest ratio. A good analyst therefore sues ratios to generate better questions, then combines profitability, asset efficiency, leverage, risk and cash-flow evidence to form an overall judgement of financial health.

❓

What explains the ratio, what risks were taken to achieve it, and is that performance sustainable?

11 / CEO bonus

A stable ROE over a weakening business

ROE should never be used alone to judge management performance. Here the CEO argues for a bonus because ROE has remained constant, despite profits failing during a difficult trading period.

❓

Was ROE maintained through stronger underlying performance, or through increased financial leverage?

The supporting ratios show deterioration in the core business.

Measure Trend Interpretation
Asset utilisation Declining More assets are being employed without a matching increase in revenue
Profit margin Declining Less profit is being generated from each dollar of revenue
ROA Declining Overall operating effectiveness of the asset base is worsening
ROE Stable Appears strong, but requires explanation
Leverage Increasing More debt is being used relative to shareholder equity

The combination of failing asset utilisation and falling margins meaning underlying operational performance is deteriorating.

ROE = ROA x Financial Leverage ↓ROA + ↑Leverage → ROE may remain unchanged

The business increases debt relative to equity. As equity becomes a smaller proportion of total financing, the remaining shareholder funds are more highly leveraged, so the headline ROE can look healthy even while the business itself becomes less efficient and more risky.

12 / Judging the claim

Rewarding the metric or the business

Increasing debt does not repair weak operating performance. It can instead mask deterioration, supporting shareholder returns while increasing:

  • Interest obligations
  • Repayment commitments
  • Exposure to downturns
  • Financial risk

A CEO should not be rewarded simply for delivering a target financial ratio. The analyst should determine how the result was achieved.

On the information provided

  • Assets increased without sufficient revenue growth
  • Margins deteriorated
  • ROA declined
  • Debt increased
  • Financial risk increased
  • Leverage kept ROE artificially stable

The maintained ROE reflects financial engineering through leverage rather than improved operating performance.

Management incentive risk

If executives are rewarded for metrics such as ROE, they may have incentives to improve the metric in ways that do not strengthen long-term business performance. Performance measures should therefore consider operating efficiency, profitability, asset productivity, leverage and risk, and the sustainability of returns.

When evaluating management performance, ask

  • Is ROE supported by improving ROA, margins and asset utilisation?
  • Has leverage increased simply to maintain shareholder returns?
  • Is management creating stronger underlying performance or merely taking financial risk?
  • Is the apparent performance sustainable?
💡

Never reward the outcome before understanding the mechanism that produced it.

13 / Working capital

How long cash stays trapped in operations

Working capital represents funds committed to the firm’s day-to-day operating cycle. The managerial issue is the opportunity cost of these funds: money tied up in working capital cannot be deployed elsewhere for investment or growth.

Working Capital = Short-term Assets - Short-term Liabilities
  • Inventory — cash tied up before goods are sold
  • Receivables — cash tied up after a sale waiting for customers to pay
  • Payables — supplier credit that helps finance operations

Measuring the operating cycle in days

Days Inventory = Inventory / COGS per day COGS per day = Annual COGS / 365 Days Receivables = Receivables / Revenue per day Revenue per day = Annual Revenue / 365 Days Payables = Payables / COGS per day
Measure What it measures What a change may indicate
Days Inventory How long cash remains tied up in inventory before sale A rising number may warrant investigation into slow-moving inventory, excess stock or deterioration in inventory quality
Days Receivables How long customers take to pay after a sale Longer collection periods may indicate more generous credit policies, weaker collection processes or poorer-quality receivables
Days Payables How long the business takes to pay suppliers Supplier credit effectively provides short-term financing for the operating cycle

A longer funding gap means more capital is tied up for longer. This can:

  • Increase borrowing requirements
  • Increase interest expense
  • Weaken operating cash flow
  • Reduce funds available for investment
  • Place greater pressure on liquidity as the business grows

Reducing the funding gap can therefore release cash and improve value without requiring additional revenue growth.

When the gap increases

An increase should not simply be accepted as a normal consequence of growth. Ask which component is responsible.

Driver Possible issue
Increase in inventory days Excess, obsolete or slow-moving stock
Increase in receivable days Weaker credit policy or slower customer payments
Decrease in payable days Less supplier credit / faster payments
Increase in funding gap More external funding required to support outcomes

The source particularly highlights investing whether deterioration is coming from inventory quality and/or customer credit policies.

The key questions

  • Is the funding gap increasing?
  • Is inventory being held for too long?
  • Are customers taking longer to pay?
  • Is supplier credit sufficient?
  • What is the financing cost of the working-capital requirement?
💡

Reducing unnecessary days in the operating cycle can release cash, lower financing costs and improve financial health even without increasing profit.

15 / Sustainable growth

How fast the business can grow on its own settings

The sustainable growth rate (SGR) estimates how quickly a business can grow revenue without issuing new equity or allowing its debt ratios to increase, assuming its underlying financial settings remain broadly unchanged.

Revenue growth usually requires additional assets to support higher sales volumes, working capital, inventory and receivables, and operating capacity. If retained earnings are insufficient to finance these assets, the firm may need to raise more debt, increasing financial risk, or issue new equity, which may be costly and dilute existing owners.

❓

Can the company’s current financing and operating model support that growth?

SGR = ROE x Retention Rate ROE = Profit After Tax / Beginning Equity Retention Rate = Retained Earnings / Profit

The retention rate represents the proportion of profit kept and reinvested rather than paid as dividends.

Worked example

Retention Rate
50%

SGR = 50%x50% = 25%

The business can grow revenue by approximately 25% while financing growth through retained earnings and proportionate debt, without issuing new equity or increasing its debt-to-equity ratio.

16 / Growth above SGR

When actual growth outruns the benchmark

Actual growth exceeding SGR is not automatically bad, it means the analyst should investigate how the extra growth was financed or enabled.

Driver Effect Interpretation
More debt Debt ratio rises Higher financial risk
New equity More owner funding Can support growth, but may be costly/dilutive
Lower dividends Retention rate rises More internal finance, but less cash to shareholders
Higher margins / lower costs ROE can improve More profits available to support growth
Better asset utilisation More revenue from existing assets Strong operational explanation for faster growth

The strongest outcome is when growth above the SGR is achieved through improved asset utilisation, this occurs when revenue grows faster than assets. The business can then support additional sales without an equivalent increase in assets and financing.

  • Spare productivity capacity
  • Better use of existing assets
  • Improving working-capital management

Improving the rate itself

Since SGR = ROE x Retention, businesses can increase SGR by changing the drivers of either component. However, not all approaches are equally attractive.

Approach Effect
Cut dividends Increases retention, but reduces shareholder distributions
Raise equity Supports growth, but introduces additional owner capital
Increase debt Can support growth but increases financial risk
Cut costs May improve profitability, but could damage long-term capability if excessive
Improve asset utilisation Supports more revenue from the existing asset base and can represent genuine productivity improvement

Operational efficiency and asset utilisation are the more attractive path to supporting higher sustainable growth.

When actual growth > SGR, ask

  • Has leverage increased?
  • Has new equity been issues?
  • Has the retention/dividend policy changed?
  • Have margins improved?
  • Has asset utilisation improved?

SGR is not a maximum growth limit, it is a benchmark for assessing whether growth is consistent with the firm’s existing profitability, dividend policy and financing structure.

💡

Growth supported by greater efficiency can be positive. Growth funded mainly through increasing debt may instead indicate rising financial risk and potential future distress.

17 / Financial risk

The risk you can measure

Financial risk arises from the use of debt financing and the firm’s ability to meet its debt obligations.

❓

How much debt does the firm carry? Can earnings comfortably cover interest? Can operating cash flow meet short-term obligations?

Ratio Formula What it indicates General rule of thumb from topic
Debt / Assets Debt ÷ Assets Proportion of assets financed by debt Preferably < 50%
Debt / Equity Debt ÷ Equity Debt relative to shareholder financing Preferably < 100%
Interest Cover EBIT ÷ Interest Expense Ability of earnings to service interest Preferably ≥ 3×

These are guidelines, not universal limits. Appropriate leverage depends on the stability and predictability of the business.

Interest Cover = EBIT / Interest Expense

An interest cover of 3x means the business generates $3 of EBIT for every $1 of interest expense. A higher ratio provides a greater margin of safety if earnings decline. A failing interest cover may signal increasing debt costs, weakening profitability, or declining capacity to service borrowing.

Cash and short-term risk

Cash Flow from Operations vs Current Liabilities

Debt ratios alone do not show whether the firm has enough cash to meet obligations. If operating cash flow is insufficient, the business may need to raise additional debt, use cash reserves or sell assets — which increases vulnerability during periods of financial stress.

Liquidity and access to funding

  • Does the business have access to additional borrowing?
  • Does it have sufficient cash reserves?
  • Could it survive a temporary fall in prices, demand or production?
💡

Holding excessive cash may reduce returns, but having access to liquidity provides resilience during temporary disruption.

18 / Business risk

The risk that needs judgement

Business risk comes from the firm’s operating model, industry and competitive environment. Unlike financial risk, many elements cannot be captured by one ratio.

Risk area Why it matters
Key customer dependence Losing one major customer could materially reduce revenue
Key supplier dependence Supplier failure or loss could disrupt operations
Reliance on a major project Failure or delay of one project could materially damage performance
Low revenue diversification Greater exposure to disruption in one product, service or market
Limited access to funding Less ability to survive temporary financial stress
Poor asset replacement Older assets may become inefficient or technologically obsolete
Low asset saleability Harder to raise cash during an economic downturn or disruption

Diversification as risk protection

A diversified business is generally less dependent on any single customer, supplier, product, service, market or project, and is therefore more resilient if one part is disrupted. The topic also recognises that specialisation can create expertise, so the issue is not diversification for its own sake, but avoiding excessive concentration risk.

Asset replacement and future risk

An apparently efficient asset base may become a risk if management is underinvesting in replacement assets.

CAPEX vs Depreciation

If depreciation consistently exceeds replacement investment, assets may be ageing without sufficient reinvestment — declining productive capacity, higher maintenance requirements, technological obsolescence and reduced ability to generate future revenue. There is no strict ratio, but the relationship should be monitored over time.

19 / Benchmarking risk

The same ratio, two different risks

Financial ratios should not be judged in isolation. Compare them with the firm’s historical performance and with relevant industry benchmarks. A debt ratio that is reasonable for a stable, predictable business may be excessive for a highly cyclical or volatile company, and a firm with stable cash flows may safely support more debt than a volatile firm exposed to uncertain prices or earnings.

💡

The same financial ratio can imply different levels of risk depending on the business model.

Integrated risk assessment

A strong assessment combines both quantitative and qualitative evidence.

Risk assessment is not about finding one “safe” ratio

  • Is leverage appropriate for the stability of the firm’s cash flows?
  • Can earnings and operating cash comfortably service debt?
  • Is the business overly dependent on one customer, supplier or project?
  • Is management investing enough to maintain productive assets?
  • Does the firm have access to liquidity during a downturn?
  • How do these risks compare with the firm’s history and its industry?

Financial health depends not only on the return being generated, but on how much risk the business must carry to generate and sustain that return.
