The testYou have it when you can take a mid-project change request and say, unaided, whether accepting it is agility or scope creep — and what you would trade to make it the former.

01 / The constraint

Scope as a project constraint

Scope defines what the project will deliver: objectives, features, outputs and boundaries. It operates alongside time and cost as the project management triple constraint, and the three are interdependent.

Scope therefore provides the baseline for stakeholder expectations, resource allocation, prioritisation, and determining whether the project has actually succeeded.

Scope in Agile

Agile does not mean scope is uncontrolled. Scope can be iteratively refined as learning occurs, but changes must be tied to project objectives, user value, available time and resources, and agreed priorities.

💡

Flexibility is valuable; unmanaged change is scope creep.

02 / The brief

Finding the real scope: “the answer is in the brief”

Clients often provide too much information rather than a genuinely focused brief. The project team should listen for the “nugget of gold” — the idea, word or need that reveals what the client is fundamentally trying to achieve.

  • The brief should be interpreted calmly rather than treated as a checklist
  • The client’s own language helps demonstrate that the proposed solution originates from their underlying need
💡

Good scoping requires problem diagnosis before solution specification. Otherwise a team can execute the requested deliverables perfectly while solving the wrong problem.

03 / Outcomes, not mediums

Scope around what changes, not what gets built

A client may request Google ads, an app, something viral, or an event or activation. These are mediums, and not necessarily the real project outcome.

❓

“What do you want the customer or intended audience to do differently the next day?”

This moves the project definition from an output toward a desired behavioural outcome.

💡

Building an app is not success because the app launches. Success depends on whether the intended users adopt it and behave differently because of it.

04 / Impact

Attention, memory, behaviour

Attention alone can generate a temporary “sugar hit”. Even memorable work can fail if users remember the stunt rather than the brand or intended message.

💡

Reach and social engagement are not sufficient evidence of project effectiveness if they do not contribute to the intended behaviour or KPI.

05 / Two rings

Outer-ring and inner-ring design

Layer Purpose Risk if used alone
Outer ring Gain attention and draw people in Creates reach without meaningful impact
Inner ring Deepen engagement and change behaviour May have impact but fail to attract sufficient participation
06 / The core idea

The “20-word” test

Before choosing channels or execution details, the team should be able to explain why are we doing this? and what is the central idea?

💡

The concept should usually be explainable in roughly 15-30 words.

If it requires a lengthy explanation, then the underlying concept is probably not sufficiently clear. A strong concept should be simple, make intuitive sense, connect directly to the brief, generate excitement, and be capable of being scaled into execution.

Idea first, execution second

  • Avoid allowing the existing budget or delivery mechanism to determine the idea too early
  • First identify the strongest response to the problem
  • Then determine how to scale and execute it within the available constraints
Budget

Emphasises a spending limit.

Investment

Emphasises the return generated by the expenditure.

07 / Executable scope

The idea is not the project

A compelling idea may be generated quickly; delivering it effectively can require months. Agency or project value comes from converting the concept into an executable system.

  • Fits the investment
  • Remains safe and feasible
  • Avoids damaging compromises
  • Supports the intended customer behaviour
  • Integrates numerous operational details
💡

Execution quality protects the strategic intent of the idea.

Avoid destructive compromise

During delivery, teams face repeated opportunities to simplify the concept. Some adaptation is necessary, but each compromise should be assessed against one question.

❓

Does this change undermine the element that makes the project effective?

08 / The example

Microsoft Surface activation

  • Core concept was deliberately simple: hero the product itself
  • An enormous, functioning replica created attention
  • The surrounding customer journey — including queues and product education — turned attention into engagement and behaviour
  • Technical execution protected the illustration and therefore the integrity of the concept
💡

The project was more than a visually striking illustration; the full scope was the ecosystem around the idea.

09 / Teams and pitching

Scope, teams and delivery

For large projects, divide work into specialist or cross-functional teams, empower those teams to deliver their component, coordinate through weekly syncs and shared documentation, and maintain a common project philosophy and decision-making culture.

💡

This enables complex projects with hundreds of contributors to remain coherent without central management controlling every task.

Pitch scope ≠ deliver scope

A pitch does not need to perform the entire project in advance. Its job is to demonstrate that the team understands the problem, communicate the central solution simply, and establish confidence that the project can be executred.

Manage expectations without removing surprise

  • Clients need enough visibility to approve and trust the scope
  • Teams should avoid withholding major elements that could make the final product materially different from what was approved
  • Small non-critical enhancements can create a positive surprise without jeopardising scope
10 / Scope creep

Unpriced change, arriving one request at a time

💡

Scope creep = adding features, tasks, audiences or requirements after delivery begins without corresponding changes to time, cost or resources.

It often emerges gradually through apparently harmless requests: “just one more feature”, stakeholder wishlists, mid-project ideas, unclear decision rights, weak initial scope definition. The issue is not change itself — it is unpriced and unmanaged change.

Scope creep creates Why it matters
Timeline slippageMore work enters an unchanged schedule
Cost overrunsAdditional resources or effort are required
Reduced qualityTeams spread effort across too many deliverables
Team exhaustionCapacity is consumed by unplanned work
Diluted designAdded features weaken the original strategic intent
Misaligned outcomesProject becomes a collection of ideas rather than a response to the original problem

Managing scope creep

When a new requirement appears, do not automatically accept or reject it. Ask:

  1. Does it strengthen the original objective?
  2. What existing priority should it replace?
  3. What happens to time, investment or resources?
  4. Is it required now, or can it move to a later iteration?
💡

Agile principle: change is legitimate when it is consciously prioritised; continually adding work without trade-offs is not agility.

11 / MVP and MoSCoW

Two ways to decide what survives

An MVP is the smallest usable version of a solution that delivers meaningful value, tests the core assumptions, enables real user feedback, and avoids committing resources to unvalidated features. It is primarily a risk-reduction and learning mechanism, not simply a cheaper product.

💡

When scope pressure increases: protect the core value proposition rather than every proposed feature.

Priority Meaning Scoping decision
Must haveProject cannot function without it Protect
Should haveImportant for the longer-term solution Deliver if capacity allows
Could haveLow-priority / relatively low-cost enhancement First candidate to defer
Won’t haveNot a priority for this project or iteration Explicitly exlcude
❓

What must survive if time or resources tighten?

  • Scope should ultimately describe the change the project is trying to create, not merely the artefacts being produced. “Build an app” is weak scope; defining the user behaviour the app must enable is stronger
  • The strongest scoping question is often “what must the audience or customer do differently because the project exists?” — that connects design choices directly to effectiveness and KPIs
  • Good scope management is not resistance to change. It is the discipline of making explicit trade-offs between scope, time, investment and value whenever change occurs
  • When delivery pressure rises, managers should protect the core idea and Must-have value proposition, not every feature originally imagined
  • A project’s apparent simplicity can hide substantial execution complexity. The managerial test is not only whether the concept is clever, but whether the entire delivery ecosystem preserves the concept and produces the intended behavioural return
12 / The purpose

Managing project boundaries

Project Scope Management is the process of defining and controlling what is included and what is explicitly excluded from a project. Its purpose is to:

  • Prevent effort being spent on work that does not support project objectives
  • Align stakeholder expectations with what can realistically be delivered
  • Create a baseline against which delivery can be assessed
  • Manage changes without allowing uncontrolled scope creep
💡

The key managerial issue is maintaining alignment between agreed scope and actual delivery throughout the project lifecycle.

13 / The artefacts

SOW, WBS and a prioritisation rule

The Statement of Work (SOW) formalises what the project is expected to deliver — objectives, deliverables, timelines and success criteria. It performs two roles.

  1. Agreement — establishes what stakeholders have committed to
  2. Reference point — provides a baseline when questions or disagreements about scope arise later
💡

Management implication: ambiguity in the SOW increases the likelihood of expectation gaps and makes later scope control more difficult.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

The WBS decomposes the agreed project scope into smaller, manageable components.

Scope statement → Deliverables → Smaller work componenots

The WBS connects what has been promised with what the project team must actually execute. MoSCoW supports it by distinguishing essential requirements from work that can be deferred.

💡

MoSCoW acts as a scope-control mechanism when requirements compete for limited project capacity.

14 / The process

Six steps, from governance to control

Plan scope requirements

Create the scope management plan describing how project and product scope will be defined, validated and controlled. This establishes the governance process before detailed scope decisions are made.

❓

How will we decide what belongs in the project, who approves it, and how will later changes be handled?

Without this process, scope decisions become ad hoc?

15 / Requirements

Collecting them, then deciding which become scope

Determine and document stakeholder needs required to achieve project objectives. Methods may include interviews, focus groups, surveys and other stakeholder consultation. The objective is not simply to collect stakeholder wishlists.

🚨

Risk: incomplete or poorly understood requirements can create downstream revisions, disagreement and rework.

Define scope

Requirements are consolidated with requirements documentation, the project charter and the scope management plan. The result is the project scope statement, which becomes the basis for project activity.

Requirements

Stakeholder needs.

Scope

The agreed project response to those needs.

Not every identified requirement necessarily becomes part of the final project scope.

Scope definition → Decomposition → Manageable execution
💡

Managerial implication: decomposition improves visibility into resource requirements, scheduling and delivery risk before work progresses too far.

16 / Validate and control

Two processes people conflate

Validation obtains stakeholder acceptance of completed deliverables. Deliverables are inspected or reviewed, compared with what was agreed, and then either accepted as complete or returned for revision.

Validation is challenging because project teams and stakeholders may have different interpretations of what “complete” means. The clearer the agreed acceptance criteria, the less subjective it becomes.

💡

Validation should not be treated as an end-of-project surprise. Users and stakeholders should not be engaged sufficiently early for mismatches to surface while they are still manageable.

Control scope

Scope control monitors both project scope and product scope during delivery. Performance is compared with project requirements to identify variance, and a gap may require corrective action, revision of project work, or an approved change to the project plan.

Planned Scope ←→ Actual Delivery
Process Core question Focus
Validate scope “Have stakeholders accepted this deliverable?” Formal acceptnace
Control scope “Are we still delivering what was agreed?” Variance and change management
💡

A deliverable can be technically completed but still fail validation, while scope control occurs continuously throughout exeuction.

17 / Change

Change is not automatically scope creep

Scope control does not mean refusing all changes. The objective is to distinguish two things that look alike from the outside.

Managed change

Reviewed, justified and incorporated into the project plan.

Scope creep

Work expands without corresponding review or adjustment.

When actual and planned scope diverge, management should determine whether delivery needs correction to return to the agreed scope, or the scope itself should formally change. This protects project time, resources and intended outcomes.

Effective practices

  • Keep scope realistic — scope must reflect what can credibly be achieved with available resources and constraints. Overcommitting during scope definition simply transfers the problem into delivery
  • Involve users early — improves requirement quality, surfaces misunderstandings earlier, and reduces the risk of rejection during formal validation
  • Deliver incrementally and test — breaking delivery into incremental, testable stages creates repeated opportunities to compare actual outputs with stakeholder expectations
Deliver → Test → Validate → Adjust
  • Scope management is a governance cycle, not a one-off definition exercise: define the boundaries, translate them into executable work, validate outputs, continuously control variance
  • Managerial risk occurs when requirements, agreed scope and actual delivery drift apart. Managers should be able to trace each major deliverable back to an agreed requirement and objective
  • Validation and control solve different problems: validation confirms stakeholder acceptance; control identifies and manages deviations from the approved scope
  • A strong SOW and WBS reduce ambiguity from opposite directions: the SOW establishes what has been promised, while the WBS makes the work required to deliver that promise visible
  • Incremental delivery improves scope management because it converts stakeholder acceptance from a late-stage judgement into an ongoing feedback process, reducing rework and delivery risk
18 / The second question

Scope is necessary, but not sufficient

A project can meet its formal constraints and still fail commercially.

Traditional delivery

Did we deliver the agreed scope, on time and on budget?

CX asks a second question

Did the delivered outcome create a positive and valuable experience for the customer?

  • Technical completion does not guarantee customer satisfaction
  • Conversely, a project may deviate somewhat from its original budget, timeline or brief yet still be perceived as successful if it creates a strong customer outcome
  • Project success both should assess both delivery performance, and customer experience and value created
💡

Scope defines what is delivered; CX determines how that delivery is actually experienced.

19 / The mindset

Customers cannot be “owned”

CX begins from the assumption that organisations cannot permanently possess customer loyalty. They must continually engage customers, demonstrate value, influence future behaviour and earn continued participation.

One-off transaction → ongoing relationship

This creates a subscription mindset, and it applies beyond literal subscription businesses. The managerial implication is that every interaction can either strengthen or weaken the customer’s willingness to continue the relationship.

20 / Three levels

UI vs UX vs CX

Concept Focus Example question
UI — User Interface Surface interaction, screens, buttons, layout “Can the user operate this interface?”
UX — User Experience How the user interacts with the product or system “Is using this product intuitive and effective?”
CX — Customer Experience Total relationship across multiple touchpoints “How does the customer experience the organisation overall?”
21 / What good CX creates

Human-centred outcomes, not entertainment

Good CX should contribute to positive emotion, stronger relationships, customer autonomy, competence and meaningful engagement. CX is not entertainment for its own sake.

💡

The aim is to create interactions that customers interpret as valuable and remember positively.

Customers as participants

Effective CX treats customers as active participants, rather than passive recipients of a finished product.

Design for the customer → Design with the customer journey in mind
💡

Customer reactions and behaviours should be part of the project logic rather than something assessed only after launch.

Beyond the immediate product

Organisations can differentiate even when the technical output is similar by intentionally designing the process surrounding the output. Multi-functional teams help, because CX often crosses organisational boundaries rather than belonging to a single department.

💡

If the customer journey crosses functions, CX ownership must also cross functions.

22 / Memory and meaning

Why experience matters

CX is fundamentally human-centric because interactions are felt, interpreted, given personal meaning and remembered. Customers do not experience an organisation as a set of internal processes; they experience a sequence of moments.

Technically efficient transactions can still create negative CX if they generate uncertainty, frustration, lack of control or unmet expectations. Similarly, small positive interactions can disproportionately improve how the overall experience is remembered.

💡

CX design therefore focuses on intentional interaction, not simply functional completion.

23 / The bridge

The customer journey connects scope to experience

A customer journey describes how a customer interacts with a product, service or organisation over time.

First contact → interaction/use → service/support → ongoing engagement

A scope document may specify what the project must deliver, but journey thinking asks what the customer encounters before using it, what happens during interaction, what happens when something goes wrong, what happens afterwards, and what they will remember.

💡

Journey thinking exposes requirements that may not be visible when scope is defined around the product alone.

24 / Touchpoints

The moments that matter

Touchpoints are individual interactions between the customer and the product, service or organisation — discovering the service, ordering, paying, waiting, receiving the service, using the product, contacting support.

Each touchpoint creates an opportunity for customer expectations to meet reality.

Positive experience

Trust, satisfaction, delight.

Negative experience

Frustration, confusion, disappointment.

Customers usually judge the overall experience from the combined journey, not from one isolated interaction.

💡

Poor CX = strong product + poor surrounding touchpoints.

A well-scoped core deliverable may still fail if waiting, onboarding, payment, support or other journey stages create excessive friction.

25 / Journey mapping

Making hidden friction visible

Journey mapping tracks the customer’s needs, expectations, behaviours, emotions, frustrations and opportunities for improvement across each stage of the journey.

Product-focused view

“Did we deliver the coffee?”

Journey mapping

“What happened from the moment the customer arrived until after they received and consumed it?”

💡

Scope should account for the experience around the core deliverable when losing surrounding interactions materially affect customer value.

26 / Integration

Project discipline plus empathy

Scope tools such as the SOW and WBS establish what must be delivered, what work is required and where project boundaries sit. CX and journey mapping add how customers encounter those deliverables, where friction occurs, what expectations customers bring and what interactions create value.

SOW defines the promise → WBS organises deliver → Journey mapping tests the experience
💡

The objective is to ensure the project scope reflects the customer reality the project is intended to improve.

Five questions before work enters scope

  1. Does this requirement improve a meaningful customer touchpoint?
  2. Does it address a genuine customer need or merely add features?
  3. Which part of the journey does it improve?
  4. What customer emotion, behaviour or relationship outcome should change?
  5. Is that improvement important enough to justify its impact on scope, time and cost?
💡

These questions prevent CX from becoming another source of uncontrolled scope expansion.

  • Project success should not be judged solely by adherence to scope, time and cost — managers should also assess whether the project creates a customer experience that generates satisfaction, engagement and continued value
  • The customer journey is the practical mechanism for connecting CX to scope because it reveals where the project’s deliverables actually intersect with customers’ lived experience
  • UI and UX optimisation can still leave major CX failures untouched; managers need to examine the complete relationship across touchpoints rather than optimise individual interfaces in isolation
  • CX insight should inform scope, but it should not become an excuse for unlimited features. The managerial question is whether proposed work improves a material moment in the customer journey sufficiently to justify the required investment
  • The strongest projects combine scope discipline with customer empathy: clearly define what will be delivered, then ensure those deliverables support an experience customers actually value and want to repeat
27 / The problem

Growth depends on younger clients

$RUs has historically built its reputation around trusted, personalised financial advice for older and wealthier clients. Its strategic problem is future client acquisition.

Target segment: Australian’s in their 30s and 40s.

  • Increasingly building wealth, buying property and engaging with superannuation
  • Digitally native and likely to expect more accessible digital interactions
  • Adviser relationships can be sticky once established
🚨

Strategic risk: fail to acquire younger clients → competitors establish the relationship → $RUs may lose decades of future client value.

💡

The initiative is fundamentally a customer acquisition and long-term relationship strategy, not simply an app-development project.

28 / The killer idea

Future You

StellarCX proposes the Future you simulator: an AI powered mobile experience in which users interact with a digitally aged version of themselves.

Today’s financial choices → modelled future outcomes → communicated back by “Future You”

The purpose is to make financial planning personal, understandable, emotionally relevant and engaging for a younger audience. It moves financial advice away from abstract future outcomes by turning those outcomes into an interaction with a representation of the user’s own future self.

Strategic fit

The concept is intended to solve two problems simultaneously: make $RUs more relevant to a younger, digitally oriented audience, and preserve its existing positioning around quality, personalisation and trust.

💡

The proposed transformation is not intended to replace $RUs’ trusted identity with a generic digital offering.

The strategic objective is: traditional advice → fully automated digital service.

29 / Proposed scope

Three features, and one that stretches the boundary

Future You simulator

Headline features: user selfie, AI-generated ageing, financial projections, real-time interaction. The user’s digital guidance is based on projected income, lifestyle and financial decisions.

💡

CX logic: rather than telling a user that saving more today may improve retirement outcomes, the system allows the user to experience that consequence through a personalised representation of their future.

AI-powered financial assistant

The conversational AI is intended to explain complex financial concepts in plain language, contextualise guidance and provide access at any time.

Complex financial information → conversational explanation → lower engagement barrier

Estate and inheritance planning

Tools addressing inheritance, property, superannuation and intergenerational wealth transfer. The strategic logic is that younger clients may increasingly need to prepare for wealth transfers from older generations — but this feature also materially expands the proposed product scope beyond the core Future You concept.

❓

Scope question: is inheritance planning a Must have for validating Future You, or a later-stage extension once the core proposition has been proven?

30 / Why it may work

Technology is not the primary value

Murray’s enthusiasm is explicitly not based on using AI for its own sake. The intended mechanism runs through engagement to a relationship.

This aligns with the project scoping principle that a medium or technology itself is not the solution. The Future You concept attempts to solve a common weakness of financial planning: the benefits of today’s decisions may feel distance and abstract.

information about the future → an experience of the future

By representing those consequences through the customer’s own future self, StellarCX seeks to make them immediate, relatable and emotionally meaningful. That is the central CX proposition.

31 / Engagement layer

From digital product to customer relationship

The proposed app should not be understood as a static digital brochure. Its intended role is a smart financial companion capable of creating repeated interactions with $RUs.

Initial engagement → repeated value → trust → deeper advisory relationship
💡

The project has potential strategic value even before a user becomes a conventional financial-advice client.

CX objective

The app’s success should not be judged only on whether its functionality works. Relevant customer outcomes include:

  • Whether users understand financial concepts btter
  • Whether they trust the experience
  • Whether the interaction motivates meaningful financial behaviour
  • Whether engagement develops into a stronger relationship with $RUs

These behavioural outcomes are more important than novelty alone.

32 / Brand

Repositioning, and what earned media is not

Future You could reposition $RUs from a boutique heritage financial adviser toward a more digitally progressive brand without abandoning its trust-based positioning. Potential brand message: established expertise + contemporary digital capability.

A distinctive Future You experience could attract fintech coverage, podcasts, influencers and online communities such as Reddit.

💡

However, earned media is not itself evidence of project success.

Distinctive idea → attention → engagement → changed perception/behaviour

A viral app that fails to generate trusted financial relationships would not achieve the underlying strategic objective.

33 / Tensions

Three trade-offs the scope has to hold

Innovation vs trust

$RUs must introduce a highly innovative AI experience without damaging the trust on which its existing brand depends.

Too conservative

Little differentiation; weak attraction for digitally progressive customers.

Too aggressive

Greater privacy, ethical and reputational exposure; possible erosion of confidence in the brand.

The project's scope must therefore balance novelty + credibility.

Personalisation vs privacy

The product concept potentially relies on highly personal information: photographs, financial data, projected financial outcomes and lifestyle assumptions.

More personal data → potentially richer CX → greater privacy and trust exposure

Greater personalisation may increase relevance, but it also increases the sensitivity of the customer relationship. Privacy therefore cannot be treated as a technical issue added after the experience has been designed.

AI guidance vs compliance

The concept includes AI-generated financial guidance, creating an important boundary question: what can Future You explain, suggest or recommend without creating unacceptable compliance risk?

The source material flags regulation, privacy, ethics and financial guidance through AI as unresolved considerations. The project team must determine the acceptable boundary between education, contextual guidance and personalised financial advice. The material does not specify the final regulatory solution.

34 / Stakeholders

Competing perspectives on what the product may become

Future You cannot be scoped solely around the desired user experience, because several stakeholders shape what the product can legitimately become.

Stakeholder Likely priority
UsersPersonalisation, usefulness, simplicity, trust
$RUs / MurrayGrowth, younger-client acquisition, brand transformation
MarketersDifferentiation, engagement, earned media
Developers / StellarCXFeasibility, scalability, secure implementation
RegulatorsAppropriate financial guidance, privacy, compliance

The final product will therefore emerge from trade-offs between desirable CX, strategic ambition and acceptable risk.

💡

Scope implication: not every desirable feature should automatically enter the final scope simply because it improves engagement.

35 / Scoping it

Define around the outcome, protect the killer idea

Weak project definition

“Build an AI financial app.”

Stronger framing

Create a trusted, engaging digital experience that helps $RUs establish meaningful relationships with younger prospective clients.

This keeps the project focused on the underlying business problem rather than the technology selected to solve it.

Protect the killer idea

The Future You interaction is the creative element that differentiates the proposition, so the scope should protect the mechanism that makes it compelling:

  • Personal future-self representation
  • Meaningful financial projections
  • Understandable conversation
  • Connection between current behaviour and future consequences

Additional features should be tested against whether they strengthen that mechanism or simply enlarge the product.

The MVP question

❓

Will interacting with a personalised future self materially increase younger users' engagement with financial planning and $RUs?

Potential later-stage functionality should not distract from validating that proposition. The case creates a natural tension between launching an ambitious platform and preserving a focused, testable initial scope.

36 / Success

What success should ultimately mean

Future You should not be judged purely by app completion, feature count, downloads or social-media attention. The project's underlying logic suggests success should relate to whether it helps $RUs:

Reach younger clients → engage them meaningfully → build trust → influence financial behaviour → establish longer-term relationships

This gives the project a strategic outcome against which scope decisions can be assessed.

  • Future You is not fundamentally an AI project; it is a client-acquisition and relationship strategy enabled by AI. That distinction should govern every scope decision
  • The strongest feature is also the source of much of the project's risk: greater personalisation can increase engagement while simultaneously increasing privacy, ethical, compliance and trust exposure
  • The key scoping question is whether additional features strengthen the core behavioural proposition or merely make the app larger. Estate planning, for example, may be strategically useful without necessarily belonging in the initial MVP
  • Managers should resist treating publicity or technical novelty as success. The critical test is whether younger customers trust the experience, engage with financial planning and move toward a durable relationship with $RUs
  • The final scope will inevitably be a stakeholder trade-off: the most exciting customer experience is not automatically the most compliant, technically feasible or strategically appropriate solution
