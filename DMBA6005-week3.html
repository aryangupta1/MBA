Ideation and prototyping

How design thinking opens a problem up and then narrows it back down, why a prototype is built early, kept rough, and used to learn rather than to impress — and what separates long-term strategic value from a quick win.

Pre-live session 73 key terms 98 flashcards
01 / The core idea

Design thinking as a problem-solving approach

Design thinking is a human-centred, iterative approach for understanding problems and developing solutions around genuine user needs rather than assumed requirements.

Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Iterate

Ideation within design thinking

Ideation is the divergent process of generating many possible solutions before deciding which ones deserve further development.

  • Prioritise possibility before perfection
  • Suspend judgement during initial idea generation
  • Challenge assumptions and obvious solutions
  • Encourage unconventional ideas because they can reveal new directions
  • Narrow ideas only after sufficient exploration
💡

Separate idea generation from idea evaluation. Judging ideas too early reduces the solution space.

02 / Two modes

Divergent and convergent thinking

A strong design process deliberately alternates between expansion and selection. Rather than beginning with a predetermined answer, we need to explore both the problem space and the solution space.

The two modes and the question each one asks:

Thinking mode Purpose Key question
Divergent Explore many problems, perspectives or solutions What else could be happening / possible?
Convergent Evaluate and prioritise the strongest options Which problems or solution should we pursue?
Problems: explore possible underlying problems → identify the problem worth solving Solutions: generate multiple responses → select candidates for prototyping/testing
💡

A project can meet scope, cost and schedule yet still fail if it solves the wrong problem.

03 / Framing

Start with the user, not the requested solution

A client’s requested feature should be initially treated as a hypothesis about the solution, not necessarily as the true problem.

Observe users → understand needs/behaviours → define problem → generate solutions → test

Useful techniques include:

  • Bringing customers directly into workshoips
  • Using customer stories or recorded experiences
  • Observing behaviour rather than relying only on stated preferences
  • Co-designing with users and staff
💡

Before investing heavily in delivery, test whether the proposed project solves something customers actually value.

04 / Evidence

Research should enable decisions

Design thinking requires research, but more research is not automatically better. The mistake is following the methodology so rigidly that the team collects information without converting it into useful business insight.

💡

Research effort should be proportionate to the problem: some situations only require a small number of participants, while others demand much larger evidence bases.

Research → synthesis → insight → decision

The design process is a tool for reaching better outcomes, not a checklist that must be followed mechanically.

05 / Who counts as a user

Designing beyond the “average” user

Designing only for the typical customer can unintentionally exclude important groups. Teams should deliberately consider edge users, including people with:

  • Accessibility requirements
  • Financial hardship or vulnerability
  • Limited digital access
  • Different levels of technological confidence

Designing with these users can:

  • Expose weaknesses invisible when studying typical customers
  • Make products more inclusive
  • Reduce downstream accessibility and service risks
  • Sometimes improve the experience for the broader customer base
💡

Design for variation in human needs, not an imaginary average customer.

06 / Representing users

Personas, and where user understanding comes from

Personas can help teams represent different user behaviours during design but their usefulness depends on how they are created.

The purpose is not demographic storytelling. It is identifying meaningful behavioural differences that should change the design. AI can accelerate persona creation, but it cannot replace validation with real users.

Empathy is necessary but insufficient

There are three complementary sources of user understanding.

Source Contribution
Empathy Ability to understand and advocate for another person’s perspective
Lived experience Direct experience of the situation being designed for
Lived expertise Broader knowledge gained by representing or working extensively with affected communities

Using all three reduces the risk of designers believing that simply imagining themselves in somebody else’s position gives them complete understanding.

07 / Where design sits

Multidisciplinary design

Human-centred design works best when it is integrated with other business capabilities rather than isolated as a “creative” activity.

A–E framework

The notes give five headings and no more — nothing is written under any of them:

  • A — Architecture & Technology
  • B — Business & Experience
  • C — Change & Capability
  • D — Data & AI
  • E — Execution

One relationship is worked through: business + experience.

  • Business provides commercial and organisational constraints
  • Design provides customer/user understanding
  • Neither should dominate the decision independently

Technology example

For a SaaS product, customer research may identify onboarding friction qualitatively, while product analytics reveal precisely where customers abandon the workflow.

💡

Human insight explains why; data helps establish where/how often.

08 / The three tests

Desirability, feasibility and viability

After divergent ideation, ideas need to survive three different tests.

Lens Core question Typical considerations
Desirability Do users want or benefit from it? Trust, usefulness, accessibility, experience
Feasibility Can we realistically deliver it? Technology, skills, data, integration, resources
Viability Should the organisation sustain it? Cost, revenue/value, scale, risk, strategy

10 / Takeaways

Analytical takeaways

  • Do not confuse the client’s requested solution with the underlying problem
  • Diverge before converging: explore both multiple problems and multiple solutions
  • Strong design combines human evidence, business requirements, technology and data
  • Personas require research validation, not imagination alone
  • Include edge users, not merely the average customer
  • Judge proposed innovations through desirability, feasibility and viability
  • For $RUs, the key issue is whether the digital human genuinely removes barriers to earlier financial engagement - not simply whether AI technology can be built.
11 / The core idea

Prototype to learn, not to prove

A prototype is an intentionally incomplete representation of an idea used to make it tangible enough to test, discuss and improve before committing to full development. The objective is not to make the first idea look impressive. it is to learn quickly and cheaply.

Prototyping helps teams:

  • Turn abstract ideas into something observable
  • Test assumptions rather than rely on guesses
  • Expose weaknesses while they are still inexpensive to fix
  • Gather user and stakeholder feedback
  • Compare alternative solutions
  • Refine the idea through repeated iteration
  • Avoid substantial investment in the wrong solution
💡

Prototype to learn, not to prove that your first idea was right

12 / Fidelity

Why prototypes should initially be imperfect

Early prototypes should deliberately be quick, inexpensive and low-fidelity. The more effort invested in an idea, the harder it becomes psychologically and organisationally to abandon it.

A highly polished early prototype can create:

  • Attachment to the solution
  • Resistance to criticism
  • Pressure to continue because of time already invested
  • Reduced willingness to explore alternatives
Change → Discard → Rebuild → Compare

Keeping the prototype cheap protects the team from becoming committed to an idea before evidence shows that it deserves commitment.

Prototype fidelity should follow learning needs

Early prototypes might include:

  • Sketches
  • Paper interfaces
  • Storyboards
  • Wireframes
  • Simple models
  • Mock-ups
  • Clickable demonstrations

More detailed prototypes should emerge only when the team needs to test more specific aspects of the proposed solution.

13 / Agile design

Prototyping as Agile design

Prototyping and Agile project management share the same underlying logic.

Build something small → Test → Learn → Adapt → Repeat

Both reject the assumption that every requirement and solution can be correctly determined at the beginning of a project. The parallel holds line by line:

Agile Prototyping
Small delivery increments Small representations of the solution
Frequent feedback Frequent user testing
Adapt backlog/requirements Adapt design
Respond to change Revise assumptions
Continuous improvement Iterative refinement

This is why prototyping can be considered Agile design: it brings experimentation and adaptability into the design stage rather than waiting until development is substantially complete.

Why this matters

Early testing helps prevent teams from entering long development cycles before discovering that:

  • Customers do not understand the solution
  • Assumptions were incorrect
  • Important requirements were missed
  • The proposed feature is technically problematic
  • The solution does not address the underlying problem
💡

Build early and test often reduces the cost of learning

14 / From ideation to prototype

Diverge, converge, then make it tangible

Prototyping follows naturally from ideation. Teams initially generate a wide range of possible solutions without judging them too early; the objective is to expand the solution space and challenge obvious answers. Teams then evaluate ideas and narrow them to the most promising directions.

Selected ideas become prototypes that can be:

Made tangible → tested → refined → tested again

The process may then diverge again if testing reveals new possibilities.

💡

The purpose is not to perfectly execute an idea; it is to discover whether the team is doing the right thing before doing the thing right

What makes an idea work?

A successful idea must solve a real problem, not merely sound innovative. Teams should favour ideas that are:

  • Understandable to users
  • Useful in solving the identified need
  • Practical within project constraints
  • Capable of being tested and improved
  • Aligned with available budget and schedule

Avoid jargon

Complex terminology can disguise weak thinning.

❓

Who has the problem, what are we chanigng, and why will it improve their experience?

15 / Planning

Prototyping improves project planning

Prototypes provide evidence can improve planning before major commitments are made. Testing may expose:

  • Misunderstood requirements
  • Unnecessary features
  • Technical challenges
  • User experience problems
  • Dependencies
  • Scope changes

This gives project managers better information for developing:

Planning area What the evidence informs
Scope What actually needs to be built
Schedule How complex delivery is likely to be
Resources Which capabilities are required
Risk assessments Where uncertainty remains

Prototyping is not simply a design activity; it can improve the quality of project estimates and decisions.

16 / Stakeholders

Prototyping and stakeholder buy-in

Abstract concepts are difficult for stakeholders to evaluate because different people may imagine different solutions from the same description. A prototype creates a shared reference point.

The prototype communicates “Here is what we mean”, so stakeholders can then:

  • See or experience the proposed solution
  • Identify misunderstandings
  • Provide specific feedback
  • Compare alternatives
  • Participate in refining the design

Why this improves engagement

Early involvement creates co-ownership of the solution. Rather than stakeholders seeing a finished product for the first time near delivery, they participate in shaping it throughout development. This can:

  • Strengthen trust
  • Improve communication
  • Increase support for the project
  • Reduce disruptive late-stage changes
17 / T-Design

Breadth across the solution, depth where it matters

T-design balances breadth and depth when determining what should be prototypes. Think of the prototype as the shape of a T.

Horizontal line — breadth

The horizontal part represents the range of features or overall experience. Each feature is represented only enough for users and stakeholders to understand what exists, how elements relate, and what the overall solution might do. There is little depth because these components do not all require detailed testing.

Vertical line — depth

The vertical part represents a small number of important features explored in much greater detail. Go deeper where something is:

  • Innovative
  • Uncertain
  • Complex
  • Difficult for users to imagine
  • Central to the value proposition
  • Particularly important to validate

Resources are concentrated on areas where learning is most valuable.

Why T-Design is efficient

T-Design avoids two extremes:

Approach Problem
Prototype everything deeply Wastes time and resources
Prototype everything superficially Fails to properly test critical assumptions
T-Design Broad overall picture + deep testing where uncertainty matters
💡

Prototype what needs to be learned, not everything that could eventually be built

There is little value spending significant effort prototyping familiar functionality that stakeholders can already understand.

When T-Design is useful

T-design is particularly valuable for:

  • Early-stage prototypes
  • Stakeholder presentations
  • Pitches
  • User testing
  • Testing unfamiliar or innovative functionality
  • Demonstrating overall feasibility without building the full system

it aligns strongly with Agile because it encourages teams to show enough of the whole solution while investing deeply only where evidence is needed

18 / Risk and takeaways

Prototyping as risk reduction

Prototyping reduces uncertainty before uncertainty becomes expensive. The earlier a bad assumption is identified, the cheaper it usually is to correct.

Prototype → Feedback → Learning → Adjustment → Reduced uncertainty

Frequent testing also prevents the project from drifting too far from:

  • User expectations
  • Stakeholder requirements
  • Project objectives

The greatest risk is not producing an imperfect prototype.

💡

It is perfectly building something nobody needed

Analytical takeaways

  • Prototype to learn by doing rather than guessing.
  • Early prototypes should be intentionally cheap and imperfect so teams remain willing to change or discard them.
  • Prototyping applies Agile principles to design through short feedback and iteration cycles.
  • Ideation moves from divergent exploration → convergence → tangible testing.
  • A working idea solves a real problem within relevant user and project constraints.
  • Prototypes improve scope, scheduling, risk identification and stakeholder communication.
  • Stakeholders give better feedback when reacting to something tangible rather than an abstract proposal.
  • T-design = breadth across the solution + depth where uncertainty or innovation is greatest.
  • The purpose of prototyping is ultimately to determine whether the team is building the right thing before investing heavily in building it right.
19 / Why personas

What a persona is for

Personas are realistic representations of target users that help Agile teams make decisions around specific user needs rather than an abstract “user”. Agile provides flexibility and iteration; personas ensure that this flexibility remains directed toward customer value.

They help teams decide:

  • Which problems are worth solving
  • Which features matter most
  • How features should be prioritised
  • What user stories should focus on
  • How design and technical decisions affect real users
💡

Designing for “everyone” usually produces a generic solution for no one in particular

Personas therefore act as a continuing reference point throughout ideation, prototyping and Agile delivery. Everything that follows in this topic — the empathy map, the two persona types, the user story, the backlog — is an attempt to keep that reference point in front of the team while it makes those decisions.

20 / What makes it useful

What Makes a Useful Persona?

A persona should feel like a credible individual with motivations, constraints and context, rather than a demographic segment with a name attached. Three habits separate the one from the other.

Be Specific Rather Than Statistical

Avoid

Age: 20-40, professional, wants to save money

Prefer concrete information such as

“I’m 39, work part-time and organise my work around school pickups.”

Specificity helps teams reason about actual behaviours and design decisions. A range can be agreed to by everyone in the room without changing anything anyone does next.

Use first-person language

First-person descriptions make needs and frustrations easier to understand:

  • “I want…”
  • “I struggle with…”
  • “I worry that…”
  • “I usually…”

This shifts the persona from a database profile towards a human perspective.

Include Meaningful Context

Useful persona information can include:

  • Life stage
  • Occupation and circumstances
  • Goals
  • Frustrations and pain points
  • Behaviours
  • Attitudes
  • Motivations
  • Relevant constraints
  • An illustrative quote
💡

Only include information that could change a product, service or project decision

21 / The image

Visual Identity Matters

A persona should include an image that communicates something about the person’s context and identity, rather than an interchangeable corporate stock image.

For example, an image showing the person:

  • In their home environment
  • Interacting with technology
  • Caring for family
  • Performing an activity relevant to the service

Can provide useful design context

The objective is not decoration. It is to make the persona easier for the team to visualise and remember during decision making. That is the same standard the rest of the persona is held to: the image earns its place if it changes what the team pictures when it argues about a feature.

23 / Empathy maps

Empathy Maps

An empathy map helps teams understand a user’s experience by examining decisions such as:

Think → Feel → Say → Do

Rather than merely describing who someone is, empathy mapping investigates how they experience the problem. It can reveal:

  • Emotional frustrations
  • Hidden concerns
  • Motivations
  • Behavioural contradictions
  • Unmet needs
  • Contextual barriers

Relationship to personas

Empathy maps can provide richer inputs for persona development

Research → Empathy Map → Patterns/Insights → Persona

This prevents personas from becoming purely demographic profiles. For example, a financial services customer may say they intend to invest but consistently avoid acting because they feel uncertain or intimidated. The difference between stated intent and behaviour may become more important to the design than age or income alone.

24 / Type of Personas

Type of Personas

My notes carry this heading with nothing written under it. Everything sits under the sub-headings below.

Data-Driven Personas

Data-driven personas are derived from evidence such as:

  • Interviews
  • Surveys
  • Customer analytics
  • Observed behaviour
  • Usage patterns

They identify meaningful patterns among real or potential users

Value

They provide:

  • Evidence-based customer understanding
  • Defensible design decisions
  • Insight into real behaviours and market segments
  • Stronger foundations for product and service strategy

They prevent teams from designing around unsupported assumptions

Extreme Personas

Extreme personas intentionally represent unusually strong behaviours, constraints or edge cases. Their purpose is not necessarily to represent the average customer. Instead, they stretch the design team’s thinking. They can expose:

  • Accessibility issues
  • Unusual constraints
  • Hidden assumptions
  • Weaknesses in the service
  • Opportunities for more inclusive solutions

Example

For a digital financial service, an extreme persona might be someone who:

  • Has extremely low digital confidence
  • Refuses to disclose personal information online
  • Requires significant accessibility support

Designing with this person in mind may reveal improvements that also benefit mainstream customers.

Using Both Together

Persona type Primary purpose
Data-driven Keep design grounded in reality
Extreme Stretch thinking and reveal blind spots

The approaches are complementary:

💡

Data-driven personas establish what is happening → extreme personas test whether the design remains robust at the edges

25 / Into user stories

Personas and User Stories

Personas become particularly useful in Agile when they are translated into user stories. A user story describes a requirement from the user’s perspective:

As a [type of user], I want [goal], so that [value/outcome]
Written from the persona ✅

As a cautious investor approaching retirement, I want financial terminology explained simply so that I can make decisions without feeling overwhelmed.

The emphasis is on:

User→Need→Value

User Stories and Scope

User stories help define what should be delivered without prematurely prescribing exactly how the team must build it. This supports scope management because teams can:

  • Break larger requirements into manageable pieces
  • Prioritise based on user-value
  • Remove low-value functionality
  • Adapt requirements as feedback emerges
  • Connect features to an identifiable user outcome

A strong user story answers:

  1. Who needs something?
  2. What do they need?
  3. Why does it create value?

If the “so that” component has no meaningful answer, the proposed functionality may deserve reconsideration

26 / Into the backlog

User Stories in Scrum

User stories can form important work items within the Product Backlog

Persona/User Need → User Story → Product Backlog → Prioritisation → Sprint

The Product Owner prioritises work using considerations such as:

  • User need
  • Business value
  • Feedback
  • Dependencies
  • Project priorities

Selected stories can then move into a sprint and progress through workflow stages such as:

To Do → In Progress → Done

The backlog therefore remains dynamic, changing as the team learns more about users and the solution.

What User Stories are Not

A user story should remain a short expression of user need and value

It is not:

  • A detailed technical specification
  • A long requirements document
  • A disguised checklist of predetermined functionality
  • A vague instruction such as “improve performance”
  • Technical implementation language pretending to be user-focused
Not a user story ⛔

“Implement AI-Powered scalable API integration with CRM”

A user story ✅

“As a customer returning to the service, I want my previous conversation available so that I do not have to explain my situation again”

The technical solution can then be determined by the team.

27 / Across the cycle

Personas Across the Agile Decision Process

Personas should not be created once and then forgotten. They can inform the whole design and delivery cycle:

Persona → Ideation → Prototype → User Stories → Backlog → Testing → Learning

As new research and feedback emerge, personas may also need to be refined or challenged

This keeps Agile from becoming merely efficient delivery

💡

Speed is useful only if the team is moving forward toward the right user outcome

Analytical Takeaways

  • Personas replace the vague concept of the user with specific needs, behaviours and context
  • Useful personas contain actionable insight, not unnecessary demographic detail
  • Empathy maps deepen understanding of what users think, feel, say and do
  • Data-driven personas ground decisions in evidence; extreme personas expose blind spots and stimulate innovation
  • Personas help translate user understanding into prioritisation, features and user stories
  • A user story should clearly connect user→need→value, without prescribing unnecessary implementation detail
  • In scrum, user stories help turn customer needs into manageable, prioritised backlog items
  • The managerial test for a persona is simple: Does knowing this information change what we would design, prioritise or deliver?
28 / The core idea

Strategic value is not the same as a quick win

A project can produce impressive short-term metrics without creating meaningful strategic value. The two are measured on different timescales, and only one of them survives the end of the spending. The question this topic asks is which of the two a project should be judged by.

The key distinction is:

Short-term win Long-term strategic value
Immediate clicks, downloads, redemptions or sales Stronger brand, trust and customer relationships
Easy to measure Often slower and harder to measure
May improve a campaign metric Improves future competitive position
Can disappear when spending stops Can compound over time

Both columns describe real results. The difference is that one of them is finished when the campaign is, and the other is still working afterwards. A successful project should therefore be assessed not only by what it achieves now, but by whether it strengthens the organisation’s future position.

💡

A metric can look successful while the underlying strategy creates little or no additional value

29 / The wrong metric

Avoid optimising the wrong metric

The coupon example illustrates the danger of vanity metrics. If coupons are given to customers already intending to purchase, the campaign reports one result and the business receives another. Redemption may be extremely high and the campaign appears successful, but no new customer behaviour was created, and revenue may actually fall because discounts were given unnecessarily. The error is not the coupon itself; it is reading redemption as evidence that the coupon caused something.

💡

Measure incremental value, not just visible activity

The same trap is available to $RUs, whose most reportable numbers are all counts of activity. For $RUs, metrics such as:

  • Website visits
  • App downloads
  • Social impressions
  • Ad clicks

are not valuable by themselves

❓

Did this activity improve awareness, trust, engagement, conversion or long-term customer relationships

30 / The strategic context

Why trust sets the strategy for $RUs

$RUs wants its future projects to support broader business goals: improving market position, increasing profitability, strengthening brand image, and attracting younger clients earlier in their financial lives.

The strategic challenge is that financial services depend heavily on trust and reputation

Customers may be reluctant to share sensitive financial information, act on financial advice, or transfer significant wealth with an organisation they do not recognise or trust.

$RUs cannot rely only on immediate customer acquisition, it must build:

Awareness → Familiarity → Credibility → Trust → Relationship → Lifetime Value
31 / The deferred return

Recognition today, trust tomorrow

For younger customers, immediate financial-advice revenue may not be the most important outcome. The sequence below runs over several years, and none of its early steps produces a sale.

  1. Become aware of $RUs today
  2. Engage with useful content over several years
  3. Develop confidence in the brand
  4. Eventually seek advice when financial needs become more complex

This creates customer lifecycle value rather than a one-of transaction

❓

“How do we become the trusted financial partner they think of when they need help?”

Brand equity as a strategic asset

Brand equity is the value created by customer awareness, associations, trust and reputation. For $RUs, strong brand equity can reduce perceived risk for prospective clients, increase willingness to engage with advisers, differentiate the business from competitors, improve customer retention, support referrals and increase future customer acquisition efficiency.

Every one of those effects lowers the cost or raises the odds of a future transaction rather than producing one now. This means a project may create strategic value even if it does not immediately maximise sales.

💡

In trust-based industries, reputation can be an investment whose return appears later

32 / Growth as an outcome

Growth should be an outcome, not the only goal

Pursuing rapid growth directly can create poor incentives. If the project focuses only on customer numbers, downloads, traffic and immediate revenue, teams may compromise service quality, customer experience, trust and brand positioning — the same assets the strategy depends on. None of those four numbers is worthless. The risk is that they become the only ones the project is held to.

A stronger strategic approach is:

Deliver genuine customer value → strengthen trust and brand → deepen relationships → sustainable growth follows

Growth therefore becomes a consequence of doing the right things well, rather than the sole project objective.

Being a “servant of growth”

The idea of being a servant of growth means that leadership should support the conditions that create sustainable growth rather than chase growth metrics directly. For Chris, this means asking:

  • What does $RUs need to become for future customers?
  • What customer relationships should the project create?
  • What reputation should the brand build?
  • How will the project contribute to future competitive advantage?

The project. should therefore serve:

Customer value + brand trust + strategic positioning
33 / Murray’s idea

Murray’s initial ideas: growth or strategic value?

Murray’s interest in younger customers represents more than immediate growth

The strategic logic is that younger customers represent future wealth accumulation, investment needs, superannuation decisions, inheritance and intergenerational wealth transfer, tax and trust requirements, and long-term advisory relationships. Therefore, engaging them earlier can increase customer lifetime value

However, the strategic value depends on how $RUs engages them. If the project merely generates more traffic, it remains a growth tactic. If it builds familiarity, trust, useful relationships and positive brand associations, then it becomes a long-term strategic initiative

❓

How should this project change how younger customers perceive and interact with $RUs?

Product versus strategic positioning

The question shifts attention from internal output to external meaning. The project could strengthen $RUs position as:

  • A trusted source of financial help throughout different life stages

This positioning has greater long-term strategic potential than any individual campaign or digital feature

34 / The three channels

Google Ads vs App vs Social Media

None of these channels automatically creates long-term strategic value. They are tools, not strategies, and their value depends on what role they play in the broader strategy.

Option Strategic strength Main limitation
Google Ads Captures people already searching for financial help Often transactional; awareness may disappear when spending stops
App Can support ongoing customer interaction and service relationships Low value if customers have no strong reason to use it repeatedly
Social media Can build familiarity, education and brand personality over time Engagement metrics can become vanity metrics if not tied to meaningful outcomes

Google Ads

Google Ads are strongest for capturing existing demand. Someone who searches for “How should I invest an inheritance?” already has a need. Ads can therefore support:

  • Lead generation
  • Customer acquisition
  • Targeted campaigns

However, they may contribute less to long-term brand building if the relationship ends after the click

Strategic role: useful acquisition tool, but unlikely to be the complete long-term strategy.

App

An app has potential strategic value if it creates ongoing usefulness. For example, an app could support:

  • financial goal tracking;
  • educational content;
  • adviser interactions;
  • personalised financial journeys.

If customers only need financial advice occasionally, they may have little reason to download or repeatedly use another app.

💡

An app is not a strategy simply because it is digital.

What recurring customer problem would make the app worth returning to?

35 / The joined-up answer

Social media, and which approach best supports long-term goals

Social media may have the strongest direct connection to the challenge of building relationships with younger prospective customers. It can support:

  • ongoing financial education;
  • brand familiarity;
  • repeated exposure;
  • accessible explanations of complex topics;
  • development of trust before customers need formal advice.

The strategic value is therefore less about immediate conversion and more about:

awareness → familiarity → credibility → future consideration

However, success should not simply be measured through likes, views or followers. The important question is whether social engagement ultimately strengthens brand trust and meaningful customer relationships.

Which approach best supports long-term goals?

The strongest answer is unlikely to be Google Ads or App Or Social Media in isolation. Each can play a different strategic role:

Social media → build awareness and familiarity Google ads → capture customers when needs emerge Digital service/app → deepen the relationship once engagement begins
💡

Strategic value comes from how the channels work together to move customers from awareness toward trust and an ongoing relationship

Measuring long-term strategic value

Measuring that journey takes more than the metrics each channel reports about itself. Project success should include both short-term performance indicators and longer-term strategic outcomes

Useful measures could include:

  • Brand awareness
  • Trust and reputation
  • Quality of customer engagement
  • Repeat interaction
  • Adviser enquiries
  • Customer conversion over time
  • Retention
  • Referrals
  • Customer lifetime value

Measure outcomes that reflect strategic intent, not merely metrics that are easy to collect

36 / Takeaways

Analytical takeaways

  • Shorty-term success does not automatically equal strategic value
  • Avoid vanity metrics that show activity without demonstrating incremental value
  • Financial services depend heavily on trust, familiarity and reputation
  • For $RUs, engaging younger customers should focus on lifetime relationships, not immediate transactions.
  • Brand equity can create future competitive advantage even when returns are not immediate.
  • Growth should often be the result of creating customer value, not the sole target.
  • Google Ads, apps and social media are tools, not strategies.
  • The strongest approach links channels across the customer journey and supports long-term relationship building.
  • Chris should judge the project by one central question: Will this initiative make $RUs more trusted, relevant and valuable to future customers over time?
