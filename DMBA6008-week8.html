01 / The core idea

What growth is the market pricing in?

A company’s current share price can be used to infer the earnings growth rate the market is pricing in. Using the Gordon growth framework:

P0 = EPS0(1 + g) / (Re − g)
Variable Meaning
P0Current share price
EPS0Current earnings per share
gImplied perpetual earnings growth rate
ReRequired return on equity / equity hurdle rate

Rearranging to solve for growth:

g = (P0Re − EPS0) / (P0 + EPS0)
02 / Interpretation and a worked example

An expectations measure, not a forecast

  • In an actively traded market, P0 reflects investors’ collective expectations about the company’s future earnings
  • Implied growth provides a quick benchmark for assessing how optimistic or pessimistic the market’s expectations are
  • It is an expectations measure rather than a forecast of what growth will actually occur
Worked example — given
P0
$30
Re
10%
EPS0
$2.039
g = ((30 × 0.10) − 2.039) / (30 + 2.039) g = 0.961 / 32.039 ≈ 3%
💡

A $30 share price implies approximately 3% annual earnings growth under the model’s assumptions

03 / Share price and growth expectations

News moves expected growth, and the price follows

Positive news about a company’s future opportunities can increase expected growth, so investors may be willing to pay a higher share price. The increase can be interpreted as the market pricing in stronger future growth expectations.

Negative information about the company or its markets can lower expected growth, and selling pressure can reduce the share price. A falling share price therefore signals weaker market expectations, holding the other model inputs constant.

04 / Using implied growth

A back-of-the-envelope expectations test

❓

What future growth must investors already be expecting to justify today’s share price?

This shifts analysis from company performance alone to the relationship between performance and expectations. A high share price is therefore not automatically attractive: it may already incorporate substantial future growth expectations.

  • Share prices embed expectations: implied growth extracts the earnings growth assumption consistent with the current market price
  • The key analytical question is not simply whether a company will grow, but whether actual growth can meet or exceed the growth already priced into the shares
  • Changes in share price can reflect changes in expected growth, although the calculation also depends on the estimated equity hurdle rate
  • Implied growth provides a useful benchmark for challenging whether market expectations appear realistic when analysing a company’s valuation
05 / Growth does not automatically create value

Size is not the same as returns

Management often treats growth — higher revenue, market share or expansion into new markets — as inherently desirable. But growth only creates shareholder value when the returns generated justify the additional assets, financing and risk required to support it.

The alternative is to use retained earnings to improve returns from existing assets rather than expanding the asset base.

❓

Should the business grow, or should it extract more value from what it already owns?

06 / When growth can destroy value

Perplexed Limited: faster growth, lower price

Perplexed Limited begins with a share price of $10 and revenue growth of 5% p.a. Increasing revenue growth to 10% p.a. causes the modelled share price to fall: $10 → $7.60.

  • The business does not generate enough retained earnings to finance the assets required for faster growth
  • Debt increases significantly, with the debt-to-equity ratio rising from 61.9% to 125.3% across the forecast period
  • Higher leverage increases levered beta, so shareholders demand a higher equity hurdle rate, which reduces the present value of future shareholder cash flows

This links back to the sustainable growth rate: growth beyond what internally generated funds can support may require increasingly risky external financing.

💡

Growth can destroy value when the financing and risk required to support it outweigh the benefits of additional revenue

07 / Asset utilisation as an alternative

Getting more from existing assets

Instead of accelerating revenue growth, management can improve asset utilisation:

Asset utilisation = Revenue / Net non-current assets

Raising Perplexed Limited’s asset-utilisation ratio from 0.95 to 1.05 means the company can generate the same level of revenue with fewer assets, or more revenue from the existing asset base. After the improvement, the share price moves from $10 to $17.91.

Rather than using retained earnings simply to expand, management may create substantially more value by improving the productivity and efficiency of existing assets. Innovation can be aimed at extracting more from the current asset base rather than merely expanding the business.

08 / The growth versus value decision

Two uses of retained earnings

Growth strategy Asset-utilisation strategy
Expands revenue and asset baseExtracts more from existing assets
May require substantial new financingReduces additional capital requirements
Can increase leverage and financial riskCan improve returns without equivalent balance-sheet expansion
Creates value only if incremental returns justify the cost and riskCreates value through greater efficiency and productivity
💡

Growth is an objective about size; value creation is an objective about returns relative to the resources and risk required

  • More growth is not necessarily more value; the financing requirement behind growth matters just as much as the revenue increase itself
  • Management should assess whether growth can be financed sustainably before pursuing higher revenue targets
  • Rising leverage can offset the benefits of growth by increasing shareholder risk and the required return on equity
  • When assets are under-utilised, improving asset productivity may create substantially more shareholder value than expansion
❓

Which use of retained earnings creates the greatest value?

09 / Acquisition through an NPV lens

Benefits against the cost of obtaining them

A corporate acquisition creates value for the acquiring company’s shareholders when its net present value is positive:

NPVacquisition > 0

Value is created when the present value of the acquisition’s benefits exceeds the additional costs required to complete and integrate the deal.

NPV acquisition = PV(Synergies) + PV(Control Benefits) - Acquisition Premium - PV(Integration costs) - Transaction Costs
❓

The framework separates why the acquisition creates value from what must be paid to obtain that value

10 / Synergies

Benefits available only because the companies are combined

Synergies are benefits available only because the two companies are combined: the value of the combined firms is greater than the value of the firms operating separately. Potential sources include:

  • Cost reductions from eliminating duplicated operations
  • Better utilisation of underused assets
  • Increased revenues through access to new customers or markets
  • Combining complementary capabilities or resources

The relevant measure is the present value of the incremental benefits generated by combining the businesses.

💡

Synergy is not simply the total performance of the acquired company; it is the additional benefit created by the combination

11 / Control benefits

Managing the target more effectively

Control benefits arise when the acquirer believes the target could be managed more effectively. Unlike synergies, they do not necessarily depend on combining overlapping operations. Potential changes include replacing or restructuring management, removing poorly performing products or business units, changing strategic direction, digitising or restructuring operations, and providing capital to pursue better growth opportunities.

Synergy benefits Control benefits
Come from combining two businessesCome from changing how the target is managed
Depend on interaction between acquirer and targetCan arise from improving the target itself
E.g. shared systems, lower duplicated costsE.g. restructuring, digitisation, management change

An acquisition may generate both, or primarily one.

12 / The acquisition premium

Paying above standalone value for control

The target’s existing shareholders normally require more than its standalone market value to surrender control. The amount paid above the target’s fair market value is the acquisition premium:

Acquisition Premium = Purchase Price - Standalone Fair Value
Example
Fair value
$100m
Purchase price
$120m

Premium = $20m

The acquisition must generate sufficient benefits to justify this additional outlay. Acquisition premiums can vary significantly, potentially ranging from around 10% to 50% of the purchase price depending on the deal.

13 / Integration and transaction costs

Combining the businesses, and doing the deal

Integration costs arise from making the two businesses operate effectively together: migrating onto common systems or platforms, closing facilities, operational restructuring, redundancies, and combining processes and infrastructure. Because these costs may occur after the acquisition date, PV(Integration Costs) is used in the NPV calculation. Integration is particularly important because expected synergies may not materialise until the businesses have actually been combined.

Transaction costs are separate because they relate to executing the acquisition rather than operating the combined business: investment banking fees, legal fees, deal advisory costs, and financing and transaction-arrangement costs.

Cost Purpose
Acquisition premiumConvince target shareholders to sell
Integration costsCombine the businesses after acquisition
Transaction costsStructure, negotiate and execute the deal
14 / Acquisition value creation

A strategically attractive target is not automatically a good acquisition

The economic logic for the acquisition to create value:

PV(Synergies+Control Benefits) > Premium + Integration + Transaction Costs

The price paid and the cost of realising the expected benefits determine whether value is actually created.

❓

Can synergies and control benefits exceed the premium and implementation costs required to obtain them?

  • An acquisition should be evaluated as an investment decision, with value creation determined by NPV rather than growth or deal size
  • Synergy and control should be assessed separately because they represent different strategic reasons for acquiring a company
  • Integration is central to acquisition economics: projected synergies have little value if management cannot successfully combine the businesses
  • A good company can still be a poor acquisition if the acquirer pays too much for the expected benefits
15 / Acquisition as an investment decision

Build it, or buy it

A company seeking growth can either:

  • Grow organically — build capability and enter new markets itself
  • Acquire another company — pay more upfront, but gain immediate access to an established business and its performance history

An acquisition should be assessed using incremental cash flows, and it creates value when:

NPVacquisition > 0
❓

What cash flows change, and how does risk change, if the acquisition proceeds?

Assumptions in this example

  • Value comes entirely from synergies; there are no control benefits
  • Synergies can be delayed, as integration needs to occur before the combined businesses can realise them
  • All provided figures are assumed to already incorporate tax effects
16 / The cash-flow timeline

Costs upfront, benefits later

Period Cash flow Reason

Year 3+$50mSynergies

💡

The key issue is timing: costs occur largely upfront, while acquisition benefits arrive later

17 / Terminal value

The synergy as a perpetuity from Year 5

From Year 5 onwards, the $50m annual synergy is assumed to continue indefinitely with no growth. Using the perpetuity growth formula:

TV5 = CF / (r − g) TV5 = 50 / (0.10 − 0) = $500m
  • The terminal value is measured at Year 5, so it must still be discounted back to today when calculating NPV
  • Zero growth simplifies the valuation to:
TV = CF / r
18 / Present value of the acquisition

Discounting at 10%: an NPV of about zero

Discounting the Year 2–5 synergies and the Year 5 terminal value at 10% gives PV(Synergies) ~= $455m. The $33m integration cost occurs at the end of Year 1: PV(Integration) = 33/1.10 = $30m.

NPV = PV(Synergies) - Premium - PV(Integration) - Transaction Costs NPV = 455-400-30-25 NPV ~= $0
19 / Interpreting a zero NPV acquisition

Acceptable, but not desirable

A zero NPV does not mean the acquisition earns nothing. It means the acquisition is expected to earn exactly the 10% required return demanded by investors.

NPV = 0 ⇒ Expected return = Required return
NPV Interpretation
> 0Creates shareholder value
= 0Earns the required return but creates no additional value
< 0Fails to earn the required return and destroys value

The deal is therefore acceptable, but not desirable: shareholders receive their minimum required return, but no additional value is created.

  • Acquisition analysis should focus on incremental cash flows, not simply the size or attractiveness of the target company
  • Delayed synergies matter because benefits realised later have a lower present value, while premiums and transaction costs are typically paid immediately
  • Integration is economically important twice: it creates a direct cost and can delay the synergies needed to justify the acquisition
  • A zero-NPV acquisition earns the hurdle rate but provides no economic surplus to the acquiring shareholders
  • The acquisition only becomes value-creating if synergies increase, arrive sooner, costs fall, the premium falls, or the required return changes favourably
20 / Choosing the appropriate hurdle rate

The acquisition’s risk, not the acquirer’s

The hurdle rate used to value an acquisition should reflect the risk and financing structure of the acquisition, not automatically the acquiring company’s existing risk. Two questions decide it:

❓

Is the target in the same industry or sector, with similar business risk?

Will the acquisition use the same debt/equity funding mix as the acquiring company?

💡

Use existing WACC when acquisition risk and financing mix are unchanged

If the target operates in a different sector, its business risk may be higher or lower, and that change in risk must be incorporated into the hurdle rate used in the NPV calculation.

21 / Cadence Ltd: existing WACC

Market-value weights, CAPM and the after-tax cost of debt

Financing Book value Market value WACC weight
Debt$100m$100m25%
Equity$100m$300m75%
Total$400m100%

WACC weights use market values, because they reflect current investor expectations and the current cost of raising capital. The cost of equity uses CAPM:

Re = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)
Rf = 4% MRP = 6.5% β = 1.2 Re = 4% + (1.2 × 6.5%) Re = 11.8%

The after-tax cost of debt, then the WACC:

Rd = 7% T = 30% Rd(1 − T) = 7%(1 − 0.30) = 4.9%
WACC = wdRd(1 − T) + weRe WACC = (0.25 × 4.9%) + (0.75 × 11.8%) WACC = 10.08%

This is appropriate for an acquisition only where the target has approximately the same operating risk, represented by the same beta, and the same market-value financing mix, 25% debt and 75% equity.

22 / Acquisition in a different industry

Healthcare buying hospitality

Suppose Cadence operates in healthcare but proposes acquiring a hospitality business:

  • Hospitality has a different exposure to economic conditions
  • Its business risk therefore differs from Cadence’s existing healthcare operations
  • Using Cadence’s 10.08% WACC would fail to price this different risk correctly
💡

The discount rate should match the risk of the cash flows being valued

Hospitality has an assumed unlevered beta of 1.4, which represents the sector’s underlying business risk before financing effects.

23 / Re-levering the target’s industry beta

Hospitality business risk, Cadence’s leverage

The hospitality beta of 1.4 represents business risk without debt. Cadence intends to finance the acquisition using its own 25% debt / 75% equity structure, so the sector beta must be re-levered to reflect the financial risk introduced by Cadence’s capital structure. Cadence’s debt-to-equity ratio is:

D/E = 100/300 = 33.3%

Using the levered beta relationship:

βL = βU[1 + (1 − T) D/E] βL = 1.4[1 + (1 − 0.30)(0.333)] βL ≈ 1.727

This combines hospitality business risk with Cadence’s intended financial leverage.

24 / Acquisition-specific WACC

From 10.08% to 12.65%

Using the acquisition-specific levered beta, the new cost of equity is:

Re = 4% + (1.727 × 6.5%) Re ≈ 15.23%

Shareholders now require substantially more than Cadence’s existing 11.8%, because the acquisition exposes them to greater risk. Assuming a 25% debt weight, a 75% equity weight and an unchanged 4.9% after-tax cost of debt:

WACCacquisition = (0.25 × 4.9%) + (0.75 × 15.23%) WACCacquisition ≈ 12.65%

💡

The acquisition hurdle rate is higher because the target operates in a higher-risk industry

25 / Debt cost may also change

Re and Rd may both need adjusting, not just the beta

The Cadence example holds debt constant at 7%, 4.9% after tax. Lenders may often demand a higher rate if:

  • The target operates in a riskier industry
  • Management lacks experience in that sector
  • Diversification introduces execution uncertainty
💡

An acquisition-specific hurdle rate may require adjustment to both Re and Rd, not just the beta

  • Do not automatically discount an acquisition at the acquirer’s existing WACC. The hurdle rate must reflect the risk of the acquisition itself
  • Existing WACC is appropriate only when the acquisition has broadly the same business risk and financing mix as the acquiring company
  • For a different industry, start with the sector’s unlevered beta, then re-lever it using the intended capital structure
  • Higher acquisition risk raises beta, the required return on equity and ultimately WACC, which reduces the present value of acquisition benefits
  • Match the discount rate to the risk of the cash flows being valued
26 / Three horizons of growth

Different horizons, different financial expectations

The Three Horizons of Growth framework separates investments according to how mature they are and what role they play in the firm’s future.

Horizon Role Strategic objective Typical financial profile
H1 — Current businessExisting core operationsScale and optimiseHigh cash flow, stronger profitability
H2 — Adjacent businessEmerging businesses using existing capabilitiesGrow and establish scaleLow profitability, limited cash generation
H3 — Future businessNew technologies, markets or business modelsExperiment and discoverHigh uncertainty, little/no current return
💡

Different horizons require different financial expectations and metrics.

⛔

Applying H1 measures to H3 opportunities can lead management to reject potentially valuable future businesses too early

27 / Horizon 1 — current business

Generate and optimise cash

H1 represents the firm’s existing customers, products and core business model. It takes most current management attention and generates the cash required to sustain operations, meet investor expectations and finance future growth. Investment is generally incremental: maintaining products, adding features and supporting the existing offering.

H1 should emphasise cash generation, profitability and margins, high or sustainable return on investment, efficient use of working capital and an appropriate financing structure. The business is mature enough to be assessed using conventional financial performance measures.

Metric What management is assessing
EBITDAOperating/cash-profit performance
Cash from operationsAbility of the core business to generate cash
ROIReturns generated from existing investments
Operating cash cycle / funding gapWorking-capital efficiency
Debt-to-equityWhether the business is financed efficiently
Interest coverCapacity to service debt from earnings
28 / Horizon 2 — adjacent and emerging businesses

Scale revenue efficiently

H2 covers opportunities where the firm already has relevant competencies, but which are not yet a major part of the business: entering an existing market in which it does not currently compete strongly, expanding the product range, or developing the next generation of its offering. H2 initiatives are intended to grow into future H1 businesses.

Revenue may already exist, but profitability is generally low, cash generation is limited and ROI is initially weak. Management has already invested in capabilities or assets and now needs to scale revenue efficiently. The immediate priority shifts from profitability toward revenue growth + capital efficiency.

H2 metric Why it is relevant
Net Present Value (NPV)Particularly useful, because current cash flows may be small or negative while much of the economic value lies in future periods
Internal Rate of Return (IRR)Useful for assessing the return generated by an individual investment, retaining the earlier limitation that IRR should not be relied upon for comparing competing projects
Asset utilisationWhether the business is generating sufficient activity from the assets already committed
💡

H2 requires management to look beyond current-period profitability and assess whether today’s investment can become an economically attractive scaled business

29 / Horizon 3 — future businesses

Learning whether an opportunity deserves more investment

H3 consists of opportunities that may eventually disrupt or replace the existing business model: entirely new markets, emerging technologies, fundamentally different business models, and startup investments and experimentation. The opportunity may still be poorly defined, and a high proportion of initiatives can fail.

Activity includes prototypes, customer testing, experimentation, corporate venture investment, and monitoring emerging technologies and startups. The objective is not immediate profitability but learning whether an opportunity deserves further investment.

Why traditional measures fall short

Measures such as ROI are often unsuitable because revenues may not exist, future cash flows are highly uncertain, investment may still be small, and the business model itself may still be evolving. Management instead evaluates whether the initiative is reaching milestones that justify the next stage of investment.

30 / Measuring H3

Evidence, optionality and scalability

Metric / tool Purpose
Prototype and testing milestonesTest whether the concept works and whether customers respond
Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)Cost of acquiring a customer
Lifetime Value (LTV)Long-term economic value generated by a customer
ChurnRate at which customers stop using or renewing the product
RunwayTime before available cash is exhausted
Total Addressable Market (TAM)Potential scale of the market

These measures focus more on evidence, optionality and scalability than on current accounting returns.

31 / Financial logic across the horizons

Learn, scale, optimise

The financial objective changes as an initiative matures.

32 / Real option value

The value of future choices

H3 businesses can create value even before they generate significant cash flows, because they provide the organisation with future choices. A company may pursue multiple experiments knowing that many will fail; successful experiments create the option to make a larger investment later.

💡

Part of the value of an innovative organisation comes from the portfolio of opportunities it retains, rather than only its current cash flows; real option value considers the value associated with the likelihood that these future opportunities will eventually be exercised

33 / Analytical takeaways

Match the metric to the maturity

  • Financial metrics should match the maturity of the business. H1 profitability measures are not necessarily appropriate for evaluating H2 or H3 investments
  • H1 should generate and optimise cash, H2 should scale economically, and H3 should create evidence and strategic options
  • Management must balance investment across all three horizons: excessive focus on H1 protects today’s profitability but may leave the company without a future growth engine
  • Early-stage opportunities should be evaluated through milestones, learning and optionality, rather than being rejected solely because ROI is poor
❓

Is the core creating cash? Can the emerging business scale? Is the future opportunity worth preserving and developing?
