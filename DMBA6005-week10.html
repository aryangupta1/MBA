DMBA 6005 · Agile Project Development · Week 10

QUALITY AND
PUTTING IT ALL TOGETHER

Two topics on keeping quality and coordination intact as delivery grows. Quality control and quality assurance treats quality as conformance to requirements and fitness for use: QA prevents defects in the process, QC detects them in the output, testing ends in a go/no-go decision, and PDCA — or Deming’s PDSA — turns evidence into improvement, with ISO 9000 and ITSM carrying it to scale. Scaling out and scaling up separates two kinds of growth: DevOps extends delivery into continuous operations, with a limited blast radius and deliberate technical debt, while SAFe coordinates many teams through Agile Release Trains, Program Increments and a Release Train Engineer.

01 / Defining quality

Quality is fitness for purpose, not luxury

Quality is the degree to which a product or service fulfils its requirements and is fit for its intended purpose. The ISO definition emphasises conformance to requirements, rather than expense, sophistication or luxury. Two complementary perspectives follow.

Perspective The question it asks
Conformance to requirements Does the deliverable meet its agreed specifications and standards?
Fitness for use Does it actually work as intended for the people using it?
💡

A product can meet its technical specifications yet provide a poor experience if those specifications fail to capture user needs.

02 / The triple constraint

Quality emerges from scope, cost and schedule

Quality emerges from balancing scope, cost and schedule, rather than being treated as an independent feature to add at the end.

Pressure on the constraint What it can do to quality
Increasing scope without adjusting time or resources Can compromise quality
Reducing costs May undermine quality if essential controls or capabilities are removed
Compressing schedules May create defects and expensive rework
💡

Managerial implication: when constraints conflict, the project manager should negotiate scope, resources or timelines rather than silently sacrifice required quality.

Quality is a shared responsibility across the project, not something delegated exclusively to a testing team.

03 / QA versus QC

Quality assurance prevents; quality control detects

Dimension Quality Assurance (QA) Quality Control (QC)
Focus Process-oriented Product/output-oriented
Objective Prevent defects Detect defects
Primary question Are the processes capable of producing the required quality? Does the deliverable satisfy requirements?
Methods Standards, training, process reviews, audits Testing, inspection, validation
Timing Throughout the project At relevant stages throughout delivery
Outcome Consistent and reliable processes Accepted outputs or identified defects
💡

QA builds quality into how work is performed; QC verifies the quality of what is produced. Neither is sufficient independently.

04 / The cost of rework

Rework fixes the output, not the process

When QC identifies a defect, the immediate response is to correct and retest the deliverable. However, repeated rework indicates that the underlying process may be defective. The loop matters financially because rework consumes additional labour, delays delivery and increases project costs without necessarily generating additional customer value.

💡

Managerial implication: fixing a defect addresses the immediate problem; correcting its root cause improves future project performance.

05 / Quality planning

Planning quality before delivery begins

Quality planning establishes appropriate requirements, standards and verification approaches before significant delivery work begins. Key elements include:

  • Defining measurable acceptance criteria.
  • Selecting tools and methods capable of meeting requirements.
  • Establishing relevant external or regulatory standards.
  • Training project participants.
  • Planning testing and quality reviews.
  • Benchmarking comparable products or industry best practice.

Benchmarking helps establish realistic performance expectations and identify requirements that may otherwise be overlooked.

06 / Testing and acceptance

Testing at every level, ending in go or no-go

The lecture emphasises testing at multiple levels. Quality control should then produce a clear go/no-go decision against predefined acceptance criteria. Testing should occur iteratively rather than being concentrated at the end of delivery.

07 / Test-driven development

Tests first, then scope discipline

The lecture connects quality management with writing tests before implementation. The underlying logic runs:

  1. Define expected behaviour
  2. Implement
  3. Test
  4. Refine until requirements are met

Defining tests early clarifies what success means and reduces the likelihood of unnecessary development. Once agreed requirements are satisfied, additional functionality should return to the prioritisation process rather than being added informally.

💡

Key principle: quality is not maximising functionality. It is delivering the required functionality reliably without unnecessary complexity, cost or scope expansion.

08 / ISO standards

The ISO 9000 family

The ISO 9000 family provides an internationally recognised foundation for quality management systems (QMS). The lecture highlights its emphasis on:

  • consistent processes and documented requirements;
  • continuous monitoring and improvement;
  • customer satisfaction;
  • risk-based thinking;
  • performance rather than unnecessarily prescriptive procedures.

The 2015 revision discussed in the lecture reflects a movement towards greater emphasis on performance and risk-based quality management. Standards can also have commercial value: demonstrating conformity may support procurement eligibility, stakeholder confidence and regulatory compliance.

Terminology clarification: ISO 9000 describes quality-management fundamentals and vocabulary; ISO 9001 specifies certifiable QMS requirements.

09 / PDCA and PDSA

Plan–Do–Check–Act, and Deming’s Study

The Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) cycle provides a structured approach to improving organisational processes. The cycle repeats, allowing quality to improve as evidence accumulates.

Stage Purpose Management activity
Plan Identify problems, causes and improvement objectives Analyse recurring defects and establish a corrective approach
Do Implement the proposed improvement Trial changes to the process
Check Evaluate results against objectives Measure whether the change improved quality
Act Standardise or refine the improvement Update procedures, training and controls

W. Edwards Deming favoured Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA), emphasising that evaluation requires more than a simple pass/fail check.

💡

Key distinction: checking confirms whether a requirement was met; studying seeks to understand the results and learn from them. This shifts quality management from compliance alone towards organisational learning.

10 / ITSM and ITIL

Quality management at scale

ITSM (IT Service Management) is a structured approach to designing, delivering, operating and improving IT services in alignment with business and customer needs. It manages technology as an ongoing service, rather than simply delivering a technical product, and addresses service quality and consistency, operational roles and accountability, processes and service levels, ongoing performance measurement, and continuous service improvement.

ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library) is a widely adopted framework providing guidance for implementing ITSM. The lecture presents DevOps conceptually as a combination of three disciplines:

Discipline Contribution At organisational scale
Agile Iterative development and responsiveness Supports adaptive delivery
Lean Maximising value while reducing waste Improves efficiency and flow
ITSM Service governance, reliability and ongoing improvement Provides structured management of the resulting services

These approaches are complementary rather than mutually exclusive. ITSM and PDCA share a continuous-improvement philosophy: service requirements are established, processes implemented, performance evaluated and improvements incorporated into subsequent operations. That matters more once digital transformation moves beyond an individual project into long-term service delivery involving multiple teams, systems and stakeholders.

11 / Analytical takeaways

What quality management comes down to

  • Quality is fitness for purpose, not feature richness. Managers should assess whether deliverables satisfy agreed requirements and actual user needs, rather than equating additional functionality with higher quality.
  • Quality failures often reveal planning failures. Unrealistic scope, schedules or resource assumptions can create defects that downstream testing cannot economically correct.
  • QC provides evidence; QA converts it into improvement. The greatest long-term value comes from eliminating recurring causes of rework rather than repeatedly correcting individual defects.
  • PDCA turns quality into an organisational capability. Effective evaluation examines why results occurred, not merely whether a test passed.
  • Quality management must extend beyond delivery. At scale, ISO quality principles and ITSM frameworks help maintain consistent service performance, accountability and continuous improvement throughout the operating lifecycle.
12 / Two dimensions

Scaling out and scaling up solve different problems

As projects grow, Agile must extend beyond individual teams and fixed delivery timelines. It can grow in two directions.

Dimension Scaling out Scaling up
Objective Extend delivery into continuous operations and improvement Coordinate multiple Agile teams
Primary approach DevOps Scaled Agile frameworks (e.g. SAFe)
Main challenge Sustaining value beyond initial release Managing dependencies and strategic alignment
Success measure Continuous customer value and service reliability Integrated delivery across teams
💡

Scaling out changes the delivery lifecycle; scaling up changes the organisational coordination model.

13 / From projects to products

DevOps and continuous value delivery

Traditional project management assumes a defined beginning and end. However, digital products increasingly operate as continuously evolving services. DevOps extends iterative development into deployment, operations, monitoring and continuous improvement. The managerial shift is between two questions.

Mode The question it asks
Project delivery Has the team completed the agreed output?
Continuous value delivery Is the product consistently improving customer outcomes?

This requires three complementary organisational changes:

  1. Systems thinking Treat the product as an interconnected, evolving system rather than a collection of isolated deliverables.
  2. Amplified feedback loops Incorporate operational performance and customer feedback into ongoing development decisions.
  3. Cultural change Establish shared responsibility across development and operations, rather than treating deployment as a handover.
14 / Outcomes, not output

Measuring outcomes rather than output

Traditional delivery metrics emphasise completed tasks, features or milestones. DevOps places greater emphasis on value delivered and operational performance:

  • High release frequency is useful only if changes create meaningful value.
  • Faster delivery must not come at the expense of reliability.
  • Teams should be assessed on outcomes rather than activity alone.

The lecture cites research showing that elite performers achieved 208 times greater deployment frequency than lower-performing organisations, illustrating the potential impact of mature DevOps practices. This is a historical comparison from the lecture, not a universal performance target.

💡

Managerial implication: productivity is not simply how much the team produces, but how efficiently its work generates sustainable customer and business value.

15 / Limited blast radius

Designing for a limited blast radius

Limited blast radius means designing systems so that failures or changes in one component have minimal effects on the wider product. This requires modular components, stable interfaces between services, reduced interdependencies, isolation of failures, and independent deployment where feasible.

Example — Spotify

A recommendation component can be updated independently of the broader application, limiting the potential disruption caused by an unsuccessful change.

💡

Business implication: reducing the consequences of individual failures lowers the risk and cost of experimentation, enabling faster innovation.

16 / Technical debt

Technical debt and operational flexibility

The lecture’s Animal Logic example highlights the practical balance between delivery speed and engineering quality. Key principles include:

Principle Operational value
Consistent development and production environments Reduces deployment-related failures
Modular, adaptable systems Enables targeted changes without extensive rework
Runtime fixes Supports rapid response to operational issues
Temporary workarounds Protects urgent delivery when ideal solutions are impractical
Effective asset management Improves access to information and reduces wasted effort

Temporary fixes may be justified during critical delivery periods, but they create technical debt that requires future remediation.

💡

Managerial implication: technical debt can be a deliberate short-term trade-off, provided its future costs and remediation are understood rather than ignored.

17 / Scaling up

Coordinating multiple Agile teams

Scaling up addresses projects requiring many teams, longer planning horizons and more complex stakeholder relationships. A single Scrum team cannot simply expand to hundreds of people without creating significant coordination problems. Large-scale delivery therefore requires:

  • shared strategic objectives;
  • cross-team dependency management;
  • delegated decision-making authority;
  • integrated planning and delivery;
  • governance appropriate to organisational scale.
💡

The challenge is to introduce coordination without eliminating team autonomy.

18 / SAFe and the ART

SAFe and the Agile Release Train

The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) provides a structured approach to coordinating multiple Agile teams around shared strategic objectives. It builds on Agile, Lean and DevOps principles while introducing additional planning, coordination and governance mechanisms.

An Agile Release Train (ART) is a coordinated group of Agile teams delivering value towards a common objective. The lecture describes a typical ART as:

  • Approximately 5–12 Agile teams.
  • Roughly 50–135 people.
  • Operating under a shared vision and roadmap.
  • Synchronising delivery through common planning intervals.

Individual teams retain their own iterations and delivery responsibilities, while contributing to a larger integrated outcome.

19 / Program Increments

Program Increments: a longer planning horizon

A Program Increment (PI) is a longer planning and synchronisation period across an ART. The lecture describes PIs of approximately 6–10 weeks, compared with individual team sprints of roughly two weeks.

Team level ART level
Individual sprint planning Coordinated PI Planning
Team backlog Shared program-level backlog
Team-level delivery Integrated system delivery
Sprint reviews System demonstrations
Local blockers Cross-team dependencies

The longer planning horizon enables coordinated delivery and engagement with senior organisational stakeholders.

Example — one demonstration, many sprints

Ten teams may complete several individual sprints before presenting one integrated system demonstration to senior management.

20 / Release Train Engineer

The Release Train Engineer

The Release Train Engineer (RTE) is a servant leader responsible for facilitating coordination across the ART. The role is conceptually similar to a Scrum Master operating at a multi-team level, without replacing individual Scrum Masters. Key responsibilities:

  • Facilitate PI Planning and integrated system demonstrations.
  • Coordinate dependencies across teams.
  • Identify and remove delivery impediments.
  • Support communication between delivery teams and senior stakeholders.
  • Promote continuous improvement and alignment.

The RTE works alongside two important roles:

Role Primary responsibility
Product Manager Overall product direction, priorities and stakeholder value
System Architect Technical architecture, integration and system-wide dependencies
Release Train Engineer Coordination, delivery flow and organisational impediments
💡

Managerial implication: scaling Agile requires new coordination responsibilities, not simply more layers of command and control.

21 / SAFe or Scrum of Scrums

SAFe versus Scrum of Scrums

Both approaches coordinate multiple Agile teams, but differ in structure and governance.

Dimension SAFe Scrum of Scrums
Structure Formal, multi-level framework Lightweight coordination model
Coordination ARTs, PIs and defined roles Regular cross-team discussions
Governance Integrated strategic and delivery planning Primarily team-level coordination
Overhead Higher Lower
Suitable context Large, complex, interdependent initiatives Smaller or less complex multi-team environments

SAFe is more appropriate where extensive coordination, strategic alignment and formal governance are required. Scrum of Scrums provides an alternative where teams need regular coordination without the overhead of a comprehensive scaling framework.

💡

The correct approach depends on organisational complexity and interdependencies rather than the number of teams alone.

22 / Analytical takeaways

What scaling comes down to

  • Scaling out and scaling up solve different management problems. DevOps sustains value beyond initial delivery, while SAFe and similar frameworks coordinate delivery across larger organisations.
  • Continuous delivery changes performance management. Leaders should prioritise customer outcomes and operational reliability rather than treating release frequency or feature counts as measures of success.
  • Architecture determines delivery flexibility. Modular systems and limited blast radius reduce the cost and consequences of change, making frequent experimentation more viable.
  • Coordination must scale without destroying autonomy. Larger organisations need shared objectives, synchronised planning and clear responsibilities while preserving effective team-level decision-making.
  • Scaling frameworks impose organisational costs. Managers should select the lightest structure capable of handling actual dependencies, governance requirements and strategic complexity.
