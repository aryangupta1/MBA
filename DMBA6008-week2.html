Drivers of returns

Return comes from two places at once — how profitably a business trades, and how much capital it ties up to do it. Week 2 reads the asset mix for the business model, then takes return apart into the drivers behind it.

01 / The core idea

Asset structure as a business model signal

The combination of assets a company holds should broadly reflect how it creates and delivers value. Read that way, the balance sheet becomes useful for understanding the economic model behind the business, not just its financial position.

❓

Does the asset structure make sense given how the company operates?

02 / Putting firms on one scale

Common-size asset analysis

Common-size analysis expresses each asset category as a percentage of total assets.

Asset Category % = (Asset Category / Total Assets) × 100

Stating every category on the same scale allows comparison between companies of very different sizes, and helps identify differences driven by their business models and industries.

03 / Reading the categories

What different asset categories reveal

Each category carries a signal about the activity that sits behind it.

Asset category High proportion may indicate
Inventory Manufacturing, distribution or retail activities
Receivables Customers receive credit or payment occurs after service delivery
PPE Capital-intensive operations requiring physical infrastructure
Goodwill Significant historical acquisition activity and acquisition premiums
Other intangibles Reliance on software, intellectual property, licenses, product development or similar non-physical assets
💡

The value comes from interpreting these categories together, rather than judging any single number in isolation.

04 / Patterns

Business model patterns

What separates these patterns is not the size of the balance sheet but which categories carry the weight, and whether that weight sits in current or non-current assets.

Pattern Asset profile
Capital intensive Businesses requiring aircraft, buildings, factories or other major infrastructure will normally have a high proportion of PPE. Their balance sheets are therefore weighted toward non-current assets. Significant capital must be committed before revenue can be generated.
Digital platform Usually hold relatively little inventory and physical infrastructure. A larger proportion of assets may instead consist of software and other intangibles, and goodwill where growth has occurred through acquisitions.
Manufacturing & biopharma Manufacturing creates a need for both inventory, as products move through production and distribution, and PPE, because production requires facilities and equipment. Research-intensive industries can additionally hold significant intangible assets arising from development activity.
Retail Can hold relatively high current assets: substantial inventory available for sale, and receivables where customers are offered financing or credit. Their asset structure reflects both stock management and customer payment arrangements.
Technology example

A SaaS platform would normally be expected to have much less inventory and PPE than a hardware manufacturer.

05 / Strategy signals

Asset mix can reveal strategy

The balance sheet can provide clues about strategic choices.

Signal What it may indicate
High goodwill Growth may have relied heavily on acquisitions
High intangibles Value creation may depend on technology IP or development
High PPE Operations require substantial physical capital
High receivables Customer credit is an important part of the commercial model
High inventory Working capital management is strategically important
💡

Changes in asset composition over time can indicate that the business model or growth strategy is changing.

06 / Analytical takeaway

Judge the mix against the model, not a benchmark

A company’s asset mix should be assessed against its business model, industry and strategy, rather than against a universal benchmark.

Key questions

  • Does the asset structure logically match how the company earns revenue?
  • Which assets are essential to delivering the business model?
  • Is growth being created organically or through acquisitions?
  • Are unusually high inventory or receivables creating working capital risk?
  • Does an unexpected asset balance require further investigation?
  • The combination of assets should broadly reflect how a company creates and delivers value.
  • Common-size analysis puts companies of very different sizes on one scale.
  • Categories are read together, rather than judging any single number in isolation.
  • Capital-intensive balance sheets tilt to non-current assets; retail can tilt to current assets.
  • Digital platforms may hold their weight in software, other intangibles and goodwill.
  • A change in asset composition is itself a signal worth investigating.
💡

The balance sheet can act as a financial fingerprint of the business model — it shows where capital is committed and what resources management depends on to create value.

07 / The core idea

Return is driven by both profitability and capital use

Measures such as ROA and ROE combine two things at once: an income statement outcome — profitability — and a balance sheet base, the assets or equity used to generate that profit.

💡

Improving returns is not only about increasing profit, it also requires managing how much capital is tied up in the business.

08 / The driver chain

From market position through to return

Returns can be understood as a chain that runs from market position through to return. Each financial outcome along it has its own main drivers and its own management focus.

The core driver chain names those drivers and that focus, outcome by outcome.

Financial outcome Main drivers Management focus
Profitability Volume + Margin Product mix, pricing, demand
Asset Efficiency Asset utilisation Capacity and productive use of assets
Working Capital Inventory, receivables, payables Prevent growth from absorbing excessive cash
Capital Structure Debt/equity funding mix Finance assets appropriately
Sustained Returns Competitive advantage period Protect returns from competitive erosion
09 / Profitability

Volume × margin

Profitability fundamentally depends on volume — how much the business sells — and margin, the profit generated from each sale. Products can have different combinations of the two:

  • High volume / low margin
  • Low volume / high margin

Management therefore needs to decide which products, customers and markets deserve investment rather than simply maximising sales.

Technology example

Basic plan

High volume, with lower margins.

Enterprise contracts

Fewer customers, but larger margins.

A SaaS company may balance the two.

10 / Balance sheet drivers

Assets, working capital and funding

Asset utilisation

Asset Utilisation = Income / Assets

The issue is whether assets are being used hard enough to generate revenue.

  • Spare or under-utilised capacity reduces returns
  • Surplus assets may need to be sold, redeployed or restructured
❓

Are those assets producing enough income?

Working capital

Growth can increase requirements for:

  • Inventory
  • Receivables
  • Payables

Poor working capital management can cause a growing, profitable business to absorb sustainable cash. Management must therefore consider the cash requirement created by growth, not just the additional revenue.

Capital structure

Returns are also influenced by the funding mix between debt and equity. The appropriate mix depends on how the business chooses to finance the assets needed to operate and grow.

11 / External drivers

Financial outcomes reflect what is happening competitively

Key factors include:

  • Consumer demand
  • Competitive behaviour
  • Continued competitiveness
  • Efficiency of asset management
  • Funding decisions
💡

Financial ratios should be interpreted alongside the company’s market and strategic position.

12 / Competitive advantage period

How long can superior returns last?

The competitive advantage period is the period for which a company can maintain returns above its cost of capital before competitors catch up. A strong current return is therefore not enough.

❓

How long can this level of superior return realistically continue?

13 / Sustainable advantage

What holds the period open

Competitive advantage may be supported by:

Source of advantage What it provides
Superior brand Customers continue choosing or paying more for the offering
Core-category innovation The company remains ahead of competitors in its main market
Strategic partnerships Relationships provide capabilities or market access competitors cannot easily replicate

These advantages require continuous maintenance and reinvestment rather than being treated as permanent.

14 / Analytical takeaway

What to carry out of drivers of returns

  • A return measure combines an income statement outcome with a balance sheet base.
  • Returns improve by raising profit and by managing the capital tied up in the business.
  • Profitability depends on volume and margin, so products, customers and markets differ in what they deserve.
  • Spare or under-utilised capacity reduces returns; surplus assets may need to be sold, redeployed or restructured.
  • Growth creates a cash requirement, not only additional revenue.
  • The funding mix between debt and equity should suit the assets being financed.
  • Financial ratios should be read alongside the company’s market and strategic position.
  • Sustainable value creation depends on whether the company can preserve the competitive advantages supporting those returns.

Key management questions

  1. What is driving returns: margin, volume or asset efficiency?
  2. Are assets producing adequate revenue, or is there excessive capacity?
  3. Will growth create excessive working-capital requirements?
  4. Is the funding mix appropriate?
❓

How long can the company maintain superior returns before competitors erode them?

15 / The purpose

Why decompose a return at all

The DuPont model breaks return measures into their underlying drivers. That lets an analyst explain why ROA or ROE changed, rather than simply observing that they changed.

💡

An improving financial ratio is only meaningful once the driver of that improvement is understood.

16 / Return on assets

The two drivers behind ROA

The first DuPont relationship splits the return on assets into the two drivers that produce it.

ROA = Asset Utilisation x Profit Margin ROA = (Revenue / Assets) × (Profit / Revenue)

The second line is the same identity written out, and it shows why the decomposition holds: revenue cancels, leaving profit over assets.

Each driver carries its own management interpretation.

Driver What it measures Management interpretation
Asset utilisation Revenue generated per dollar of assets How effectively assets generate sales
Profit margin Profit generated per dollar of revenue Pricing power and/or control of expenses
ROA Profit generated from the asset base Overall operating efficiency of invested assets
17 / Return on equity

Adding financial leverage

The model then introduces financial leverage. Fully expanded, the return to shareholders carries a third term.

ROE = Asset Utilisation x Profit Margin x Financial Leverage ROE = ROA × (Assets / Equity)

The assets-to-equity ratio shows how much of the asset base exists for each dollar of shareholder equity. A higher ratio generally indicates that more assets are being financed through debt relative to equity.

18 / Reading a higher ROE

Higher ROE is not always better

An increase in ROE can come from very different sources.

Cause of higher ROE Interpretation
Higher asset utilisation Assets are generating more revenue
Higher profit margin Revenue is being converted into more profit
Higher leverage More debt is being used relative to equity

The first two represent improvements in operating performance. The third can increase shareholder returns mathematically, but it also introduces greater financial risk.

💡

ROE should never be interpreted without examining what caused it to change.

19 / Evaluating an acquisition

Why a bigger profit proves little

An acquisition will normally increase:

  • Assets
  • Revenue
  • Expenses
  • Potentially profit

Therefore, higher absolute profit after an acquisition is weak evidence of success.

❓

Has the management generated a better return from the additional capital invested?

DuPont analysis helps separate the acquisition’s effects into:

  1. Changes in asset productivity
  2. Changes in profitability
  3. Changes in financial leverage
20 / Offsetting drivers

One component improves as another weakens

A transaction may improve one component while weakening another.

Higher asset utilisation Lower profit margin
Assets generate more revenue Additional revenue carries proportionately higher expenses

These effects can offset each other, leaving ROA unchanged. This demonstrates why analysing only revenue growth, profit growth or one financial ratio can give an incomplete picture.

21 / The appearance of improvement

Leverage can look like better performance

Unchanged ROA + Higher Leverage → Higher ROE
  • Underlying operating returns have not improved
  • Shareholders appear to earn a higher return
  • Improvement comes from using more debt

Higher ROE may reflect greater risk rather than better management performance.

22 / Management and takeaway

Judging the source of a return

DuPont analysis is useful when assessing management bonuses or strategic performance. Management should not necessarily be rewarded simply because profit increased or ROE increased.

Analysts should determine whether the improvement came from:

  • Generating operating efficiency
  • Better margins
  • Productive deployment of new assets
  • Simply greater leverage
🚨

Management performance should be evaluated on the quality and source of returns, not just the headline return.

Analytical takeaway

The DuPont model turns a return ratio into a financial story.

ROE = Asset Efficiency x Profitability x Leverage

When evaluating a strategic decision, ask:

  • Did the investment improve asset utilisation?
  • Did margins improve or deteriorate?
  • Did ROA improve?
  • Is higher ROE driven by better operations or simply more debt?
  • Has management increased returns by creating value, or by increasing shareholder risk?
23 / The core idea

What sustainable growth means

The sustainable growth rate (SGR) is the maximum rate at which a business can grow while keeping its financial policies broadly unchanged, particularly its capital structure.

❓

How fast can we grow without requiring an unsustainable increase in debt or new equity?

24 / Why growth needs financing

Growth has to be paid for

Revenue growth often requires additional resources such as:

  • More working capital
  • Additional inventory and receivables
  • More non-current assets
  • Additional funding to finance those assets
Revenue Growth → More Assets → More Financing Required

Growth is only “free” when the business has enough unused capacity in its existing assets to support additional revenue.

25 / Core drivers

Return on assets, leverage and retention

The sustainable growth rate reflects three broad factors.

SGR is driven by Return on Assets, Leverage and Retention
Driver Why it matters
Return on assets Higher asset productivity generates more profit from the existing capital base
Equity-to-assets / leverage Determines how much growth can be financed without changing financial risk
Retention ratio Determines how much profit remains in the business to finance future growth
Retention Ratio = 1 - Dividend Payout Ratio

Higher dividend payouts therefore leave less internally generated funding available for growth.

26 / Above the rate

Growth above the sustainable rate

If management targets growth above the SGR while operational and financial drivers remain unchanged:

  • Required assets increase faster than retained equity
  • Retained earnings become insufficient to finance the growth
  • Additional debt becomes the balancing source of finance
  • The debt-to-equity ratio rises
Growth > SGR → Higher Leverage

This creates a potential warning sign because growth may be commercially achievable but financially unsustainable at the existing risk level.

27 / A constraint, not a forecast

Sustainable growth is constraint based

The SGR assumes important operating and financial relationships remain broadly unchanged, such as:

  • Expense margins
  • Working-capital requirements
  • Asset utilisation
  • Dividend policy
  • Target capital structure

If these remain constant, the SGR represents the growth rate that keeps the firm’s debt-to-equity relationship unchanged.

💡

So the measure is not a forecast of demand; it is a financing constraint on growth

28 / Management levers

Raising the sustainable growth rate

If management wants to grow faster without increasing leverage, it must change one or more underlying drivers. Possible responses include:

  • Increase profitability → generate more internal funds
  • Improve asset utilisation → generate more revenue without proportional asset growth
  • Reduce working-capital requirements → release cash
  • Retain more earnings → reduce the dividend payout
  • Raise new equity → increase the equity funding base
❓

What operational or financing change will make that growth possible?

29 / Asset capacity

Growth and asset capacity

A critical assumption is whether existing assets have spare capacity.

However, management should not overuse existing assets if doing so would:

  • Reduce service quality
  • Increase failures or maintenance
  • Create operational bottlenecks
30 / Strategic importance

Where strategy meets finance

The sustainable growth rate connects strategy with finance. Management may identify strong customer demand and attractive market opportunities, but demand alone does not determine feasible growth. A credible growth strategy must align:

Market Opportunity + Operational Capacity + Financing Capacity

Otherwise, aggressive growth can increase leverage and financial risk even when revenue and profits are increasing.

31 / Analytical takeaway

Testing a growth plan

❓

Can we finance and operationally support 20% more growth without changing our acceptable level of risk?

When analysing growth plans, ask:

  • Will growth require proportional investment in new assets?
  • Are existing assets already at capacity?
  • How much additional working capital will growth absorb?
  • Are retained earnings sufficient to finance expansion?
  • Will leverage rise beyond an acceptable level?
  • Could profitability, asset efficiency or retention be improved instead?
💡

Growth should be assessed against the business’s financial capacity to support it, not simply management ambition or market demand.

Q1 / Reading a high ROA

What does a high ROA tell us about a business model — and what might it conceal?

Return on assets is a fraction, so it can rise for two entirely different reasons: the numerator did better, or the denominator got smaller. Only the first is unambiguously good news.

ROA = Profit ÷ Assets

A genuinely high reading points to pricing power, tight cost control, or assets that are worked hard. But the identical reading appears when assets have simply left the balance sheet — rented rather than owned, operated by a franchisee, or built in someone else’s factory. It appears again when an ageing asset base is being run down rather than replaced, because accumulated depreciation shrinks the denominator each year while the plant keeps producing.

So the useful question is never how high the ratio is. It is which half of it moved, and why.

What shrank the asset base Benign reading Worrying reading
Assets leased, not owned Capital discipline flexibility without owning the infrastructure Hidden obligation the commitment is still there, only the asset is not
Production outsourced Focus capital concentrated where the advantage sits Dependence the capacity, and its risk, belongs to a supplier
Assets not replaced Timing a genuine pause in the replacement cycle Underinvestment today’s return borrowed from tomorrow’s capacity
Recognition or measurement Policy a defensible accounting choice Presentation the ratio moved while the business did not
🔑

A high ROA describes where the assets are as much as how well they are used — and assets can be efficient, or simply absent.

Industry example — Apple

Apple designs its products and has them assembled by contract manufacturers, so much of the plant that physically makes an iPhone sits on another company’s balance sheet rather than Apple’s.

The efficiency is real, but part of it is a decision about whose balance sheet carries the factory.

In class — the angle to take

Resist grading the level. Ask instead whether the asset base shrank because capital was released, or because capital was never committed — and who is holding the risk that came off the balance sheet.

Q2 / The speed limit

Can a company grow too quickly?

Yes — and the failure is a cash failure, not a profit failure. Growth is bought before it is paid for: inventory, wages and capacity go out the door first, and the customer pays later. Every additional dollar of sales therefore drags a slice of working capital and fixed assets behind it.

Cash generated internally grows roughly in step with profit and the share of it retained. Cash absorbed by growth rises faster than that. Past the point where the two lines cross, the shortfall must come from borrowing or new equity — and a company that keeps accelerating through that point can be profitable and out of cash at the same time.

The non-financial limits bind at the same moment: hiring, service quality and culture rarely scale as quickly as an order book.

🔑

Fast growth does not fail because it is unprofitable. It fails because the cash arrives after the spending, and the gap widens with every extra unit sold.

Industry example — Tesla’s Model 3 ramp

Tesla built assembly capacity and its supply chain ahead of the deliveries that would pay for them, and returned to capital markets repeatedly through the ramp. The binding constraint in that period was cash and production, not demand.

Order book full, balance sheet stretched — the classic shape of growing faster than internal funding allows.

Illustrative — not real company data

Assume, purely to see the mechanism, that every $100 of new sales needs $40 tied up in stock and receivables, and that customers pay two months later.

Doubling sales locks up $40 for every $100 of the increase before a cent of it returns. Both numbers here are invented.

Q3 / Same ratio, different path

How can two companies with similar ROA be on very different trajectories?

ROA is a product, not a level — margin multiplied by asset turnover. Two firms can land on the same number from opposite corners: one selling a little at a large mark-up, the other selling a great deal at a small one. The ratio is identical; almost nothing else is.

ROA = Profit Margin x Asset Turnover

The two models break differently, which is what makes the trajectories diverge. The high-margin firm is exposed to anything that dents desirability or pricing power. The high-turnover firm is exposed to anything that interrupts volume, because a thin margin gives it nothing to absorb the shock with.

ROA is also silent on how the asset base was funded. A firm expanding out of retained earnings and one expanding on new borrowing can post the same return while accumulating very different risk.

High margin, slow turnover Thin margin, fast turnover
Where the return comes from Each unit carries a lot of profit Each asset is turned over many times
What protects it Brand, scarcity, pricing power Scale, cost position, logistics
What breaks it Desirability discounting, once started, is hard to reverse Volume a small fall in throughput wipes out a thin margin
Growth needs Patience, and protection of the price Capital for capacity, and working capital to fill it
🔑

Equal ROA is not equal performance. Decompose it before comparing, then ask how each firm is paying for the assets underneath it.

Industry example — the two ends of one curve

Costco deliberately caps the mark-up it takes on merchandise and earns from moving enormous volume through its warehouses, supported by membership fees.

Hermès constrains the supply of its leather goods and sells through its own boutiques, earning a large margin on a small number of units.

Opposite corners of the same curve — and opposite answers to almost every strategic question that follows.

In class — the angle to take

Push the discussion past the decomposition to funding. Two firms on the same curve, one self-funding and one borrowing to expand, are not equally safe — and the ratio will not tell you which is which.

Q4 / An acceptable decline

When is it strategically acceptable to run a declining ROA?

When the denominator arrives before the numerator. Capacity, platforms and market entry are paid for up front and load slowly, so the asset base steps up in one year while the profit it supports appears over several. The ratio falls first and recovers later — a J-curve, not a failure.

That is only acceptable on two conditions. The new capital must be expected to earn more than it costs, and the dip must be caused by investment rather than by erosion. In the headline ratio those two look identical. Underneath, they do not.

Split the movement with DuPont. Assets added ahead of revenue show up as falling asset turnover with the margin roughly intact. Competitive erosion shows up in the margin. The first is a timing problem; the second is a business problem.

The diagnostic runs in a fixed order.

  1. Confirm the asset base actually grew — a falling ROA on a flat asset base is not an investment story at all.
  2. Check whether the margin held. If it fell, the problem is in the business, not in the timing.
  3. Ask whether the new capital is expected to earn more than it costs, and over what period.
  4. Set a date by which the return should have recovered, and hold management to it.
🔑

A declining ROA is defensible when it is the timing of a good investment. It is indefensible when it is the arithmetic of a deteriorating one — and only the drivers underneath tell you which.

Industry example — Amazon

Amazon ran thin retail margins for years while pushing cash into fulfilment capacity, and later into the data centres behind AWS. The infrastructure was built ahead of the volume it would eventually carry, so the asset base grew before the earnings it supported.

The asset base led; the return followed. That is the shape a J-curve is supposed to have.

In class — the angle to take

Concede the point quickly, then add the condition. A declining ROA buys nothing on its own; it is acceptable only where there is a stated return, a stated horizon, and a driver split that shows turnover — not margin — doing the falling.

Q5 / Capital allocation

How do capital allocation decisions shape sustainable growth and long-term returns?

Capital allocation is the standing decision about where each dollar of earned cash goes — back into the existing business, into an acquisition, against debt, or out to shareholders. It matters twice over. The share retained sets the ceiling on growth the firm can fund itself; the return earned on what is retained decides whether that growth is worth having.

SGR is driven by Return on Assets, Leverage and Retention

Retention alone is not a strategy. Earnings ploughed into projects that return less than the cost of capital still enlarge the asset base, so the reported return is diluted while the balance sheet grows. Paying everything out avoids that trap but caps self-funded growth at nothing, leaving expansion dependent on markets that may not be open.

💡

Retention sets how fast the business can grow on its own; the return on what is retained decides whether that growth creates value.

In class: the sharper question is not whether management reinvests, but at what return — the sustainable growth relation multiplies the two together.

Industry example — Berkshire Hathaway

Berkshire has retained essentially all of its earnings for decades rather than paying a common dividend, on the stated logic that cash is worth keeping only while it can be redeployed at an attractive return.

The two decisions are treated as one: capital is retained because there is somewhere worthwhile to put it, not as a default.

Q6 / Ratio versus value

Is maximising ROA always in shareholders’ best interest?

No — because shareholders own value, not a ratio, and a ratio can be raised by shrinking what sits beneath it. Value created is the spread between the return and the cost of capital, multiplied by the capital on which it is earned, and compounded for as long as the advantage lasts. A firm can lift its average return by declining every project that beats the cost of capital but sits below its current average, and be worth less for it.

The same pressure discourages spending that is expensed rather than capitalised — research, brand, training — and rewards deferring maintenance, since both flatter the numerator or shrink the denominator today at the cost of the advantage period tomorrow.

Using ROA as What it does Verdict
A scoreboard Reports the return the assets already in place are earning sound
A hurdle for new projects Rejects value-creating investment that sits below the current average wrong rule
A management target Rewards shrinking the denominator — deferred maintenance, expensed investment, moving assets off the balance sheet rather than out of the business gameable
💡

ROA is a good scoreboard and a poor hurdle: owners are made better off by every project that beats the cost of capital, including ones that lower the average.

In class: ask what the ratio would do if the firm simply sold its lowest-returning division. The average rises, and the owners are not obviously richer.

Industry example — Amazon

For much of its history Amazon reported thin profits while directing operating cash into fulfilment capacity and, later, AWS — both large, asset-heavy commitments that enlarge the denominator long before they show up in the numerator.

A management team optimising near-term return on assets would have found the case for building either of them very hard to make.

Q7 / External conditions

How can external conditions change sustainable growth while ROA holds steady?

ROA is measured above the financing line, so it can sit still while the terms on which growth is financed move underneath it. Three channels do the work. Borrowing cost is charged after the operating result, so a stable operating return can still leave less profit to retain. Availability is separate from price — lenders and equity markets decide whether funding beyond internally generated cash is offered at all.

Inflation is the quiet one: the same physical volume of trade ties up more working capital and costs more to replace, while historical-cost depreciation understates that replacement bill — so a steady reported ROA can conceal a rising real reinvestment requirement. Volatility does the rest, since uncertain earnings make prudent managers hold buffers instead of funding growth.

💡

Sustainable growth depends on the price, the availability and the real cost of the capital that funds asset growth — none of which appear anywhere in ROA.

In class: keep the price of funding and its availability apart. They move for different reasons and constrain growth in different ways.

Industry example — Qantas and the airline fleet

An aircraft fleet is bought or leased, never funded out of a single year’s trading. Like every large carrier, Qantas renews and expands its fleet on financing terms set outside the business.

Capacity growth is therefore governed by the cost and availability of that financing, even in a year when the flying operation itself performs exactly as before.

Q8 / High return, real risk

How can a high-ROA company still face distress or strategic risk?

A high ROA says the assets currently in the business earn well. It is silent on three things that decide survival. It is an average, so it hides how few products, customers or markets produce the profit. It is a snapshot, so it says nothing about how long the advantage period stays open. And it is struck above the financing line, so it is blind to the fixed obligations sitting on top of those assets.

Distress arrives where those meet: interest, leases and maturing debt do not fall when earnings do, and a business can be profitable on paper while failing to pay on the day payment falls due. A high return can even be the warning — earned by refusing to reinvest, it buys today’s ratio with tomorrow’s position.

Blind spot The question the ratio hides
Concentration How many products, customers or markets is the profit actually coming from?
Durability Is the competitive advantage period opening or closing?
Financing structure ROA is struck before interest — how much debt sits on those assets, and when does it mature?
Liquidity timing Does the cash arrive before the obligations fall due, or after?
Measurement Is the denominator small because the business is lean, or because its real commitments are expensed or held off the balance sheet?
🚨

A high ROA describes the assets in place today; distress comes from concentration, from a closing advantage period, and from the obligations ROA is measured above.

In class: pair the ratio with two questions — how many sources produce the profit, and what has to be paid whether or not it keeps arriving.

Industry example — Nokia

Nokia’s handset business was highly profitable and led the market before the smartphone displaced it. The returns were real, and were being earned on assets already in place.

What the ratio could not report was how long the position behind it would hold — which turned out to be the only question that mattered.
