Drivers of returns

Return comes from two places at once — how profitably a business trades, and how much capital it ties up to do it. Week 2 reads the asset mix for the business model, then takes return apart into the drivers behind it.

Pre-live session 53 key terms 80 flashcards
01 / The core idea

Asset structure as a business model signal

The combination of assets a company holds should broadly reflect how it creates and delivers value. Read that way, the balance sheet becomes useful for understanding the economic model behind the business, not just its financial position.

❓

Does the asset structure make sense given how the company operates?

02 / Putting firms on one scale

Common-size asset analysis

Common-size analysis expresses each asset category as a percentage of total assets. Stating every category on the same scale allows comparison between companies of very different sizes, and helps identify differences driven by their business models and industries.

03 / Reading the categories

What different asset categories reveal

Each category carries a signal about the activity that sits behind it.

Asset category High proportion may indicate
Inventory Manufacturing, distribution or retail activities
Receivables Customers receive credit or payment occurs after service delivery
PPE Capital-intensive operations requiring physical infrastructure
Goodwill Significant historical acquisition activity and acquisition premiums
Other intangibles Reliance on software, intellectual property, licenses, product development or similar non-physical assets
💡

The value comes from interpreting these categories together, rather than judging any single number in isolation.

04 / Patterns

Business model patterns

What separates these patterns is not the size of the balance sheet but which categories carry the weight, and whether that weight sits in current or non-current assets.

Pattern Asset profile
Capital intensive Businesses requiring aircraft, buildings, factories or other major infrastructure will normally have a high proportion of PPE. Their balance sheets are therefore weighted toward non-current assets. Significant capital must be committed before revenue can be generated.
Digital platform Usually hold relatively little inventory and physical infrastructure. A larger proportion of assets may instead consist of software and other intangibles, and goodwill where growth has occurred through acquisitions.
Manufacturing & biopharma Manufacturing creates a need for both inventory, as products move through production and distribution, and PPE, because production requires facilities and equipment. Research-intensive industries can additionally hold significant intangible assets arising from development activity.
Retail Can hold relatively high current assets: substantial inventory available for sale, and receivables where customers are offered financing or credit. Their asset structure reflects both stock management and customer payment arrangements.
Technology example

A SaaS platform would normally be expected to have much less inventory and PPE than a hardware manufacturer.

05 / Strategy signals

Asset mix can reveal strategy

The balance sheet can provide clues about strategic choices.

Signal What it may indicate
High goodwill Growth may have relied heavily on acquisitions
High intangibles Value creation may depend on technology IP or development
High PPE Operations require substantial physical capital
High receivables Customer credit is an important part of the commercial model
High inventory Working capital management is strategically important
💡

Changes in asset composition over time can indicate that the business model or growth strategy is changing.

06 / Analytical takeaway

Judge the mix against the model, not a benchmark

A company’s asset mix should be assessed against its business model, industry and strategy, rather than against a universal benchmark.

Key questions

  • Does the asset structure logically match how the company earns revenue?
  • Which assets are essential to delivering the business model?
  • Is growth being created organically or through acquisitions?
  • Are unusually high inventory or receivables creating working capital risk?
  • Does an unexpected asset balance require further investigation?
  • The combination of assets should broadly reflect how a company creates and delivers value.
  • Common-size analysis puts companies of very different sizes on one scale.
  • Categories are read together, rather than judging any single number in isolation.
  • Capital-intensive balance sheets tilt to non-current assets; retail can tilt to current assets.
  • Digital platforms may hold their weight in software, other intangibles and goodwill.
  • A change in asset composition is itself a signal worth investigating.
💡

The balance sheet can act as a financial fingerprint of the business model — it shows where capital is committed and what resources management depends on to create value.

07 / The core idea

Return is driven by both profitability and capital use

Measures such as ROA and ROE combine two things at once: an income statement outcome — profitability — and a balance sheet base, the assets or equity used to generate that profit.

💡

Improving returns is not only about increasing profit, it also requires managing how much capital is tied up in the business.

08 / The driver chain

From market position through to return

Returns can be understood as a chain that runs from market position through to return. Each financial outcome along it has its own main drivers and its own management focus.

The core driver chain names those drivers and that focus, outcome by outcome.

Financial outcome Main drivers Management focus
Profitability Volume + Margin Product mix, pricing, demand
Asset Efficiency Asset utilisation Capacity and productive use of assets
Working Capital Inventory, receivables, payables Prevent growth from absorbing excessive cash
Capital Structure Debt/equity funding mix Finance assets appropriately
Sustained Returns Competitive advantage period Protect returns from competitive erosion
09 / Profitability

Volume × margin

Profitability fundamentally depends on volume — how much the business sells — and margin, the profit generated from each sale. Products can have different combinations of the two:

  • High volume / low margin
  • Low volume / high margin

Management therefore needs to decide which products, customers and markets deserve investment rather than simply maximising sales.

Technology example

Basic plan

High volume, with lower margins.

Enterprise contracts

Fewer customers, but larger margins.

A SaaS company may balance the two.

10 / Balance sheet drivers

Assets, working capital and funding

Asset utilisation

The issue is whether assets are being used hard enough to generate revenue.

  • Spare or under-utilised capacity reduces returns
  • Surplus assets may need to be sold, redeployed or restructured
❓

Are those assets producing enough income?

Working capital

Growth can increase requirements for:

  • Inventory
  • Receivables
  • Payables

Poor working capital management can cause a growing, profitable business to absorb sustainable cash. Management must therefore consider the cash requirement created by growth, not just the additional revenue.

Capital structure

Returns are also influenced by the funding mix between debt and equity. The appropriate mix depends on how the business chooses to finance the assets needed to operate and grow.

11 / External drivers

Financial outcomes reflect what is happening competitively

Key factors include:

  • Consumer demand
  • Competitive behaviour
  • Continued competitiveness
  • Efficiency of asset management
  • Funding decisions
💡

Financial ratios should be interpreted alongside the company’s market and strategic position.

12 / Competitive advantage period

How long can superior returns last?

The competitive advantage period is the period for which a company can maintain returns above its cost of capital before competitors catch up. A strong current return is therefore not enough.

❓

How long can this level of superior return realistically continue?

13 / Sustainable advantage

What holds the period open

Competitive advantage may be supported by:

Source of advantage What it provides
Superior brand Customers continue choosing or paying more for the offering
Core-category innovation The company remains ahead of competitors in its main market
Strategic partnerships Relationships provide capabilities or market access competitors cannot easily replicate

These advantages require continuous maintenance and reinvestment rather than being treated as permanent.

14 / Analytical takeaway

What to carry out of drivers of returns

  • A return measure combines an income statement outcome with a balance sheet base.
  • Returns improve by raising profit and by managing the capital tied up in the business.
  • Profitability depends on volume and margin, so products, customers and markets differ in what they deserve.
  • Spare or under-utilised capacity reduces returns; surplus assets may need to be sold, redeployed or restructured.
  • Growth creates a cash requirement, not only additional revenue.
  • The funding mix between debt and equity should suit the assets being financed.
  • Financial ratios should be read alongside the company’s market and strategic position.
  • Sustainable value creation depends on whether the company can preserve the competitive advantages supporting those returns.

Key management questions

  1. What is driving returns: margin, volume or asset efficiency?
  2. Are assets producing adequate revenue, or is there excessive capacity?
  3. Will growth create excessive working-capital requirements?
  4. Is the funding mix appropriate?
❓

How long can the company maintain superior returns before competitors erode them?

15 / The purpose

Why decompose a return at all

The DuPont model breaks return measures into their underlying drivers. That lets an analyst explain why ROA or ROE changed, rather than simply observing that they changed.

💡

An improving financial ratio is only meaningful once the driver of that improvement is understood.

16 / Return on assets

The two drivers behind ROA

The first DuPont relationship splits the return on assets into the two drivers that produce it.

ROA = Asset Utilisation x Profit Margin

Each driver carries its own management interpretation.

Driver What it measures Management interpretation
Asset utilisation Revenue generated per dollar of assets How effectively assets generate sales
Profit margin Profit generated per dollar of revenue Pricing power and/or control of expenses
ROA Profit generated from the asset base Overall operating efficiency of invested assets
17 / Return on equity

Adding financial leverage

The model then introduces financial leverage. Fully expanded, the return to shareholders carries a third term.

ROE = Asset Utilisation x Profit Margin x Financial Leverage

The assets-to-equity ratio shows how much of the asset base exists for each dollar of shareholder equity. A higher ratio generally indicates that more assets are being financed through debt relative to equity.

18 / Reading a higher ROE

Higher ROE is not always better

An increase in ROE can come from very different sources.

Cause of higher ROE Interpretation
Higher asset utilisation Assets are generating more revenue
Higher profit margin Revenue is being converted into more profit
Higher leverage More debt is being used relative to equity

The first two represent improvements in operating performance. The third can increase shareholder returns mathematically, but it also introduces greater financial risk.

💡

ROE should never be interpreted without examining what caused it to change.

19 / Evaluating an acquisition

Why a bigger profit proves little

An acquisition will normally increase:

  • Assets
  • Revenue
  • Expenses
  • Potentially profit

Therefore, higher absolute profit after an acquisition is weak evidence of success.

❓

Has the management generated a better return from the additional capital invested?

DuPont analysis helps separate the acquisition’s effects into:

  1. Changes in asset productivity
  2. Changes in profitability
  3. Changes in financial leverage
20 / Offsetting drivers

One component improves as another weakens

A transaction may improve one component while weakening another.

Higher asset utilisation Lower profit margin
Assets generate more revenue Additional revenue carries proportionately higher expenses

These effects can offset each other, leaving ROA unchanged. This demonstrates why analysing only revenue growth, profit growth or one financial ratio can give an incomplete picture.

21 / The appearance of improvement

Leverage can look like better performance

Unchanged ROA + Higher Leverage → Higher ROE
  • Underlying operating returns have not improved
  • Shareholders appear to earn a higher return
  • Improvement comes from using more debt

Higher ROE may reflect greater risk rather than better management performance.

22 / Management and takeaway

Judging the source of a return

DuPont analysis is useful when assessing management bonuses or strategic performance. Management should not necessarily be rewarded simply because profit increased or ROE increased.

Analysts should determine whether the improvement came from:

  • Generating operating efficiency
  • Better margins
  • Productive deployment of new assets
  • Simply greater leverage
🚨

Management performance should be evaluated on the quality and source of returns, not just the headline return.

Analytical takeaway

The DuPont model turns a return ratio into a financial story.

ROE = Asset Efficiency x Profitability x Leverage

When evaluating a strategic decision, ask:

  • Did the investment improve asset utilisation?
  • Did margins improve or deteriorate?
  • Did ROA improve?
  • Is higher ROE driven by better operations or simply more debt?
  • Has management increased returns by creating value, or by increasing shareholder risk?
