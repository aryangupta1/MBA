DMBA 6008 · Finance, Strategy and Technology · Week 1

Key value principles

A business creates value only when it earns more on its assets than the cost of the capital used to finance them. Everything in this week is a way of testing that sentence.

Pre-live session 20 key terms 25 flashcards
01 / The core idea

Value creation logic, end to end

The key value principle links business activity into a single chain. Assets generate revenue, revenue becomes profit after costs, and profit is judged against the asset base that produced it. That return is then compared with what the capital cost to raise.

❓

Did we earn more than the cost of capital?

02 / Return on assets

What ROA actually measures

ROA measures the efficiency of asset usage — how much profit is generated per unit of investment. A higher ROA means better asset productivity.

Return on Assets = Profit / Assets
Basic example
Profit
$20
Assets
$200

ROA = 20 / 200 = 10%

SaaS company example
Total assets
$50M
Annual profit
$7.5M

ROA = 7.5 / 50 = 15%

Invested in cloud servers, engineers and customer acquisition systems. The assets are highly productive if WACC < 15%.

03 / Financing the assets

Capital structure and the hurdle rate

Each capital provider demands compensation for the risk they take. Debt is lower risk, so it carries a lower return; equity is higher risk, so it carries a higher one.

Source Provider Required return
Debt Banks / Lenders Interest rate
Equity Shareholders Expected return

Because higher risk means a higher required return, a risky startup carries a high WACC while a stable utility carries a low one. WACC is the blended cost of all financing sources — the minimum return the firm must earn.

WACC = (weight of debt × cost of debt) + (weight of equity × cost of equity)

Managerially, WACC is the hurdle rate. Below it, investors are not compensated enough. Above it, excess value is created.

04 / The decision rule

The value creation test

Value Spread = Return on Assets − WACC
Case Meaning
ROA > WACC Value created
ROA = WACC Break-even
ROA < WACC Value destroyed
05 / The time dimension

Why single-year ROA is not enough

ROA is a snapshot metric. Investments, however, last multiple years, generate uneven cash flows, and involve upfront costs. Net present value evaluates total value over time by converting all future cash flows into today's dollars.

NPV = sum of discounted cash flows − initial investment
NPV result Meaning
NPV > 0 Value creation
NPV = 0 Break-even
NPV < 0 Value destruction

The unified formula stack

ROA = Profit / Assets WACC = Weighted cost of capital Value Spread = ROA − WACC NPV = sum of discounted cash flows − initial investment

Each does a distinct job: ROA is the efficiency of assets, WACC the cost of financing, the value spread the annual value signal, and NPV the total lifetime value.

06 / The eight principles

Value creation as a decision cascade

Value creation is a connected sequence, each principle a filter. An investment may pass one stage and fail a later one — a project can be profitable yet destroy value if it needs excessive capital, carries high risk, or does not support long-term strategy.

Profitability
Does growth product sustainable profit margins?

Revenue or market-share growth has limited value unless it converts into profit.

Capital Efficiency
How much capital is required to generate profit?

Profit must be evaluated relative to the assets and investment required.

Capital Availability
Is this the best use of limited organisational funds?

Attractive projects compete with other investment opportunities through capital allocation.

Capital Structure
What mix of debt and equity should finance the investment?

Financing choices affect cost, flexibility and financial risk.

Risk Assessment
What risks exist in the underlying assets and cash flows?

Higher-risk investments require a higher return and hurdle rate.

Value Creation
Does the investment return exceed its financing cost?

Economic value is created only when the asset earns more than the capital supporting it.

Value Measurement
Is the asset worth more than its acquisition or development cost?

Future cash flows must be valued today and compared with the investment cost.

Strategic Alignment
Does the investment support long-term strategy and capabilities?

Financial viability alone does not ensure sustainable organisational value.

Where each principle bites

Sustainable profitability

Growth should be judged by its ability to produce sustainable margins, not simply higher sales. Warning signs include:

  • Rapid revenue growth accompanies by declining margins
  • Customers acquired through unsustainable discounts
  • Rising operating costs that offset additional revenue
  • Market-share gains that do not produce future pricing power

Capital efficiency

Two businesses may earn the same profit on very different investment. A cloud product that serves more customers with little extra infrastructure is more capital-efficient than a hardware platform needing new manufacturing capacity for every increase in demand.

Capital availability and opportunity cost

Capital is limited even in profitable organisations, so management ranks competing uses — a new product, an acquisition, infrastructure, a new market, debt reduction, or returning funds to shareholders. Choosing one forgoes another, so judge an investment against the next best alternative, not against doing nothing.

Capital structure and financial flexibility

Management balances the lower cost of debt against the flexibility of equity, fixed interest obligations, dilution, refinancing exposure and preserved borrowing capacity. An efficient-looking structure turns dangerous if it leaves the company unable to absorb a shock or fund an opportunity. Financial flexibility has value even when profit does not show it.

Risk assessment

Different assets carry different risks, and those must be built into the required return — uncertain demand, technological obsolescence, cybersecurity or regulatory exposure, customer concentration, currency movements, execution risk, volatile or delayed cash flows. An inadequate risk adjustment makes a poor investment look attractive.

Cost versus value

Cost is what was paid; value is the present worth of the future cash flows. A cheap asset can be poor value if those cash flows are weak, an expensive one can create value if they are strong — and paying more than economic value destroys shareholder value.

Strategic alignment

Financial attractiveness is necessary, not sufficient. An investment should fit organisational capabilities, competitive position, market trends, customer needs, investor expectations and strategic priorities. A SaaS company may find a profitable opening in semiconductor manufacturing that is still wrong for it, needing different capabilities, supply chains, regulatory knowledge and capital intensity.

Integrated investment assessment

A sound investment decision should pass all eight tests:

  1. Will it generate sustainable profit?
  2. Is the expected profit efficient relative to the capital required?
  3. Is it the best use of limited funds?
  4. Can it be financed using an appropriate capital structure?
  5. Have the asset-specific risks been recognised?
  6. Will its return exceed the required return?
  7. Is its economic value greater than its cost?
  8. Does it support the organisation's long-term strategy?

Failure at any major state may justify redesigning, delaying or rejecting the investment.

❓

Is this the best risk-adjusted and strategically aligned use of the organisation's limited capital?

07 / Assessing performance

Why performance needs ratios

Absolute assets, revenue and profit mislead when businesses differ in size — a bigger business earns more simply by having more capital invested. Use relative measures: how efficiently assets generate revenue, how efficiently revenue becomes profit, and whether the return compensates investors for risk.

Asset Utilisation = Revenue / Assets Profit Margin = Profit / Revenue

Asset utilisation measures how well the asset base generates revenue — but a high ratio can sit on weak margins. Profit margin measures how well revenue converts to profit, reflecting pricing power, lower costs, process efficiency or product mix. Neither is sufficient alone: a strong margin still fails if the revenue demands an excessive asset base.

Decomposing return on assets

Return on Assets = Asset Utilisation × Profit Margin Profit / Assets = Revenue / Assets × Profit / Revenue
Performance model Asset utilisation Profit margin
High-volume model Higher Lower
Premium-margin model Lower Higher
Strong overall performer Effective balance Effective balance

A business can improve its return by generating more revenue from existing assets, increasing the profit earned from each dollar of revenue, or improving both simultaneously.

Accounting performance versus economic performance

A high return on assets is not automatically economic value. Accounting performance asks how much profit is being generated?; economic performance asks is the return sufficient for the capital employed and the risk taken? A business can look strong on revenue, profit and return ratios and still miss investors’ required return.

Financing costs as a risk signal

The returns lenders and shareholders demand carry information about perceived risk — volatile cash flows, uncertain profits, weak asset security, operating leverage, competitive instability, execution risk. So the cost of financing is not only an expense; it is a market signal of risk.

Risk-adjusted performance

Performance Spread = Return on Assets − WACC
Result Interpretation
Positive spread Return exceeds the risk-adjusted requirement
Zero spread Investors receive exactly their required return
Negative spread Return is insufficient for the risk and capital employed

A business with a lower reported return may outperform economically if it carries far less risk and needs less financing. ROA alone ignores business risk, funding structure, cash-flow reliability, investor expectations and cost of capital — a higher ROA is only superior once measured against the return that specific business requires.

❓

Which business earns the strongest return relative to its risk-adjusted cost of capital?

The assessment framework

  1. Scale: What assets, revenue and profit does it generate?
  2. Asset efficiency: How effectively do assets generate revenue?
  3. Margin efficiency: How effectively does revenue generate profit?
  4. Overall return: What return is generated from the asset base?
  5. Risk: How uncertain are the profits and cash flows?
  6. Financing requirement: What return do capital providers require?
  7. Economic performance: Does the return exceed the required return?
💡

Each stage adds information that may change the initial assessment.

Management and capital allocation implications

Risk-adjusted performance should drive whether to add capital, improve efficiency, reprice, cut costs, dispose of underperforming assets, restructure financing, reduce risk, or exit. Do not allocate more capital to a business merely because it reports higher revenue or profit — growth increases value destruction when the underlying return stays below the required return.

08 / Takeaways

What to carry out of Week 1

  • Revenue growth ≠ value creation.
  • Only ROA above WACC creates economic value.
  • Capital structure directly affects WACC, and risk determines the required return.
  • Profitability is only the first test of value creation.
  • Capital should be allocated according to relative returns and opportunity cost.
  • Financing decisions must preserve both cost efficiency and flexibility.
  • Financially viable investments can still fail through strategic misalignment.
  • Absolute profit is not sufficient for comparing differently sized businesses.
  • Asset utilisation and profit margin explain the operating drivers of return.
  • A higher return is not superior when it requires an even higher risk-adjusted return.
  • Strong management decisions integrate financial performance, risk and strategy, rather than assessing each separately.
